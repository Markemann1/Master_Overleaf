\section{Results and Socio-Economic Valuation}
\label{4-sec:4 results}

This chapter reports what the analysis of \autoref{3-sec:3} found, in the order the four stages of \autoref{fig:3.2 methodology spine} produce it. \autoref{4-subsec:4.0 technical envelope} reports how the dispatch uses the physical resource sized in \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope}, how the model fares against the public record and against its own tests, and what its dispatch looks like across a week and across a year. \autoref{4-subsec:4.1 DH operator results} reports the operator's ledger: the capability ladder, the cost levels, reserve participation and the accumulator. \autoref{4-subsec:4.2 triangulated social value} prices the socio-economic channels on the same dispatch. \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} divides the socio-economic total between the district heating operator, Hafslund Celsio, and the parties that receive the remainder, and attributes the measured quantities across the barrier types of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}. \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.4 sensitivity analysis} reports how far each result moves when its inputs do. Every run is one of the six scenarios of \autoref{tab:3.5.2 scenario stack}, two tariff structures crossed with three reserve-access levels, solved on the 2023, 2024 and 2025 delivery years and measured against a no-flexibility counterfactual on its own tariff structure. The scenario numbering is the one \autoref{fig:3.5.2 scenario stack} declares, and the rungs R0 to R3 are those of \autoref{tab:3.5.1 ladder}. Two rules from \autoref{3-sec:3} govern every number below. A cost level is model output on the operator's dispatch perimeter, and outside 2024 it is a counterfactual under the held 2024 policy; the differences carry the argument. And a quantity priced with a banded unit value is reported as a band with its endpoints named, never as a point.

\subsection{The Physical Resource and the Modelled Plant}
\label{4-subsec:4.0 technical envelope}

The envelope of \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope} is a stock; this subsection reports what the dispatch does with it, and whether the plant the model dispatches is the plant that exists. \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.1 envelope in use} reports which of the three power tiers bind, how often, and what the technical distance between them is worth. \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation} reports the outcome of every test \autoref{3-subsec:3.8 model validation} defined, and explains the two years in which the model and the record part company, one of which the rest of the chapter has to keep returning to. \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.3 dispatch} shows the dispatch itself, across a winter and a summer week and across the accumulator's year.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{The Envelope in Use}
\label{4-subsec:4.0.1 envelope in use}

The three power tiers of \autoref{tab:3.3 technical envelope} are 292.6 MW of installed electric-boiler capacity, about 250 MW that the heat network lets the fleet draw in any one hour, and 29 MW that may be sold into the manual reserve market. The dispatch meets all three, and it meets them in the order the envelope predicts. In every interruptible-tariff run in every delivery year the fleet's highest hourly grid draw is exactly 250 MW: the concurrent bound is reached, and it binds on the scheduled position in 456 of the 8,784 hours of 2024, 214 hours of 2023 and 223 hours of 2025, between 2.4 and 5.2 per cent of the year, against 210, 59 and 121 hours in the respective no-flexibility counterfactuals. The metered draw sits at the bound in fewer hours, 374, 176 and 220, because an upward activation sheds part of a scheduled position that is already at the limit. Under the standard tariff the same fleet peaks at 221 MW in 2024, 127 MW in 2023 and 197 MW in 2025 at today's access, and the bound never binds. The monthly capacity charge holds the peak below the network limit before the network limit is reached, which is the stacking of two constraints that assumption A9 of \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions} describes, observed in the runs rather than assumed.

What the outer of the two constraints costs is the first attributed quantity of the chapter. Re-solving the baseline run with the concurrent bound moved from 250 MW to the 292.6 MW the concession licenses and the network already connects, the thermal-bottleneck sensitivity of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack}, lowers the annual net cost by 2.22 MNOK in 2024, by 0.66 MNOK in 2023 and by 1.37 MNOK in 2025, and raises the flexibility value by 1.31, 0.57 and 0.85 MNOK in the same years. Under the standard tariff the same relief is worth nothing: the 2024 standard-tariff run's cost moves by less than 0.001 MNOK, because a fleet that the capacity charge stops at 221 MW cannot use headroom above 250. The relieved state is a heat network debottlenecked to capacity that is already standing. It is not the connection queue of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers}, and the figure prices no grid reinforcement.

The other distance \autoref{fig:2.4.1 three tier capacity} names, between the 29 MW reserve-eligible today and the concurrent bound, is priced in \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.3 reserve market participation} from a continuous sweep of the access constraint, and its result is stated here so that the two distances stand in the same units. Widening reserve access from 29 MW to 250 MW lowers the 2024 baseline run's annual net cost by 135.9 MNOK, from 631.97 to 496.06 MNOK, and widening it further to the licensed 292.6 MW lowers it by nothing more. The regulatory distance is worth roughly sixty times the technical one in the same year, and the same 250 MW bound caps both the dispatch and the reserve the dispatch can back.

On the energy side the accumulator does the work the envelope assigns it and runs into both of its limits. In the 2024 baseline run the store discharges 99.8 GWh over the year, about two hundred full cycles of its 495 MWh usable volume. Over the 365 complete days of that year it drains to the 55 MWh non-cyclable floor on 113 days and reaches the 550 MWh nameplate on 77, so on half the days of the year the tank as sized, rather than the price signal, is what binds; \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.5 storage sizing} sweeps the size for that reason. The network's own thermal mass, named in \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope} as a component of the resource, stays unquantified, and the sensitivity of the energy bound to the admissible temperature band cannot be reported, because the network water volume and the temperature band that \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope} names as the inputs the sizing would need were not obtained. The quantified energy stock remains the accumulator alone, and it remains a lower bound.

The heat side of the envelope closes at 557.5 MW: waste incineration at 155 MW, heat pumps at 55 MW, the electric boilers at 247.5 MW of delivered heat from 250 MW of draw, and the accumulator at 100 MW of discharge. Above that load the peak boilers run by necessity. Demand exceeded it in 273 hours of 2024, 91 hours of 2023 and 10 hours of 2025, against maximum loads of 763, 693 and 578 MW. The operator's own account puts the load up to which everything in the system is flexible at roughly 550 to 600 MW (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026), and the capacity frame reproduces that ceiling from the concession inventory and the operator's stated concurrent bound alone.

\subsubsection{Validation Against the Public Record and the Model's Own Tests}
\label{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation}

\autoref{fig:4.1 validation share} sets the model's electric-boiler share of heat production against the share Hafslund Celsio reports to the national district heating register, for every year of the five-year bound \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}. The modelled share tracks the observed one across the five years, with a rank correlation of $+0.90$, and sits below it in every one of them, by a mean of 6.3 percentage points: 3.5 points in 2021, 10.3 in 2022, 10.4 in 2023, 4.4 in 2024 and 3.1 in 2025. The test carries a band of nine percentage points against a sixteen-year observed range of 12.5 to 30.7 per cent, so the share test passes in 2021, 2024 and 2025 and fails in 2022 and 2023. The annual delivered volume, tested in the same pass against the same register, matches within 0.26 per cent in 2024 and within 20 MWh in 2023 and 2025, so the misses are misses of dispatch and not of the demand series. The model runs the boilers less than the operator does in every year, and by far the most in two of them.

The two failing years fail for different reasons, and neither reason is the activation rule, because 2021 runs on the same binary rule and passes. 2022 is the energy-crisis year. Its mean day-ahead price in NO1 was 1{,}939 NOK/MWh, four times the 487 NOK/MWh of 2024, in the year the Russo-Ukrainian war disrupted European gas supply and drove the price spikes of 2021 and 2022 \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023}, to which Norway responded with the retail support scheme of \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.2 policy evolution} \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}. A cost-minimising dispatch all but stops running electric boilers at that price level, at 2.2 per cent of production, and the operator ran them at 12.5 per cent regardless. The price level alone carries that year.

2023 is the case the rest of this chapter has to keep returning to, because it is the outlier in every result below, and its explanation is one mechanism. The obvious hypothesis fails: 2023 was not an expensive year in the sense that matters. Its mean price of 761 NOK/MWh is the same as 2021's 758, and it had seven times as many hours under 300 NOK/MWh as 2021, 2{,}207 against 310, and more than twice as many under 400. On the count of cheap hours alone the boilers should have run more in 2023 than in 2021. They ran roughly half as much, 182 GWh against 341. \autoref{tab:4.1 2023 mechanism} shows what separates the two years: when the cheap hours fell. In 2021, 67 per cent of the hours under 400 NOK/MWh fell in the October to March heating season; in 2023, 18 per cent did, and three quarters of them fell in July, August and September, when Oslo's heat load is at its summer minimum. Mean heat demand in 2023's cheap hours was 103 MW against a year-round mean of 228, a ratio of 0.45, where in 2021 the cheap hours were above-average demand hours at a ratio of 1.13. Stated from the other side, the correlation between hourly heat demand and the day-ahead price in 2023 was $+0.68$, the highest of the five years: cold hours were expensive hours. A boiler can only exploit a cheap hour if there is heat to make in it, and in 2023 the opportunity thermal flexibility lives on, cheap power arriving while heat is wanted, barely existed. The value of the flexibility is a product of the price level and of the coincidence of low prices with heat load, and the 2021 to 2023 pair isolates the second factor because the first is held equal. That is a result in its own right, and it is the reason 2023 sits low on the ladder, loads the coincident peak, carries the smallest tariff barrier and returns a negative gap in the subsections below. What it does not explain is the model's under-dispatch: the operator ran the boilers at 19.5 per cent in 2023 anyway, and whatever made that rational sits outside the design, as \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} states.

The two emergent tests come out the way the operator describes the plant, with one instructive exception. The load level at which the dispatch runs the peak boilers in every hour is 458 MW in 2024 and 2025 and 432 MW in 2023, about a hundred megawatts below the operator's 550 to 600 MW band. The difference is choice rather than necessity: the pellet tranche of \autoref{tab:3.4.2 peak stack} undercuts electric heat whenever the day-ahead price exceeds roughly 530 NOK/MWh, and cold hours are expensive hours, so the peak fleet is dispatched on price well before the heat side of the envelope closes at 557.5 MW, and the physical ceiling itself lands inside the operator's band. The behavioural test is the one with the exception. In the 88 dearest hours of 2024 the no-flexibility counterfactual runs the electric boilers in two hours, a mean of 0.2 MW of heat across the 88, and in no hour at all in 2023 and 2025; the operator abstains from boiler operation in the highest-price hours without exception (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). The flexible baseline run breaks that rule in 32 of the 88 hours of 2024, 18 of 2023 and 27 of 2025, a mean of 10.4, 5.9 and 8.5 MW of heat across the 88 hours and about 28 MW in the hours the boilers run, and every one of those hours carries a reserve commitment that the boiler is backing. The model departs from the operator's stated practice only where a reserve obligation forces it to, and the operator held no such obligation in the two years before it entered the market. That is the reserve-backing mechanism of \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}, met here for the first time in the price-ranked hours rather than the demand-ranked ones.

Three tests fail on the current build, and each is diagnosed before it is described, as \autoref{3-subsec:3.8 model validation} requires; nothing was tuned in response to any of them. The first is the 2023 share test above. The second is the test that the store never charges and discharges in the same hour. It fails in five of the eighteen runs, all at partial or applied access, in between 2 and 64 hours of the year, with between 94 and 2{,}944 MWh cycled through the overlap. In every one of those hours the dump coolers stand at their 62.5 MW capacity and the waste incinerator sits on its minimum-load floor, and a down-regulation commitment is live in between two-thirds and all of them. The optimisation is disposing of must-run heat it has nowhere else to put, by cycling the tank and paying the round-trip loss to destroy it, and the cycling charge prices that at between 0.011 and 0.068 per cent of the objective. It is a priced dispatch decision under a binding physical constraint, and forbidding it would need the binary variables the formulation excludes. The third is the consistency test between the shadow price on the access ceiling and the finite-difference slope of the access curve, which fails on all six curves in the same direction and by roughly the same factor: the harvested dual sums the shadow price on the upward ceiling alone, while the access parameter caps both directions, and the downward row binds more often, so the dual is half a derivative. The finite-difference curve is nonincreasing on every one of the six, which is the convexity the same test also checks, and every marginal value of access reported in this chapter is the finite difference and never the dual. One further check moved and is reported so that it is not mistaken for a tuned one. The identity that shed reserve cannot exceed draw was held to an absolute tolerance of one millionth of a megawatt on a quantity that reaches 250 MW, and four applied-access runs exceeded it by between 1.0 and 3.2 millionths of a megawatt in exactly one hour each, an order of magnitude inside the solver's own feasibility tolerance. The tolerance is now scale-relative and the worst violation is printed whether the row passes or fails. That is a correction to a check no solver can satisfy, not a relaxation to admit a result.

\subsubsection{Dispatch in Two Weeks, and the Accumulator's Year}
\label{4-subsec:4.0.3 dispatch}

\autoref{fig:4.1 dispatch weeks} shows the hourly dispatch of the baseline run, scenario 1 at 2024, across two weeks, 10 to 16 December and 5 to 11 July, each chosen as the week whose seven-source dispatch mix lies closest to its season's mean. The summer week carries two further conditions, both judgements: it had to contain negative-price hours, because the fleet's response to them is a behaviour this chapter discusses and a summer week without any would misrepresent the season, and its negative axis was capped at forty per cent of the panel height for legibility, which excluded the week of 27 July, at 64 per cent below zero. In the winter week the electric boilers average 100.5 MW of heat, the day-ahead price runs from 3 to 6{,}238 NOK/MWh, the accumulator charges 3{,}250 MWh across the week and discharges into the price spikes, and nothing is dumped. The merit order is visible doing its job: every price spike collapses the boiler block and the peak-fuel bands take over, with the pellet tranche pinned at its 56 MW cap almost continuously and the bio-oil tranche at 1{,}330 NOK/MWh$_{th}$ acting as the true swing fuel, which is why the three peak fuels are drawn as separate bands. The summer week is the opposite problem. The incinerator alone nearly covers demand, so there is almost nothing to optimise until a run of negative-price hours, 24 in the week and 15 of them consecutive, reaches $-$228 NOK/MWh; the plant then runs the boilers to 145.3 MW, fills the tank, and still dumps 1{,}186 MWh of heat, because being paid to consume electricity is worth more than the heat is worth storing. The peak boilers never fire all week.

The mean daily swing is 256 MWh, the median 260 and the maximum 495, against a usable volume of 495 MWh, so a typical day moves half the tank, and there is no seasonal trend underneath the daily movement. It also carries the summer stagnation, in which 58 days swing under 20 MWh and one run of 13 days sits above 90 per cent full, because with the incinerator alone covering demand there is nothing to arbitrage. That is demand too low to need the tank, and it is a different thing from the 2023 mechanism of \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation}, which is cheap power and heat load failing to coincide. The floor is nameplate less the declared usable fraction of \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance}, and it moves with that declaration.

\subsection{Commercial Results: the Operator's Ledger}
\label{4-subsec:4.1 DH operator results}

Every figure in this subsection is read off the operator's ledger, and the ledger's perimeter has to be stated before the first level is quoted. A cost level below is the operating cost of dispatch on the model's own perimeter: electricity at spot, grid tariff, electricity tax, fuel and variable operating cost, less reserve revenue. It excludes heat revenue, which is invariant to dispatch, capital cost and financing, and it is not a statement about the firm; the firm-level figures come from the audited accounts. Outside 2024 a level is a counterfactual under held 2024 policy, on the standardisation of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack}, and every difference is unaffected by it. The subsection leads with the capability ladder, because every value in it is a step between adjacent rungs or a difference from one of the two references of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder}. It then reports the cost levels and the delivery-year bound, reserve participation and the value of access, and the accumulator's size and payback. The four parts are the two scenario axes, a bound and a sweep, not levels of participation.

\subsubsection{The Capability Ladder and the Flexibility Value}
\label{4-subsec:4.1.1 baseline}

\autoref{fig:4.2 ladder baseline} draws the four rungs of \autoref{tab:3.5.1 ladder} for scenario 1 at 2024, the baseline run. The price-blind rung R0 costs 772.84 MNOK for the year. Dispatching against the hourly price signal takes 75.08 MNOK off that, the accumulator a further 20.59, and reserve participation at today's 29 MW a further 45.20, to the baseline run's 631.97 MNOK: a fall of 140.87 MNOK, or 18 per cent of the price-blind cost, over the whole ladder.

Two of those rungs are objects the chapter quotes separately, and the ladder makes the coincidence exact. The top rung R3 is the baseline run itself, at 631.97 MNOK, and the second rung R1 is its no-flexibility counterfactual, at 697.76 MNOK, identical to the decimal in both files the model writes. The headline flexibility value of 65.79 MNOK per year is therefore the sum of the two upper steps alone, the accumulator and reserve participation, and it excludes the largest capability on the ladder. Price-responsive dispatch is worth 75.08 MNOK, more than the other two combined, and it sits outside the headline because the counterfactual already dispatches on price. The most valuable thing the plant does is invisible in the figure the thesis leads with, and that is a property of the reference rather than a caveat: the counterfactual of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder} is the plant as it actually runs, and the plant actually runs against hourly prices. The reserve step of 45.20 MNOK is reserve at this scenario's 29 MW, so it moves along the access axis where the two rungs below it do not.

\autoref{fig:4.2 ladder cross} stacks the three steps at all six scenarios and all three delivery years, and one caveat has to be read into it before anything else. The 2023 group runs on the binary activation rule, because the fifteen-minute balancing record for NO1 begins on 17 October 2023, and that rule overstates activation duration by a factor of about 1.16 against the fractional rule the 2024 and 2025 groups use, as \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} sets out; so the 2023 reserve steps are on a different definition of activation from the other two years' while sharing an axis with them. The seam reaches the reserve step only. The two lower rungs sell no reserve, and their 2023 values stand on the same footing as the other years'. \autoref{tab:4.2 ladder steps} gives the steps.

\label{4-subsec:4.1.2 spot arbitrage}

\autoref{fig:4.2 levelised cost} draws the levelised cost of heat for the eighteen runs on a tariff-complete basis, with each tariff structure's own no-flexibility counterfactual as a dashed rule. The basis matters, and it is the one \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} defines for the tariff comparison: the interruptible tariff's subscribed-capacity charge, fixed in subscribed megawatts and correctly left out of the optimisation's objective, is added back before the two tariff structures are stood side by side, because the standard tariff pays no such charge. The interruptible bars therefore sit 15.5 to 17.3 NOK/MWh above the levelised cost the model's own output files carry, which is computed on the objective alone, and the standard bars do not move; a reader checking the drawing against the run artefacts will find that difference, and it is deliberate. The figure supports one reading and forbids another. The reading is the vertical distance from a bar to its own tariff structure's rule, which is the flexibility value per megawatt-hour of heat delivered: on the interruptible tariff at 2024 it runs from 34 NOK/MWh at today's access to 103 at applied access, and on the standard tariff from 27 to 76, the ladder's finding in per-unit form. Across the access axis the interruptible bars fall from 410 to 392 NOK/MWh in 2023, a 4.4 per cent fall, and from 314 to 196 in 2025, a 38 per cent fall. Access buys almost nothing in 2023 because there was almost nothing to arbitrage.

\subsubsection{Reserve Participation and the Value of Access}
\label{4-subsec:4.1.3 reserve market participation}

Reserve participation is the step that scales, and \autoref{tab:4.2 core results} carries its revenue at every run: upward capacity payments plus upward activation energy, 50.9 MNOK at the 2024 baseline, 138.2 at partial and 189.3 at applied access, with the down-regulation leg inside net cost rather than in the column. The operator entered the manual reserve market in 2025 (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026), so the 2023 and 2024 figures are what today's 29 MW would have earned had it been offered, as \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions} states, and no reserve figure from those years reconciles against the operator's accounts.

\autoref{fig:4.2 reserve benchmark} sets the modelled revenue per megawatt of eligible capacity against the one published estimate of the same mechanism, the anchor report's case exercise on the operator's own boilers presented in \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}: NOK 330{,}000 per MW per year in FCR-N capacity over an October to April season \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. The comparison is legitimate where a comparison with the conversion headline would not be, because both sides price the same asset selling into the system operator's reserve markets in the same unit, and it is legitimate on one segment only. The published figure is a capacity payment, so the like-for-like comparison is with the modelled capacity payment alone: 0.34 MNOK per MW per year at today's access in 2023, 0.92 in 2024 and 0.64 in 2025, or 1.03, 2.80 and 1.95 times the benchmark. With activation energy added, the modelled value is 1.12, 1.76 and 1.45 MNOK per MW per year. The two are never summed; the benchmark is context, and the registry the socio-economic arithmetic reads refuses it as an addend. The best sentence the comparison supports concerns volume. The published figure comes from a single-boiler case exercise and states no participating volume, so it is volume-independent by construction, while the modelled value falls as eligible capacity rises, to 0.50 MNOK per MW of capacity payment at 200 MW in 2024. The model converges on the published flat assumption from above: the published figure is a fair approximation at large volume and understates the value at the volume the operator actually holds. Three caveats travel with the comparison. The products differ, FCR-N against manual reserve. The published estimate counts capacity only. And it assumes delivery across October to April, months the same report places 30 to 40 per cent below the annual average for capacity prices, so it is conservative on its own terms, which is what makes the comparison fair rather than flattering. The one place the modelled capacity payment falls below the benchmark is 2023 at 100 and 200 MW, at 0.71 and 0.44 times, for the reason every 2023 figure carries. The near-exact match at today's access in 2023, at 1.03 times, is a coincidence at one point on one product between two sources that share no method, and it is noted as one.

At the 29 MW held today one additional megawatt of reserve access is worth 1.49 MNOK per year. The practical ceiling therefore sits below what the operator already has, and the zone the current regime argues over changes almost nothing. The value is in the 100 to 200 MW range that no allocation is offering.

\subsubsection{The Accumulator: Size and Payback}
\label{4-subsec:4.1.5 storage sizing}

\autoref{fig:4.2 storage sizing} sweeps the accumulator's usable energy across ten sizes from zero to three times the installed store, at scenario 1 in 2024, re-solving the whole problem at each. That is consistent with what \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.3 dispatch} shows of how it is used: the store is a daily asset that moves half its volume on a typical day and saturates at one end or the other on half the days of the year, so the binding thing is its size, and its size is already past the steep part of the return.

Cost levels are dispatch-perimeter operating costs, counterfactual outside 2024, and are never the basis of a claim.

\subsection{The Socio-Economic Valuation}
\label{4-subsec:4.2 triangulated social value}

The socio-economic side prices the physical quantities the runs above produce at the published unit values of \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}, channel by channel, and it prices nothing else. The anchor report's headline of NOK 1 to 1.6 billion per year is the value of converting electric heating to district heating, and it is not a figure this subsection produces, approaches or bounds against \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}: it is carried as context, outside every sum, and the one category error the valuation is built to prevent is reading it as the value of flexibility. What follows reports the two priced channels at every interruptible-tariff run, the three channels that carry no number, the mechanism that decides the sign of the grid channel, and how the two perimeters diverge as the store grows. The un-captured externality the priced channels sum to is the gap of \autoref{3-subsec:3.7 gap and attribution}, and its division is reported in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap}.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{The Priced Channels and Their Values}
\label{4-subsec:4.2.1 channels}

Of the six channels of \autoref{tab:3.6 channels}, two carry a krone value in this analysis. C1, freed grid capacity, prices the mean reduction in the operator's metered grid draw across the 88 tightest hours of the year ranked by heat demand, at the anchor report's band of 0.7 to 1.6 MNOK per MW per year with a central value of 1.0 \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}, and the mask that produced it is stated beside every figure below because the channel's sign turns on it. C3 is small at every run, between 0.79 and 4.50 MNOK per year, and it is small on a sourced answer rather than a missing one. Three of the four peak fuels are biogenic, the pellet tranche is first in the merit order, and the fossil LNG tranche is the marginal displaced fuel: its share of the peak heat that flexibility displaces is a model output, and it comes out between 0.33 and 0.47 across the nine runs. The channel is therefore reported as small and bounded by the LNG share of a predominantly biogenic peak fleet, never as zero, and its bound is swept in \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.4 sensitivity analysis}. Its sign against the second reference is the other way in two years of three, as \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder} anticipated, and both are reported with the sign. Against R1 the flexible plant burns less gas than the plant as it runs today in every year: 5{,}539, 12{,}499 and 13{,}783 MWh of LNG heat less at the baseline in 2023, 2024 and 2025. Against the price-blind R0 the whole ladder burns more in 2024 and 2025, by 52{,}062 and 94{,}820 MWh, because the first step substitutes pellets and gas for electricity in expensive hours, and the channel reads $-$7.45 and $-$13.57 MNOK. In 2023, whose mean price already favours gas over electricity at the flat R0 price, the ladder burns 48{,}411 MWh less and the channel reads $+$6.93 MNOK. The R0 direction is a decomposition observation against a reference that is not a policy option, and the gap row is C3 against R1.

C1 is the channel that carries the gap's sign, and its value depends on which hours count as the coincident peak. On the demand-ranked one per cent mask the relief is positive in 2024 and 2025 at every access level, from 6.15 MW at the 2024 baseline to 39.36 MW at applied access in 2025, and negative at every access level in 2023, from $-$8.34 to $-$29.69 MW: in the tightest hours of that year the flexible fleet drew more power than the plant would have drawn without flexibility. Swept across both selectors and five widths at all nine runs, ninety cells in all, the channel is meaningfully positive in six, all of them the demand-ranked mask at one or two per cent of hours in 2024 and 2025. At demand-ranked five per cent it is within rounding of zero, at ten and twenty per cent it is negative in every run of every year, and under the price-ranked mask it is negative almost everywhere. The narrow demand-ranked mask is the only definition under which the flexible fleet looks grid-relieving, and it is narrow precisely because it selects the coldest hours, where the boilers run regardless. \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.2 reserve and relief} gives the mechanism and \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.4 sensitivity analysis} the sweep; the consequence is stated here once, and holds throughout: no C1 figure, and no total containing one, is quoted in this chapter without the mask that produced it.

Three channels carry no number, and the priced total is a floor for that reason. Lower system prices in scarce hours, C4, is a transfer to electricity consumers for which no unit value exists at the dispatch scale of one operator, and the model is a price taker by construction, so its own dispatch cannot move the spot price it faces; the missing willingness-to-pay curve is this channel, and its absence is a declared scope choice. Faster grid connection for queued load, C5, is unpriced because one megawatt of freed headroom is either permanently avoided reinforcement or capacity released to a new load and cannot be both, and C1 already prices it once. Freed hydropower and reservoir value, C6, is the mechanism of \cite{askeland_balancing_2019} and carries no krone value by the scope guardrail of \autoref{1-subsec:1.4 scope and limitations}. All three are positive by construction; none of them could lower the total. The socio-economic total this chapter reports is therefore a lower bound on the two channels it prices, and no sentence below claims more than that.

\subsubsection{Reserve Commitment and the Coincident Peak}
\label{4-subsec:4.2.2 reserve and relief}

\autoref{fig:4.3 reserve peak} is the figure the sign of C1 turns on. Its left panel splits the change in mean grid draw across the 88 scarcest hours, ranked by heat demand, into the hours that carry a reserve commitment and those that do not, at the three access levels and three years of the interruptible tariff; the split is reported and never netted, because the two are opposite mechanisms and their mean describes neither, as \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation} sets out. Its right panel is why the sign turns over: the share of those hours in which the boiler sits exactly on the floor that backing its commitment requires. The finding is a policy result rather than a modelling detail. Selling upward reserve from an electric boiler relieves the coincident peak only when the boiler would have been running anyway. The backing constraint of the formulation, \autoref{eq:3.4.1 backed}, requires committed reserve to sit under live boiler draw, and the no-flexibility counterfactual carries no such obligation; so in an hour where a cost-minimiser would keep the boilers off, the commitment is what switches them on, in exactly the hours when power is scarcest. At today's access the committed hours relieve the peak by 7.37 MW in 2024 and 13.75 MW in 2025 and load it by 10.95 MW in 2023, across the 67 committed hours of 2023 and 2024 and the 77 of 2025, while the uncommitted hours are flat at zero in 2023, within 2.3 MW of zero in 2024, and between $-$5.8 and $+$9.2 MW across the access levels of 2025. Access amplifies whichever sign obtains, because a larger commitment is a larger floor: 2023 goes from $-$10.95 to $-$30.02 and $-$38.99 MW across the axis and 2025 from $+$13.75 to $+$24.41 and $+$46.82.

Why 2023 differs is measured in the right panel and in the counterfactual's own dispatch. The mean day-ahead price in the 88 scarcest hours was 1{,}739 NOK/MWh in 2023 against 1{,}506 in 2024 and 1{,}031 in 2025, so a cost-minimising plant kept the boilers nearly off: the 2023 counterfactual runs 34.3 MW of boiler heat in those hours against 157.3 and 142.2 in the two later years. Backing today's 29 MW commitment needs about 22 MW of boiler heat, far above what the 2023 counterfactual was running and far below what the 2024 and 2025 counterfactuals were, so the backing floor binds in 59 per cent of 2023's scarce hours and in 10 and 22 per cent of the other two years'. Where the floor is slack the commitment adds no draw and the activation shed is pure relief; where it binds, the commitment adds draw. The whole of the 2023 result is the 19.5 MW of extra scheduled draw the backing requirement creates, and it is the same fact as the 2023 validation miss of \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation} seen from the other side: the model runs far less boiler in the coincident peak than the operator did, and a counterfactual that ran the boilers the way the operator actually ran them would leave the backing constraint slack and the channel positive. The honest form of the finding is therefore not that flexibility loads the grid in 2023. It is that in the hours where a pure cost-minimiser would keep the boilers off, a reserve obligation puts them on, and the model spends more of the year in that state than the operator does.

\autoref{fig:4.3 reserve race} shows the race inside the committed hours for two runs, scenario 1 at 2024 and scenario 3 at 2025, as a waterfall over the same 88 demand-ranked hours restricted to those carrying a commitment, 67 of 88 in the first case and 72 of 88 in the second. The two panels are drawn on independent scales, and that has to be read first: the right panel's net bar draws at roughly the same height as the left's and is 6.3 times larger, $+$46.8 MW against $+$7.4. In both cases the activation shed outweighs the backing draw and the race comes out positive, so committed reserve does relieve the coincident peak on average across the committed hours in 2024 and 2025; the down-regulation draw, which raises the meter when the operator is paid to consume, subtracts 3.4 MW in the second case and nothing in the first. This is what makes the negative channel in 2023 a claim about the hours in which the shed does not outweigh the backing, rather than a blanket finding that reserve loads the grid, and the two figures are to be read together.

The sharpest reading is about the reserve product rather than about the plant. C1's sign turns on whether the boiler was going to run anyway, which makes it a statement about how upward reserve is procured: a capacity product that paid for availability without requiring backed load in the hour would not produce it. Procuring balancing capacity from a price-responsive load can therefore work against capacity adequacy in exactly the hours when adequacy is tightest. That is a tension between two things the system operator wants, and it is also the reason C5 stays qualitative: headroom the reserve product consumes in the scarcest hours is not headroom that can be released to a queued load.

\subsection{The Value Gap}
\label{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap}

The gap is a division within the socio-economic total, not a difference standing beside it. The total of \autoref{3-subsec:3.7 gap and attribution} is the operator's own captured value plus the un-captured externality of C1 and C3, measured against R1, and what is reported is how that total splits between the operator and the parties that receive the remainder. Two registers are kept apart throughout and are never summed. The un-captured externality is value created by the dispatch that actually happens and reaching the operator through no mechanism; the suppressed value is the difference in what the operator can create between two regulatory settings, and it is a transfer with an enabler. Every banded figure is a band, the spread of the anchor's three grid-valuation methods carried through rather than chosen between. The subsection reports the division at every interruptible-tariff run and the operator's share of it, prices the tariff arrangement the operator holds against the standard one, and attributes the measured quantities across the four barrier types of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}, introducing no barrier that chapter does not carry.

\subsubsection{The Tariff Barrier}
\label{4-subsec:4.3.2 tariff barrier}

What the three figures below price has to be stated before any of them is read, or all three read as an argument that one tariff is better than another, which nobody disputes. The district heating operator already holds the interruptible arrangement for its entire boiler fleet (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026), and \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} establishes what that arrangement is: the standard escape from the capacity charge, and not a regulated entitlement. The capacity charge is a conditional barrier, binding in its standard form and largely neutralised for the incumbent's interruptible connections, and the condition is discretionary. The figures therefore price a discretionary arrangement the operator has no legal right to: what is at stake if it is narrowed, withdrawn or not granted, and what an operator that does not hold one faces, which is the position of any new entrant. The price is 45.68 MNOK per year at today's access in 2024, and 84.90 if the access application succeeds.

\autoref{fig:4.4 tariff waterfall} decomposes that 45.68 MNOK for the 2024 baseline against scenario 4, the same plant in the same year with the same 29 MW of access under the standard tariff. The bars are not components of the tariff; they are the components of the total cost difference the tariff causes, and only the first of them, 19.38 MNOK, is money paid to the network. The tariff changes the marginal cost of drawing power, the optimisation re-solves, and the plant dispatches differently: it buys 36.69 MNOK less electricity and burns 61.38 MNOK more peak fuel, a net 24.69 MNOK, reserve revenue falls by 2.27 MNOK and the electricity tax by 0.66. Electric-boiler heat falls from 396.8 to 282.4 GWh, by 29 per cent, and peak-fuel heat rises from 241.3 to 314.8 GWh. The mechanism is substitution rather than surcharge. The capacity charge does not mainly charge the plant more; it changes what the plant burns, and more than half of what the standard tariff costs is not what it charges but what it makes the plant do. That is the trap \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} describes, quantified on the operator's own fleet. The two totals on the right are the same difference on two bases, and the grey one is the wrong answer, shown on purpose. The optimisation minimises the costs that depend on dispatch, and the interruptible tariff's subscribed-capacity charge, 31.01 MNOK per year billed on subscribed megawatts however the plant runs, is correctly outside its objective. The moment two tariff structures are compared that constant stops being irrelevant, because the standard tariff does not pay it: differencing the two objectives counts one structure's capacity charge at zero and gives 76.69 MNOK, and including every tariff component gives 45.68. The naive comparison overstates the arrangement's worth by a factor of 1.68, and every tariff figure in this thesis is on the complete basis. One detail of the two runs is worth a sentence, because the same mechanism returns on the socio-economic side: the interruptible run's peak grid draw is exactly the 250 MW network bound, and the standard run's is 221 MW. Under the standard tariff it is the capacity charge, and not the network, that sets the peak.

\autoref{fig:4.4 tariff social} carries the same switch across the three delivery years, in two ledgers with one sign convention, down being worse. The left panel splits the operator's total into the network bill and the dispatch response it provokes. The dispatch response is stable, at $-$26.13, $-$26.30 and $-$20.19 MNOK in 2023, 2024 and 2025, while the network bill swings from $+$10.23 through $-$19.38 to $-$2.73: in 2023 the standard tariff bills the operator less, not more, because the plant cuts its peak from 250 to 127 MW to escape the capacity charge and pays less for a connection it uses less. The bill is not exogenous; it is what the plant pays after re-optimising, so 2023's positive bill is a dispatch choice rather than a discount. The honest form is that the bill the plant ends up paying is small and can go either way, and that the cost of the dispatch that gets it there is large and stable. The headline swings, from 15.90 through 45.68 to 22.91 MNOK, only because the bill does; the barrier is behavioural. The right panel sets the operator's total against the two socio-economic channels the switch moves. Under the standard tariff the plant draws less in the 88 tightest demand hours, by 20.32, 15.21 and 23.35 MW, and burns more gas, worth $-$5.04, $-$5.19 and $-$4.89 MNOK of carbon damage above the levy, so the two channels together are worth $+$15.28, $+$10.01 and $+$18.46 MNOK to society in the three years while the switch costs the operator 15.90, 45.68 and 22.91. The net the panel marks is $-$0.62, $-$35.66 and $-$4.46 MNOK, and that net is an upper bound on the social loss, because the two channels drawn are a floor on the social benefit: the three unpriced channels of \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.1 channels} are positive by construction and none of them enters the panel. A second adjustment is the central caveat of this subsection, and it does not run in one direction. The operator's total contains the network bill, which is a transfer inside society: the operator pays it and grid ratepayers receive it, and no resource is consumed. Excluding it, the net social effect of the switch is $-$10.85, $-$16.29 and $-$1.72 MNOK, within 15 MNOK of one another and negative in every year, and the transfer is what makes the drawn net look volatile; the reading that the standard tariff is near-neutral in two years and severe in one is an artefact of the bill. The thesis does not net transfers out of any ledger, because doing so would have to be done consistently across every gap row and the network bill is only one of the transfers there, so the drawn net stands and the left panel hands the reader the transfer term so that the correction can be checked. The freed grid capacity in the right panel rests on hours ranked by heat demand, and its sign is robust to the width of that mask in a way C1's flexibility relief is not: swept across all five widths in every year, the tariff relief is positive in fifteen of fifteen cells, and the two channels together run from $+$10.01 to $+$37.13 MNOK, so the one per cent figure is the conservative end of its own range. Ranked by price instead, the relief collapses and the two channels together go negative in thirteen of fifteen cells, because under a price-ranked mask the interruptible run has already curtailed for price and the two tariff structures draw nearly the same.

The policy reading, which is the reason the figure exists, is that the two barriers interact rather than add: the more reserve the operator is permitted to sell, the more the tariff structure costs, and removing the access cap without touching the tariff hands back less than the access figures alone suggest.

\subsubsection{The Access Barrier and the Attribution}
\label{4-subsec:4.3.3 attribution}

The access barrier is priced by the bracket of \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.3 reserve market participation}: between today's 29 MW and the 200 MW applied for, upward reserve revenue grows by 55.85 MNOK in 2023, 138.42 in 2024 and 118.36 in 2025. As measured it is a difference in the operator's own ledger and therefore a transfer, and it is never reported without the enabler beside it. Over the same step the peak-boiler heat that flexibility displaces grows 1.36, 1.34 and 1.76 times, and that growth, not the revenue, is the welfare part. The transfer's own social value is not computable here, for the reason given in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.1 division}, and the payer is not a single party. The system operator procures the capacity market, activation is settled through the imbalance settlement against the balance-responsible parties under the terms that govern providers of balancing services \cite{statnett_forslag_2025}, and the system operator recovers its own costs through tariffs, so the bracket is a transfer from the reserve market rather than from one named counterparty, and the chapter names none. The price-taking caveat and the market-depth measurement of \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.3 reserve market participation} attach to every figure in this paragraph.

\autoref{tab:4.4 attribution} maps the measured quantities onto the four barrier types of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}, in the form \autoref{tab:3.7 attribution} specifies, with the kind column that keeps it from being read as a ranking. The three mechanisms \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} names each carry a row: the monthly-peak capacity charge and its interruptible conditioning is the tariff row, the access regime is the institutional row, and the absence of a product paying for the services that fall outside the reserve market is the row that carries the un-captured externality, the missing-product row. Price regulation carries no arithmetic row, because heat revenue is invariant to dispatch under the delivery obligation and the model cannot see what a ceiling on it costs; that is a property of the design stated in \autoref{3-subsec:3.7 gap and attribution}, not a gap in the data. The technical row carries a number for the first time, the 2.22 MNOK of \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.1 envelope in use} in 2024 and 0.66 and 1.37 MNOK in the other years, and zero under the standard tariff. \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} argued that the technical layer bounds what the other three types can be blamed for, and the arithmetic now says how little of it: the technical row is a twentieth of the tariff row and a sixtieth of the access bracket in 2024. No barrier surfaces that \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} does not catalogue. The compounding of the tariff and access barriers found in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.2 tariff barrier} is an interaction between two catalogued mechanisms rather than a fifth, and the reserve-backing tension of \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.2 reserve and relief} sits inside the missing-product row, as a property of the one product that exists.

\subsection{Sensitivity of the Results}
\label{4-subsec:4.1.4 sensitivity analysis}

Two kinds of input are swept, and they are reported apart because they behave differently. The first kind re-solves the model and its counterfactual at each point, so both a level and a difference are recovered; those are the four named sweeps of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack}, and the delivery years read as a bound. The second kind prices quantities the runs have already produced and re-solves nothing; those are the valuation axes of \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}. Nothing appears here that \autoref{3-sec:3} has not introduced, the programme is one-at-a-time and carries no probability, and each row states what was varied and why the result moves, so that no bar has to be taken on trust.

\subsubsection{Robustness Across Delivery Years}
\label{4-subsec:4.4.3 year robustness}

The delivery years are the test of whether the two quantities the thesis leans on hardest are artefacts of one year's weather, prices and activation record, and the answer has two halves. The sign and the ordering hold in every year. The standard tariff costs more than the interruptible one at every access level in every year; the difference rises with access in every year; the flexibility value rises with access on both tariff structures in every year; and the interruptible tariff carries more flexibility value than the standard one at every scenario. The magnitudes do not hold. The tariff difference at today's access runs 15.90, 45.68 and 22.91 MNOK across 2023, 2024 and 2025, a factor of 2.9; the access bracket 55.85, 138.42 and 118.36, each an upper bound under price taking, a factor of 2.5; and the baseline flexibility value 45.01, 65.79 and 77.46, a factor of 1.7. The accumulator step is the steadiest quantity in the whole grid, at 14.85 to 22.45 MNOK. What varies is the mechanism of \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation}, how much cheap power coincided with heat load, together with, in 2023, the definition of activation the year is measured on. The supported claim is that the sign and the ordering are robust across delivery years and the magnitudes are not, and a claim stronger than that is not made.
