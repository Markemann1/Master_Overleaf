\section{Conclusion}
\label{6-sec:6 conclusion}

This thesis asked to what extent current market and regulatory conditions produce a divergence between the commercial and socio-economic value of thermal flexibility in Norwegian district heating, and what mechanisms could reduce that divergence. The divergence exists, and it takes two forms of very different size: a small share of a total that flexibility already creates and the operator does not capture, and a much larger total that the current allocation of reserve-market access stops from being created at all. The mechanisms that could reduce it were assessed against four regulatory criteria and set against a ladder of the instruments that would have to move, running from statute at the top to the system operator's own practice at the bottom. The three sub-questions of \autoref{1-subsec:1.3 research questions} are answered here in the order they were asked: what flexibility is worth to the operator in Oslo under current conditions, what the same flexibility is worth to society and how robust that estimate is, and which regulatory, market-design and tariff features contribute most to the divergence and which are most amenable to change.

Against the plant as it actually runs, flexibility is worth 65.79 MNOK per year to the operator at the 2024 baseline, earned by the accumulator and by reserve participation at the 29 MW of capacity-area access the operator holds \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.1 baseline}. Against a price-blind plant it is worth 140.87 MNOK per year. The difference between the two figures is price-responsive dispatch, worth 75.08 MNOK per year and excluded from the headline because the counterfactual defined in \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder} is the plant as it runs, and that plant already dispatches against hourly prices. The most valuable capability the plant has is therefore invisible in the figure the thesis leads with, which is a property of the chosen reference rather than a defect in the measurement. Widening access to the 200 MW the operator has applied for scales the operator-side figure to 197.75 MNOK per year, an upper bound under the price-taking assumption.

On the two socio-economic channels this thesis prices, freed grid capacity and displaced fossil peaking, the operator captures 89 per cent of a priced total of 73.73 MNOK per year at the 2024 baseline \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.1 division}. The un-captured externality is 7.94 MNOK per year, between 6.10 and 11.64 across the band spanned by the anchor study's three grid-valuation methods, measured on the demand-ranked one per cent scarce-hour mask. That figure is a share of two channels and not of the whole, it is a band rather than a point, and it belongs to the mask that produced it.

The priced total is a floor. Three of the six socio-economic channels carry no number, because no instrument at this scale can see them, and all three are positive by construction. The 89 per cent is therefore a share of what could be priced, and the evidence does not support the stronger claim that the operator captures more than society gains.

Beside that division stands a second quantity, roughly seventeen times larger. Between the 29 MW of capacity-area access the operator holds and the 200 MW it has applied for, the current allocation withholds 138.42 MNOK per year of upward reserve revenue \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.3 attribution}. Three qualifications attach to that figure. It is a transfer from the reserve market rather than a welfare quantity, because its social value is the cost saving against whoever would otherwise have supplied the reserve, and a price-taking model does not hold that cost. It is an upper bound rather than an estimate, because at applied access the modelled activated upward energy exceeds the entire NO1 zone's activated volume for the year, and the operator states that entering at that volume would depress reserve prices. And 85 per cent of its shape is the market-depth cap, whose removal alone raises the figure to 256.42 MNOK per year \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.3 reserve market participation}. The two registers are reported side by side and are never summed: the first is a share of a total that exists, the second the difference between two totals under different rules.

Across the three delivery years the results are robust in sign and in ordering, and not in magnitude \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.3 year robustness}. Every sign and ordering the thesis leans on holds in 2023, 2024 and 2025, while the flexibility value moves across them by a factor of 1.7, the access bracket by 2.5 and the tariff difference by 2.9. What drives the variation is coincidence rather than price level: value tracks whether cheap power arrived while heat was wanted, not how cheap power was on average. The same mechanism reverses the sign of the externality in 2023, where the priced socio-economic total falls below the operator's capture in every run and the externality stands at $-$7.55 MNOK per year at the baseline. In the hours the mask selects, dispatch that reserve backing required drew more power than the reference would have drawn, and the socio-economic side priced that draw at the unit value it would otherwise have priced relief.

Which barrier contributes most has three answers, because the three quantities are objects of different kinds and cannot be ranked on a single axis \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.3 attribution}. On value that is not created at all the answer is the access allocation, at 138.42 MNOK per year in 2024, a transfer. On the operator's own ledger the answer is the capacity charge, at 45.68 MNOK per year at today's access in 2024, a cost difference between the interruptible arrangement the operator holds and the standard schedule. On the divergence as this thesis defines it the answer is the missing flexibility product, at 7.94 MNOK per year, the one welfare quantity among the three. The ordering holds in all three delivery years, and it is an ordering of differently measured objects and not a ranking of barriers by importance.

The capacity charge is behavioural before it is fiscal. Of the 45.68 MNOK per year the interruptible arrangement is worth at the 2024 baseline, 19.38 is money paid to the network and 26.30 is the dispatch the charge provokes, so more than half of what the standard schedule costs the operator is what it makes the plant do rather than what it makes the plant pay \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.2 tariff barrier}. The tariff and access barriers also compound rather than add: the interruptible arrangement is worth 45.68 MNOK per year at today's 29 MW and 84.90 at the 200 MW applied for, in 2024, because a fleet holding more reserve must draw more to back it while the capacity charge is levied on the single highest hourly draw in each month. The charge bites hardest exactly when the operator does more of what the balancing market pays for. The technical barrier, by contrast, is priced and smallest on every measure, at 2.22 MNOK per year in 2024.

The instruments that would have to move form a ladder of five tiers \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications}. The heat-price ceiling sits in statute at the top; the reduced electricity-tax rate is reset in the Storting's annual budget resolution; the capacity charge's settlement basis sits in secondary legislation and the regulator's tariff regulation; the interruptible discount has been the network company's own discretion since the dedicated chapter of the tariff regulation was deleted in 2012; and at the bottom the capacity-area cap entered force as the system operator's grid-motivated practice within existing prequalification terms. The measured magnitudes run the other way along that ladder. The instrument lowest on it carries the largest measured quantity in the thesis, and the statute at the top carries no measured quantity at all, because heat revenue is invariant to dispatch. The cheapest instrument to change is also the most valuable one to change, and that coincidence sits at the bottom of the ladder.

That ranking points at something already under examination: the system operator reports that it is studying whether the capacity-area limits could in time be applied at bid clearing rather than at prequalification, with no finished solution and no timeline. Two qualifications ride with it. The 22 to 33 MW band that the current regime argues over sits on the flat top of the access curve, so the reform being contested is not the one that would matter most; and because the tariff and access barriers interact, removing the cap without touching the tariff would hand back less than the access figures alone suggest. What is stated here is the output of a ranking and not a recommendation. It rests on one case, one operator and one interview, its largest quantity is an upper bound, its welfare quantities are a floor on two channels of six, and political feasibility was excluded from the criteria by design.

The contribution is a paired commercial and socio-economic valuation of thermal flexibility for a single Norwegian district heating system, in which the gap between the two perspectives is quantified rather than the two being presented as parallel analyses, and in which the value the operator creates without capturing is separated from the value the current access allocation prevents it from creating \autoref{1-subsec:1.5 contribution}. To the author's knowledge, no paired valuation of this kind has previously been applied to a Norwegian district heating system at the operator level with explicit quantification of the gap. The closest precedent applies a comparable paired structure to a Danish biomass-based island system, and a second study pairs a plant-level monetisation with a regional macroeconomic layer that reports gross regional product and employment rather than a welfare balance, so it transfers as a method and not as a paired comparator.

How far the two aims were met is best stated aim by aim, with what bounds each. The first aim, to measure the divergence, was met on two of the six socio-economic channels, so the measured total is a floor and the division is a share of what could be priced. The largest quantity the thesis reports is an upper bound rather than an estimate, because price taking fails at applied access and no reserve supply curve for NO1 exists against which to correct it \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}. What the operator captures is overstated by at least a third of the baseline flexibility value through foresight of reserve prices and activation, an overstatement confined to the reserve leg; the ordering of the two registers survives it, because it falls on the operator's own captured value and not on the externality beside it. The sign of the un-captured externality turns on how scarce hours are defined, and is positive in twelve of ninety mask cells, so every conclusion drawn about it carries the mask that produced it. The evidence base is one case, one operator and one interview: the regulator-side and system-operator perspectives the design sought were not obtained, and every operator-sourced claim is written as a statement about what this operator does or reports \autoref{3-subsec:3.9 validity}. The model under-dispatches the electric boilers against the public register in every delivery year and fails the share test in two of them, so its account of a high-price year is not a description of what the plant did. The physical envelope's energy side stayed unquantified, so the two perimeters are compared in kroner per year and never per unit of physical resource. The second aim, to identify what could reduce the divergence, was met as a ranking on a single case and not as a recommendation, with political feasibility excluded from the criteria by design. What this thesis established, it established by measurement against a stated counterfactual, with a band and a mask attached; what it inferred, it marked as inference.

Two directions for further work follow from those bounds, and each would close one of them. The first is a Norwegian district heating flexibility valuation framework that codifies what the corpus has not converged on, because every triangulated socio-economic estimate in this thesis carries the method risk of a literature that has not agreed how to monetise the same physical service. The second is a reserve supply curve for NO1, because it is the single addition that would turn the applied-access upper bound into an estimate; it would change the size of the enabler and leave its sign and its rank where they are. Neither those two nor any of the other directions set out in \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work} would change the ordering of the three registers, the direction of the tariff result or the location of the storage barrier. What further work would change is how precisely the divergence can be sized. What it would not change is the shape this thesis measured: in Oslo today the smaller part of the divergence is a division of value that flexibility already creates, and the larger part is value that the current allocation of access does not allow to exist.
