\section{Discussion}
\label{5-sec:5 discussion}

This chapter weighs what \autoref{4-sec:4 results} measured. \autoref{5-subsec:5.1 interpretation of results} reads the results in the terms of the research question and establishes the three categories of value the rest of the chapter works with. \autoref{5-subsec:5.2 implementation barriers} turns the measured attribution into verdicts on the four barrier types of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} and answers which of them contributes most. \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications} assesses the mechanisms that could reduce the divergence and ranks them against the instruments that would have to move. \autoref{5-subsec:5.4 transferability} states how far the finding travels and under what conditions. \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work} states what the study's limitations cost its conclusions and what work follows from them. Two rules govern every sentence below. Nothing is reported here for the first time: every quantity restates one \autoref{4-sec:4 results} presents, carried with the band, the scarce-hour mask and the reference that chapter attaches to it, and no arithmetic is performed on it. And no conclusion rests on a cost level, for the reason \autoref{3-subsec:3.2 evidence base} gives and \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.1 lp sensitivities} quantifies.

\subsection{Interpretation of Results}
\label{5-subsec:5.1 interpretation of results}

The thesis set out to measure a divergence between the commercial and socio-economic value of thermal flexibility, and found one that takes two forms of very different size. This subsection reads the two together, answers the first and second sub-questions of \autoref{1-subsec:1.3 research questions} at the level of meaning, and fixes the vocabulary the three subsections after it use.

Three categories of value came out of the analysis, and they differ in kind rather than in size. The first is value the dispatch creates today for which the operator receives nothing: 7.94 MNOK per year at the 2024 baseline run, between 6.10 and 11.64 across the freed-grid unit-value band, on the demand-ranked one per cent scarce-hour mask \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.1 division}. The second is value the access allocation stops from being created at all: 138.42 MNOK per year of upward reserve revenue between the 29 MW the operator holds and the 200 MW it has applied for \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.3 attribution}. The third carries no number at all, because no instrument at this scale can see it: lower system prices in scarce hours, faster grid connection for queued load, and freed hydropower and reservoir value are reported in words and are positive by construction \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.1 channels}. \autoref{tab:4.4 attribution} is where the first two stand side by side with the kind of each stated, and the kind is what stops them being ranked on magnitude.

That division answers \autoref{1-subsec:1.2 problem statement} in a way the problem statement anticipated and could not yet size. The divergence takes two forms, one a question of how an existing total is divided and the other a question of what the total is allowed to be, and the form that the split-incentive condition of \cite{sneum_barriers_2021} describes is the smaller of the two in Oslo today. Value created and not captured is on the order of a tenth of what the operator captures. Value the allocation withholds is on the order of twice it, roughly seventeen times the externality \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.1 division}. What the Oslo case therefore presents is mainly a blockage upstream of the transfer, and \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications} argues the policy consequence.

Before any of those magnitudes is weighed, one property of the evidence has to be stated, because it decides which comparisons the chapter may make. The sensitivity programme moves the annual cost level by hundreds of millions of kroner and the differences by single millions: sweeping the waste-incineration carbon levy across its full range moves the level by 623 MNOK and the flexibility value by 5.85 \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.1 lp sensitivities}. That is the quantified form of the evidential tiering of \autoref{3-subsec:3.2 evidence base}, and it is why no conclusion in this chapter rests on a level. Every claim below is a difference taken within one delivery year, between two runs that share every held parameter, and the held parameters cancel exactly in such a difference. \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work} states what that discipline costs.

The division between the operator and everyone else is small, and it is a floor. On the two priced channels the operator captures 89 per cent of the priced socio-economic total at the 2024 baseline, 85 to 94 per cent across the 2024 and 2025 access axis, and more than the whole of it in every 2023 run; in ten of the eighteen runs the capture exceeds the priced total \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.1 division}. The qualifier in that sentence is doing real work: the result holds on the two channels the thesis prices. The stronger sentence, that the operator captures more than society gains, is not available, because three channels of the six carry no number and all three are positive by construction.

The difference between a zero and an unpriced channel is what \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation} hands this chapter, and the three unpriced channels are three separate cases rather than one omission. Lower system prices in scarce hours is the one a richer instrument could price, and pricing it needs the willingness-to-pay curve that the price-taking design of \autoref{1-subsec:1.4 scope and limitations} declared out of scope. Faster grid connection is unpriced for a physical reason: one megawatt of headroom is either permanently avoided reinforcement or capacity released to a new load and cannot be both, and freed grid capacity already prices it once. Freed hydropower and reservoir value is unpriced by the scope guardrail rather than by any doubt about the mechanism. The small externality is therefore two findings at once. It is a finding about relative magnitude, and it is a lower bound, and both halves travel with it wherever it is quoted.

The suppressed value is a transfer, and what it enables is the welfare part. As measured, the access bracket is a difference in the operator's own ledger between two regulatory settings, so it is a transfer from the reserve market; its own social value is the cost saving against whoever would otherwise have supplied that reserve, which a price-taking model does not hold and cannot compute \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.1 division}. What access does to welfare is visible in the physical quantities the priced channels value: peak-boiler heat displaced by flexibility grows 1.36 times across the access axis in 2023, 1.34 in 2024 and 1.76 in 2025 \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.3 attribution}. Access produces the externality rather than reallocating it. Three qualifications travel with every use of the bracket, and each is a separate claim. It is a transfer and not a welfare quantity. It is an upper bound rather than an estimate, because at applied access in 2025 the model's own activated upward energy is 1.20 times the whole NO1 zone's activated volume for the year, and the operator states that entering at that volume would push reserve prices down (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). And 85 per cent of its shape is the market-depth cap, whose removal raises the bracket from 138.42 to 256.42 MNOK \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.3 reserve market participation}. The two registers are reported side by side and are never summed.

Separating what the operator captures as a saving of real resources from what it captures as a transfer sharpens that reading, and \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.1 division} reports the split and leaves the weighing here. At the 2024 baseline only 4.30 MNOK of the 65.79 the operator captures is a saving of real resources, 26.44 MNOK less fuel and variable dispatch cost against 22.15 MNOK more electricity purchased at spot, and 61.49 MNOK is transfers. Above today's access the resource saving turns negative in every year, to $-$33.57 and $-$58.48 MNOK at partial and applied access in 2024, because the fleet buys more power to back more reserve than it saves in fuel. Set against the resource saving rather than against total receipts, the 7.94 MNOK externality is nearly twice what the operator captures in real resources, and at every 2024 and 2025 run above today's access it is the only positive resource effect the priced analysis contains. The consequence is the sentence \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications} needs. A case for widening access cannot rest on the operator's revenue, because that revenue is a transfer whose welfare value the design cannot compute. It rests on the enabled externality, which is small in kroner and is the only welfare quantity in the table. The operator's interest and society's point the same way here, and the thesis can say which reason is which.

What flexibility is worth depends on what it is measured against, and the thesis prints both answers rather than choosing between them. Against the plant as it runs, flexibility is worth 65.79 MNOK per year at the 2024 baseline. Against a price-blind plant it is worth 140.87, of which 75.08 is price-responsive dispatch that the headline excludes because the counterfactual already dispatches on price \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.1 baseline}. \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder} argued that a single reference would hide the choice inside the result, and the ladder is what makes the choice visible: the largest capability the plant has is invisible in the number the thesis leads with, and that is a property of the reference rather than a defect in it.

On carbon the two references disagree in sign, which is the sharper form of the same point. Against the plant as it runs, the flexible fleet burns less gas in every year, by 5{,}539, 12{,}499 and 13{,}783 MWh of heat at the baseline in 2023, 2024 and 2025. Against the price-blind reference the whole ladder burns more gas in 2024 and 2025, by 52{,}062 and 94{,}820 MWh, so the channel reads $-$7.45 and $-$13.57 MNOK, and it burns less in 2023 and reads $+$6.93 \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.1 channels}. The reading is that the sign of a flexibility benefit can turn on a modelling choice, and that a monetised flexibility benefit is therefore only interpretable with its counterfactual named. The guard on it is the one \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder} sets: the price-blind reference is a decomposition device and not a state any regulator could choose, and the gap row is measured against the plant as it runs.

The sign of the freed-grid channel is a statement about how reserve is procured. It is positive in 2024 and 2025 and negative in 2023 on the headline mask, and positive in six of the ninety mask definitions swept \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.1 channels}. \autoref{fig:4.3 reserve peak} is where the mechanism is separated from the sign: it splits the scarce hours into those carrying a reserve commitment and those without one, and shows the share in which the boiler sits exactly on the floor that backing the commitment requires. Three things follow for this chapter. The negative sign is a property of the priced total rather than of welfare, because the three unpriced channels are positive by construction. It is the one sensitivity in the whole programme on which a sign turns rather than a size, so every figure carrying it carries its mask. And it hands \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications} a product-design question: a capacity product that paid for availability without requiring backed load in the hour would not produce the effect at all.

Across delivery years the results are robust in sign and ordering and not in magnitude, and the mechanism behind the variation is coincidence rather than price level. Flexibility value at the baseline is 45.01, 65.79 and 77.46 MNOK in 2023, 2024 and 2025, with 144.9 MNOK in 2021 and 51.2 in 2022 reported as a bound on the binary activation rule under held policy \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.2 spot arbitrage}. Value does not track the price level, whose rank correlation with it across the five years is $-0.5$; it tracks whether cheap power arrived while heat was wanted. \autoref{tab:4.1 2023 mechanism} isolates that mechanism by holding the price level equal: 2023 and 2021 share a mean price to within three kroner, and 2023 is worth a third as much because 18 per cent of its cheap hours fell in the heating season against 67 per cent of 2021's. Every sign and ordering the thesis leans on holds in all three delivery years, while the tariff difference moves by a factor of 2.9, the access bracket by 2.5 and the flexibility value by 1.7 \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.3 year robustness}. The word robust is only ever used here with that second half attached.

The one external check available on the reserve mechanism agrees with the model at the order of magnitude, and the way it agrees is itself a result. \autoref{fig:4.2 reserve benchmark} sets the modelled capacity payment per megawatt of eligible capacity against the anchor report's own case exercise on the operator's boilers, NOK 330{,}000 per MW per year in FCR-N capacity across an October to April season \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. The modelled figure is 1.03, 2.80 and 1.95 times that benchmark at today's access in the three years, and it falls as eligible capacity rises, to 0.50 MNOK per MW at 200 MW in 2024. \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.3 reserve market participation} establishes that the comparison is fair rather than flattering, because the published estimate is conservative on its own terms: it assumes delivery across the months the same report places 30 to 40 per cent below the annual average for capacity prices. What this chapter adds is the generalisation. The published figure states no participating volume and is volume-independent by construction, while the modelled value falls as volume rises, so a flat per-megawatt benchmark applied to a small holding is biased low. That is a caution about how this literature's unit values should be used, and it is bounded to the one comparison the thesis is able to make: the products differ, the benchmark counts capacity only, and the near-exact match at today's access in 2023 is a coincidence at one point between two sources sharing no method.

\subsection{Challenges and Barriers of Implementation}
\label{5-subsec:5.2 implementation barriers}

This subsection answers the first half of the third sub-question, which asks which regulatory, market-design and grid-tariff features contribute most to the divergence. It takes the four barrier types of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} in that chapter's order and states a verdict on each. The mapping between the two chapters needs one sentence before the verdicts begin. \autoref{tab:4.4 attribution} carries five rows against four categories, because the count of rows is set by what the model can separate and price rather than by the taxonomy: the monthly-peak capacity charge, the access regime and the absent flexibility product are three distinct mechanisms that \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} already names, and each carries its own number. The kind column of that table is what keeps the five from being read as a rival taxonomy or as a league table. No barrier surfaced in the analysis that \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} does not catalogue.

The technical barrier is priced, small, and a different limit from the one the corpus expects. It carries a number for the first time in this thesis, 2.22 MNOK per year in 2024 and 0.66 and 1.37 in the other two years, and nothing at all under the standard tariff, where the capacity charge holds the fleet below the network limit before the network limit is reached \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.3 attribution}. That is a twentieth of the tariff row and a sixtieth of the access bracket, and the regulatory distance between the second and third capacity tiers is worth roughly sixty times the technical distance between the first and second \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.1 envelope in use}. \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} set itself the test of whether the corpus claim that technical barriers are the most tractable of the four holds in this case, and on the thesis's own evidence it does. What binds physically is the heat network rather than the electrical connection, on the operator's own account, so the technical barrier here is a debottlenecking question about capacity that is already standing and not the connection queue, which the row does not price. That claim rests on a single source and is a statement about what this operator reports \autoref{3-subsec:3.9 validity}.

The operating economics of storage are not the barrier either, which is the second half of the technical verdict and a negative result. Every step of accumulator expansion across the swept range pays back in 0.2 to 6.0 years at the catalogue's control cost, against a technical life of 30 to 50 years from the same source, on dispatch value alone \autoref{tab:4.2 storage payback}. Three properties of that screening travel with it: the payback is simple and undiscounted, it prices capital cost alone with fixed operating cost not netted against the value, and it counts the model's own dispatch value with no reserve-market growth, no replacement and no decommissioning. Within those bounds the tank's capital cost is never what limits the value the dispatch can extract from it. Whatever holds storage investment back therefore sits upstream of dispatch, in the enabling overhead, siting and capital access the screening omits, and in the access rules that decide how much of the value is reachable at all.

The capacity charge is expensive to the operator, about a third as costly to welfare, and behavioural before it is fiscal. \autoref{fig:4.4 tariff waterfall} decomposes what the interruptible arrangement is worth at the 2024 baseline: 45.68 MNOK per year, of which 19.38 is money paid to the network and 26.30 is the dispatch response the charge provokes, buying 36.69 MNOK less electricity and burning 61.38 MNOK more peak fuel, with electric-boiler heat falling 29 per cent. More than half of what the standard tariff costs is what it makes the plant do. The two channels the switch moves are worth $+$15.28, $+$10.01 and $+$18.46 MNOK to society across 2023, 2024 and 2025. Once the network bill is treated as the transfer inside society that it is, the net social effect of the switch is $-$10.85, $-$16.29 and $-$1.72 MNOK, negative in every year and within 15 MNOK of one another \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.2 tariff barrier}. The interruptible arrangement is socially defensible in every delivery year on the thesis's own measurement, and that is the flat form of the answer.

The barrier's real shape follows from what the arrangement is rather than from what it is worth. \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} establishes that the interruptible tariff is the standard escape from the capacity charge and not a regulated entitlement: the dedicated chapter of the tariff regulation was deleted in 2012, and the discount has been the network company's own discretion since. The operator holds it for its entire boiler fleet (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026), so what the figures price is exposure to a discretionary arrangement being narrowed or withdrawn, and the position of any new entrant that is not granted one. The behavioural half is what makes that exposure large. Under the standard tariff price-responsive dispatch is worth 12 to 51 per cent less and the accumulator is worth 0.66 to 0.77 of its interruptible value, because chasing a cheap hour raises the month's settlement peak \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.1 baseline}. That is the measurement \autoref{fig:2.3.2 effektledd timeline} anticipated and this thesis is the first to make on the operator's own fleet. The cost also compounds with access, from 45.68 MNOK at today's 29 MW to 65.74 at 100 MW and 84.90 at 200 MW in 2024, because a fleet holding more reserve must draw more to back it and the charge bills the highest hourly draw of the month \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.2 tariff barrier}.

One further consequence follows by inference rather than by measurement, and it is marked as such because the model tests dispatch and not investment. The same charge falls on the peak that a new boiler raises, and \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} places a connection charge and a queue on capacity addition, while assumption A8 of \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions} holds investment outside the objective entirely. The measured quantity is therefore a lower bound on what the tariff structure costs, in the specific sense that it prices the operating consequence of a rule that also bears on the investment decision the model does not represent.

The missing product is the barrier that produces the divergence as defined, and it is small in kroner for reasons specific to this case. The un-captured externality is 7.94 MNOK per year at the 2024 baseline, $-$7.55 in 2023 and 13.29 in 2025, and it is the one welfare quantity in the attribution table \autoref{tab:4.4 attribution}. \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} gives the diagnosis: the heat market clears first and is myopic to the electricity market, no bilateral flexibility contract or purpose-designed capacity product exists, district heating participation in the seasonal balancing-capacity option market was negligible, and the new capacity market for manual reserve pays availability inside the reserve window and nothing outside it. What this thesis adds is what the missing product is worth today, and why that is a small number. The peak fleet is mostly biogenic, so the fossil tranche carries only 0.33 to 0.47 of the peak heat flexibility displaces \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.1 channels}. And grid relief appears only where the backing floor is slack, which puts the reserve-backing tension inside this row as a property of the one product that does exist rather than as a separate barrier.

The regulatory layer binds through a revenue side the model cannot see, and the one instrument it can see binds only if it is lost. Heat revenue is invariant to dispatch under the delivery obligation, so the price ceiling of the Energy Act \S5-5 carries no arithmetic row, and that silence is a property of the design stated in advance \autoref{tab:3.7 attribution}. What the model cannot supply, \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers} attests. The ceiling tracks the price of electric heating; the consumer price-support scheme pushed that price down; the heat price followed; and the operator absorbed the difference uncompensated for three to four years while power suppliers were reimbursed by the state. Extending the state-funded scheme to district heating customers on 1 October 2025 moved that burden to the state and left the ceiling in place, now indexed to a politically set retail price. Both schemes enter the analysis only as the institutional context of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}, and the account of the episode rests on a single operator source alongside the anchor report's independent statement of the same conclusion. The instrument the model does reach runs the other way. Withdrawing the reduced electricity tax would cost 60.41 MNOK per year at the calendar-weighted blend and remove a third of boiler heat, which is comparable to the entire flexibility value and larger than the tariff barrier \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.1 lp sensitivities}. It is a ceiling risk and not a barrier whose removal releases value, which is why it carries no attribution row. The grid code belongs to this layer too, as cumulative compliance conditions that govern how a portfolio is presented rather than how much of it there is.

The institutional layer carries the largest measured quantity, and the rest of the category explains why the product stays missing. The access allocation is worth 55.85, 138.42 and 118.36 MNOK across the three years as a transfer with an enabler \autoref{tab:4.4 attribution}. \autoref{fig:4.2 access curve} is where its shape can be read, and the shape is the finding. Today's whole regulatory band runs from the 22 MW practical ceiling for a single large regulating object, through the 29 MW the operator holds, to the 33 MW published ceiling for its capacity area. All of it sits on the flat top of the curve, where the marginal megawatt is worth 1.49 against 1.46 MNOK per year, so the zone the current regime argues over changes almost nothing. The value is in the 100 to 200 MW range that no allocation offers, and the curve saturates at the 250 MW network bound, above which the barrier stops being regulatory. The three other institutional conditions of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.4 institutional barriers} carry no row, and the model says why for two of them. Aggregation exists to let sub-threshold actors clear the minimum bid, and the reservation of one megawatt in each area for flexibility groups is a carve-out for the small, so for the largest operator the constraint inverts and the missing enabler is allocation rather than eligibility. The disconnect between distribution and transmission system operators is the reason the product stays missing, so the model prices the consequence without pricing the cause. Vertical integration is left as \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.4 institutional barriers} leaves it, as a structural condition rather than an evaluative judgement, and the interview did not settle it.

The system operator's own account of the access cap is the strongest institutional evidence in the thesis, because it comes from the party that set it. Statnett assesses the cap on prequalified reserve volume from grids below 110 kV as a structural challenge that will take time to resolve rather than a short-lived friction. It states that capping prequalified volume rather than accepted bids does risk leaving available regulating capacity unused, and reports that it is examining whether the limits could in time be applied at bid clearing instead, with no solution today \cite{statnett_sporsmal_2026}. The measured access curve and that statement point at the same thing from opposite ends.

Which barrier contributes most has three answers, because the three quantities are different objects. On value not created at all the answer is the access allocation, at 138.42 MNOK in 2024, a transfer and an upper bound. On the operator's own ledger the answer is the capacity charge, at 45.68 MNOK, a cost difference between two tariff regimes. On the divergence as the thesis defines it the answer is the missing product, at 7.94 MNOK, the one welfare quantity among them. \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.3 attribution} states that ordering as an ordering of differently measured objects and not as a ranking of barriers by importance, and the reason is the firewall the whole valuation is built on: a transfer, a foreclosure and an externality cannot be ranked on one axis without the category error that firewall exists to prevent. The ordering holds in all three delivery years. The technical layer is smallest on every measure, which closes the test \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} set. And the compounding of the tariff and access barriers is an interaction between two catalogued mechanisms rather than a fifth barrier, which matters for reform sequencing and is taken up next.

\subsection{Policy, Market, and Business Model Implications}
\label{5-subsec:5.3 implications}

The second clause of the main research question asks what mechanisms could reduce the divergence, and the second half of the third sub-question asks which of them are most amenable to change. This subsection answers both. It assesses five candidate mechanisms in the four regulatory criteria that \autoref{3-subsec:3.9 validity} adopted as the vocabulary for exactly this purpose, ranks them against the instruments that would have to move, and stops there. It works on the enablers rather than on where the perimeter boundary sits, which is the commitment \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} made before the results were in.

Two mechanism types run through everything that follows, and they answer to different instruments. A redistributive mechanism moves value that has already been created from the external share into the operator's. The total stays where it was. An enabling mechanism removes a precondition, so that flexible operation produces value it was previously prevented from producing. \autoref{3-subsec:3.7 gap and attribution} states the consequence this subsection argues from: redistribution alone is neutral in welfare terms, and the case for it is the second step, that changing what the operator earns changes what it dispatches and invests in, and that response moves the total. The test of any instrument is therefore whether it changes an operating or an investment decision. The gap itself does not have to close for an instrument to have worked. That test is where Norwegian legislation has already drawn the line. The proposition amending the Energy Act on surplus-heat utilisation states that the benefits at issue are mainly societal while the decisions rest on business-economic criteria, that requirements imposing costs beyond an actor's own business-economic benefit are a disproportionate burden, and that the duty it creates binds only where a project is business-economically profitable \cite{det_kongelige_energidepartement_endringer_2026}. The under-investment equilibrium of \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap} is what that boundary produces when the societal benefit is real and the private one is not.

The two types are worth very different amounts here, and on different registers. The redistributive question is worth 7.94 MNOK per year at the operator's actual position, between 6.10 and 11.64 across the band. The enabling question is worth what wider access lets the dispatch produce: displaced peaking heat grows 1.36, 1.34 and 1.76 times across the access axis in the three delivery years, and freed grid capacity grows with it, from 6.15 to 9.27 MW of relief in 2024 and from 11.31 to 39.36 MW in 2025 \autoref{tab:4.3 channels}. The reserve revenue the allocation withholds is 138.42 MNOK per year, roughly seventeen times the externality. That figure is reported here as what it is and no more. It is a transfer between reserve providers rather than a welfare gain. It is an upper bound rather than an estimate, because price taking fails at that volume. And 85 per cent of its shape is the market-depth cap. The policy reading follows from the asymmetry and has to be stated plainly, because it is the obvious objection to the ranking below. A pure redistribution argument addresses the smaller register, and the enabling argument addresses the larger one, but the welfare quantity attached to the enabler is itself small in kroner while the transfer beside it is large. The honest form is that the transfer's welfare value is unknown rather than zero: the displaced provider's cost is not held by a price-taking model, and the priced total is a floor with three of its six channels carrying no number. The enabling case therefore rests on a small measured welfare quantity that grows with access, and on three unpriced channels that are positive by construction, and not on the reserve revenue itself.

One property of the transfer bears directly on the criteria below. Its payer is not a single party: the system operator procures the capacity, activation settles through the imbalance settlement against balance-responsible parties, and the system operator recovers its own costs through tariffs \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.3 attribution}. An access reform therefore names no counterparty who could object to being charged, which is unusual among the five candidates and matters under revenue adequacy.

\autoref{tab:5.3 mechanisms} assesses the five candidate mechanisms in the four criteria. All five are mechanisms the earlier chapters already carry, and every cell restates something those chapters establish. The four criteria compete with one another, as the source that defines them says they do, so each mechanism is assessed on all four rather than scored on one \cite{bergaentzle_electricity_2019}. Where a cell would need a judgement the thesis has no evidence for, it says so.

Amenability is what the third sub-question asks for, and the chapters supply the discriminator: where each instrument lives. \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers} points at exactly this, noting that the heat-price ceiling sits in statute while the capacity charge sits in secondary legislation, and calling that asymmetry a reform-path difference. Read across all five candidates, the instruments form a ladder of five tiers, from hardest to easiest to move. The \S5-5 ceiling is statute, unchanged in wording since 2008. The electricity-tax rate is set in the Storting's annual budget resolution. The capacity charge's settlement basis sits in secondary legislation and the regulator's tariff regulation, which already permits an averaged rather than a single-maximum peak, while the large-customer schedules use the single-maximum form. The interruptible discount has been the network company's own discretion since the dedicated chapter of the tariff regulation was deleted in 2012. And the capacity-area cap entered force as the system operator's grid-motivated practice within existing prequalification terms, through operational discretion rather than through the regulatory-approval route \cite{statnett_sporsmal_2026}.

Set the measured magnitudes against that ladder and they run the other way. The instrument lowest on it, the capacity-area cap, carries the largest measured quantity in the thesis. The statute at the top carries no measured quantity at all, because heat revenue is invariant to dispatch and the design says so in advance. The cheapest thing to change is also the most valuable thing to change, and that coincidence sits at the bottom of the ladder. Naming that is the output of the ranking. It is not a recommendation, and the reasons are given below.

Three qualifications ride with the ranking and none of them is optional. The first is that the zone the current regime argues over is not where the value is: the whole 22 to 33 MW band sits on the flat top of the access curve, so the reform that would matter is not the one being contested \autoref{fig:4.2 access curve}. The second is sequencing. The tariff and access barriers interact rather than add, so removing the cap without touching the tariff hands back less than the access figures alone suggest, and the interaction strengthens as access widens \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.2 tariff barrier}. The third is that amenability cuts both ways. The reduced electricity tax is a rate the Storting resets every year, which makes it among the easiest instruments on the ladder to move, and withdrawing it would cost 60.41 MNOK per year and a third of boiler heat. Eligibility for the reduced rate attaches to the legal entity through a fuel-mix test that electric boilers do not help satisfy, so an operator that electrified far enough could fail the test on its own success \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}. Ease of movement is a property of an instrument and not a direction of travel.

The live alternatives at the bottom of the ladder are the system operator's own, which is worth saying because it means the ranking points at something already under examination rather than at a lever this thesis invented. Statnett reports that it is studying whether the capacity-area limits could in time be applied at bid clearing instead of at prequalification, and separately an earlier grid-clearance step in the connection process, so that network conditions including capacity-area headroom would be settled when connection is applied for. It reports no finished solution and no timeline for either \cite{statnett_sporsmal_2026}.

The business-model half of the question has a negative answer, and the negative answer is the useful one. An instrument aimed at the operating margin of storage is aimed at the wrong thing, because every expansion step across the swept range already pays back well inside the asset's life on dispatch value alone. What a business model would have to reach is upstream of dispatch: the enabling overhead, siting and capital access the screening omits, and the access rules that set how much of the value is reachable. The operator built its accumulator anyway, on projected rather than only observed conditions \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics}, which is consistent with a screening in which the operating economics were never the constraint.

The other half of the storage result is that the operator's optimum and society's are not the same one, and that they part company in a direction that depends on the year. \autoref{fig:4.3 storage peak} follows both perimeters across the accumulator sweep at applied access: private value rises with store size in every year, while coincident-peak relief rises in 2024, falls in 2025 by 12.2 MW across the first 124 MWh and by 14.6 MW across the first 248, and turns into loading in 2023. The misalignment that \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap} took from the Danish island case is therefore measured here on one plant, and it runs in both directions. The instrument consequence is specific. A business model that paid for storage per megawatt-hour installed would pay for relief in one year and for loading in another, so an instrument that internalises this externality has to pay for the hours rather than for the asset. Redesigning the heat price would not do it either, because that moves value only along the operator-to-customer axis and the transfer this needs runs between the operator and the power system. Both readings inherit the screening's own bounds, which are that it is undiscounted, capital-cost only, and priced on the model's dispatch value alone.

One boundary has to be restated here because a policy discussion is where it would most easily be crossed. The anchor report's headline of NOK 1 to 1.6 billion per year is the socio-economic value of converting 1.0 to 1.5 TWh of electric heating to district heating, and this thesis measures what today's system delivers through dispatch \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}. The two answer different questions and are never set beside each other. The small externality measured here is not a fraction of that headline, and no instrument assessed above is being weighed against it. The same report states that greater use of heat storage and optimised boiler operation frees capacity on top of the conversion effect, which is the increment this analysis is anchored on \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}.

What the subsection is not saying should be as clear as what it is. It assesses and ranks; it does not recommend. It has no evidence on political feasibility, and the criteria above deliberately exclude it. Its policy sentences rest on one case, one operator and one interview, its largest quantity is an upper bound rather than an estimate, and its welfare quantities are a floor on two channels of six. The ranking is offered at that strength and no other.

\subsection{Conditional Transferability}
\label{5-subsec:5.4 transferability}

\autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} states that a single case examined in depth says nothing about the population of Norwegian district heating systems, and commits this subsection to developing the conditional transferability of the findings to Trondheim, Bergen and the smaller Nordic operators, with the four regulatory criteria as the scaffold. The claim being made is analytical generalisation to theory. It is never statistical generalisation to a population, and the distinction decides what may be carried across a system boundary and what may not.

Three things transfer. The existence of the gap transfers wherever the conditions of the mechanism hold, because the mechanism is institutional rather than physical: a price ceiling that suppresses the flexibility signal, an access rule that caps what may be sold, and a tariff that bills the hour flexibility raises are rules, and rules apply to whoever is inside them. The ordering of the causes transfers, because it survived a test the design can actually run: across three delivery years of different weather, prices and activation records, every sign and every ordering the thesis leans on held while every magnitude moved by a factor between 1.7 and 2.9 \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.3 year robustness}. The magnitude does not transfer, and the design forbids claiming that it does. Cost structure, fuel mix and store size are local to Oslo, and they are what set the size of every quantity reported. One qualification travels with the ordering claim: as \autoref{5-subsec:5.2 implementation barriers} establishes, the ordering is an ordering of differently measured objects, so what transfers is that ordering and not a ranking of barriers by importance.

The four conditions of \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.3 norwegian own case} are the test of how far the finding travels, and they do not all reach the same distance. The hydropower water-value pathway is Norway-wide and absent in Denmark and Sweden, and where it is absent the socio-economic channels differ in kind rather than in size. The \S5-5 ceiling is Norway-wide and statutory, so it reaches Trondheim and Bergen unchanged. The market-access architecture is Norway-wide in its rules and binds differently by scale, which is the subject of the scale condition below. The carbon levy trajectory on a waste-fired base load is Oslo-shaped, because Trondheim and Bergen have different merit orders and a levy acts through whatever sits at the margin. The finding travels exactly as far as the conditions do, and the fourth is the one that stops soonest.

Reading the comparators through the four criteria shows why a Norwegian comparison and a Nordic one are different exercises. Within Norway the instruments are national, so non-discrimination and simplicity and stability read identically for Trondheim and Bergen, and revenue adequacy sits with the same regulator and the same revenue-cap model. What differs is economic efficiency, because the same tariff signal and the same access cap act on a different merit order and on a smaller store or none at all, so an identical instrument buys a different amount of dispatch. Across the Nordic comparators the instruments themselves differ, so the criteria are read on different objects: \autoref{tab:2.3.5 cross country comparison} records time-of-use design and a progressive tariff reform trajectory in Denmark, capacity-based components and a concluded local-market pilot in Sweden, a harmonised 1 MW bid floor across all three, and capacity-area caps on reserve prequalification that have no Danish or Swedish counterpart. The Nordic comparison is therefore a comparison of enabler sets rather than of magnitudes, and it is the compounding of the Norwegian set rather than any single condition that has no counterpart.

The thesis can also test transfer inside its own case. The same plant was solved under three years of weather, prices and activation records, and 2023 is the year in which cheap power and heat load did not coincide at all \autoref{tab:4.1 2023 mechanism}. A finding that survives 2023 has survived a change in market conditions larger than the difference between two Norwegian cities in the same year, which is what makes the within-case robustness relevant to a between-case claim. The bound around it is the five-year axis, on which 2021 is the largest flexibility value anywhere in the run set, reported as a bound under held policy and on the binary activation rule rather than as a central case \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.2 spot arbitrage}. What none of this says is anything about a different fuel mix or a different store, which is the next condition.

Scale is a condition in its own right, and it inverts. For a small operator the institutional barrier is eligibility and the absent aggregation framework; for the largest it is allocation above holdings it already has, so the two ends of the size distribution meet opposite versions of the same architecture \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap}. The reservation of one megawatt in each capacity area for flexibility groups is a carve-out for the small that reduces what a large object can reach. Storage separates the two ends further. The Norwegian tanks recorded outside Oslo run to 280 MWh at Hamar, 350 at Heimdal, 117 at Gardermoen and 60 at Bodø, and not every Norwegian system has a tank at all \autoref{2-subsec:2.2.3 thermal storage}. The accumulator step is the steadiest quantity in the whole run grid, at 14.85 to 22.45 MNOK across every tariff structure and delivery year \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.3 year robustness}, so the storage finding transfers in direction to any operator holding a tank and in no magnitude at all to one without.

The fuel-mix conditional runs in the thesis's favour, which is worth stating because it is the one condition under which the Oslo result is conservative rather than local. Oslo's carbon channel is small because three of its four peak fuels are biogenic and the fossil tranche carries only 0.33 to 0.47 of the peak heat that flexibility displaces \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.1 channels}. In a system whose peak stack is fossil the same physical flexibility would displace more carbon. In a fossil-dominated power system a load absorbing off-peak electricity displaces marginal thermal generation rather than freeing hydropower, which \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power} identifies as the value logic Norway does not share. The Oslo externality is therefore a lower bound for systems unlike Oslo, and the smallness of the measured divergence is a property of a decarbonised peak fleet rather than a weakness in the measurement.

The hydropower qualifier is mandatory and it limits the Nordic half of the claim. In a hydropower-dominated system, district heating frees generation capacity for export rather than creating headroom to absorb renewable surplus, the same shift raises forced export from reservoir draining, and such a system shows signs of inflexibility unless the district heating is designed with flexibility as an aim \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. \autoref{3-subsec:3.9 validity} attaches that qualifier to the external-validity claim directly. Because the freed-hydropower channel carries no number here by scope, what transfers to a hydropower-dominated comparator is a mechanism and not a value, and what transfers to a thermal comparator is the institutional finding without the hydropower pathway underneath it.

\subsection{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{5-subsec:5.5 future work}

\autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} names what the design cannot do however well it is executed, and states that this subsection carries the study-level limitations that surface from running the analysis. That division is kept here. What follows is what those limits cost the conclusions, ranked by how much each costs; what running the model revealed that the design did not anticipate; the scope choices of \autoref{1-subsec:1.4 scope and limitations} together with the work they leave; and the work that follows from both.

The largest cost falls on the largest number. Price taking in the reserve market fails at applied access, where the model's own activated upward energy reaches 1.20 times the whole NO1 zone's activated volume for the year, and the market-depth cap alone moves the access bracket from 138.42 to 256.42 MNOK \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.3 reserve market participation}. The enabler's magnitude is therefore a bound rather than an estimate, and the ranking in \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications} rests on the direction of the effect and on the enabled growth in displaced peaking heat, neither of which depends on the clearing price surviving the model's own volume.

Second, foresight splits into two results that must not be collapsed, because one indicts the model and the other defends it. Foresight of reserve prices and activation is worth about a third of the baseline flexibility value, concentrated entirely in the reserve leg, and it is a lower bound rather than an estimate, so what the operator captures is overstated by at least that much. Foresight over the time horizon is worth nothing, which is what makes the annual formulation defensible rather than merely conventional \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}. The ordering of the two registers survives the first result, because the overstatement falls on the operator's own captured value and not on the externality beside it.

Third, the priced total is a floor, and the cost of that is one specific sentence. Three channels of six carry no number and all three are positive by construction, so the 89 per cent capture is a share of two channels rather than of the whole, and the claim that the operator captures more than society gains is not available at any point in this thesis \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.1 division}.

Fourth, the scarce-hour definition decides a sign and not only a size. Freed grid capacity is positive in six of ninety mask cells, so every conclusion about the externality carries the mask that produced it \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.2 valuation sensitivities}. The tariff-switch relief is the more robust of the two grid quantities, positive in fifteen of fifteen demand-mask cells, and the conclusions that rest on it are correspondingly firmer than those that rest on the flexibility relief \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.2 tariff barrier}.

Fifth, the evidence base is one case, one operator and one interview. Magnitudes are local to Oslo, the construct claim is at its weakest where an operator statement is the only support, and the regulator-side and system-operator perspectives that were sought were not obtained \autoref{3-subsec:3.9 validity}. Every operator-sourced claim in this thesis is written as a statement about what this operator does or reports.

Sixth, and least costly, cost levels outside 2024 are counterfactual under held 2024 policy. That limitation is confined rather than general, because a held parameter sits on both sides of every within-year difference and cancels exactly \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}. No conclusion in this thesis rests on a level, which is a claim the chapter can make because \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.1 lp sensitivities} quantifies what a level is worth: the carbon levy moves the annual cost by 623 MNOK across its swept range and the flexibility value by 5.85.

Running the analysis then revealed six things the design did not anticipate, and each is stated with what it costs rather than repeated as a result. The model under-dispatches the electric boilers against the public register in every delivery year and fails the share test in 2022 and 2023, and the causes sit outside the design rather than inside its parameters, so its account of a high-price year is not a description of what the plant did \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation}. 2023's outlier status turned out to be one mechanism, the coincidence of cheap hours with heat load, and that single mechanism is why the priced total reverses in that year. The two references disagree on carbon in sign, which means the sign of a monetised flexibility benefit can be set by a counterfactual choice. The storage screening returned a negative result, locating the investment barrier upstream of dispatch rather than in the operating economics. The technical envelope's energy side stayed unquantified because the network water volume and temperature band were not obtained, so the two perimeters are compared in kroner per year and never per unit of physical resource. And three validation tests fail and are diagnosed rather than tuned: the 2023 share, simultaneous charge and discharge in five of eighteen runs under a binding heat-disposal cap, and a dual on the access ceiling that counts one of two capped directions \autoref{tab:4.1 validation}. The behavioural test breaks only in reserve-backing hours, which is the mechanism of \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.2 tariff barrier} met from the other side.

Four scope choices set in \autoref{1-subsec:1.4 scope and limitations} shaped what could be answered, and each is defended there and in \autoref{3-subsec:3.1 research design} rather than being an oversight. Small local systems are out of scope, which is why the multi-system question below is a research area rather than an extension. End-user price elasticity and demand-side response are out of scope, and the consequences are specific: the price-regulation channel can never carry a number, the consumer support schemes cannot be evaluated, and pricing lower system prices in scarce hours needs a willingness-to-pay curve the price-taking design excludes. Detailed technical asset design is out of scope, which leaves the network's own thermal mass unsized. And an independent cost-benefit analysis is out of scope, the warrant being that the field has not converged on a monetisation method, which is itself what the first item below proposes to address.

Twelve items of future work follow, ordered from the one the thesis's own limitations make most pressing. The first four would firm up quantities this thesis already reports. A Norwegian district heating flexibility valuation framework that codifies what the corpus has not converged on is the direction \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} names, and it is the one that would remove the method risk every triangulated estimate here carries. A reserve supply curve for NO1 would turn the applied-access bound into an estimate, and it is the single addition that would most strengthen the largest number in the thesis. Establishing what settling the 2023 reversal would take is next: a counterfactual that runs the boilers as the operator actually ran them, and a reserve product without a backed-load requirement, which together separate the mechanism from the modelling artefact \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap}. A restricted stochastic reformulation that blinds the operator to the reserve series alone would bound the foresight overstatement without the scope cost of a fully stochastic model.

Three further items would close measurement gaps this study declared rather than discovered. Pricing the lower system prices channel needs a willingness-to-pay curve at dispatch scale, and it is the one unpriced channel an instrument could reach. Sizing the network's thermal mass needs the water volume and the admissible temperature band the operator did not supply, and it would convert the energy envelope from a lower bound into a measurement. Establishing why the operator runs its boilers more than a cost-minimiser does in high-price years would test the four candidates \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} names, which are heat-quality and network-temperature requirements, contracted fuel or waste volumes, operating practice, and a hedged rather than spot-indexed power position.

The last five reach beyond this case. A multi-system paired valuation across Trondheim, Bergen and smaller municipal systems is a distinct research area, because the storage and capacity asymmetries break the single-case parameterisation \autoref{2-subsec:2.6.4 future work}. End-user welfare and distributional effects remain almost entirely unmonetised in the field and are the scope choice with the largest literature behind it. Business-model and instrument design, meaning the concrete shape of a capacity-payment product, an aggregation contract or a bilateral distribution-level flexibility agreement, is named in this thesis and designed nowhere in it. The net effect of a rising waste-incineration cost on the divergence stays an open analytical question, since it shifts dispatch toward power-to-heat while compressing the margin that funds the investment, and \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers} has already established that its direction is ambiguous. And the regulator-side and system-operator perspectives sought during this work were not obtained, which leaves the institutional account resting on the public record and one operator.

What would change the conclusions is a shorter list than what would refine them, and separating the two is the last thing this chapter does. A reserve supply curve or a market-depth model would change the size of the enabler and leave its sign and its rank where they are. Pricing lower system prices in scarce hours could make the over-capture sentence available, and would change nothing else. A counterfactual that ran the boilers as the operator did would change the sign of the 2023 result and leave 2024 and 2025 untouched. None of the twelve items would change the ordering of the three registers, the direction of the tariff result or the location of the storage barrier. The distinction the whole chapter has kept is therefore the one it closes on. What this thesis established, it established by measurement against a stated counterfactual, with a band and a mask attached. What it inferred, it marked as inference, and the two are never joined without the join being visible. \autoref{6-sec:6 conclusion} restates the measured half and the ranking that follows from it.
