\subsection{Background and Motivation}
\label{1-subsec:1.1 background}
The marginal value of flexibility in the Norwegian power system is increasing, driven by rising electricity prices, a transmission and distribution grid in need of expansion and the continued expansion of variable renewable generation \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023}. In the Oslo area, network operators have received applications for roughly $1000 MW$ of new load connections, of which only about $200 MW$ have reserved capacity \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. 

\avklar{Confirm 1000 MW / 200 MW reserved oslo connection figure: THEMA rapport 2.1.2. Have seen figures of 4 GW national queue - dont know where.}

The value of flexibility has been historically low in Norway because of the natural balancing capacity of reservoir hydropower, which could absorb and release energy at a low cost, leaving low demand for thermal flexibility \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. That structural advantage is eroding as electricity demand outpaces available grid capacity. The Norwegian flexibility logic is also distinct from the continental one: rather than absorbing surplus wind, as in Denmark and Germany, additional district heating in Norway frees hydropower in Norway for export and grid balancing. In a national reference-system simulation, hydropower generation capacity is the binding constraint on exporting at the interconnection limit, and shifting roughly a quarter of individual electric heating to district heating removes that constraint \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. This is a technical potential at national scale rather than an Oslo-specific or market outcome.

District heating operators in Norway control a substantial latent flexibility resource through electric boilers, large-scale heat pumps, and accumulator thermal storage, but the current regime locks it out on two fronts. The capacity component of the grid tariff penalises the increased peak electricity draw that flexible electric-boiler dispatch requires \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}, \cite{skytte_design_2017}. In the balancing markets, qualification requirements and the baseline automation demand keep most plants out of the reserve markets \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}.

\subsection{Problem statement}
\label{1-subsec:1.2 problem statement}
Current market and regulatory conditions create a structural split-incentive problem in the provision of thermal flexibility from Norwegian district heating \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. DH operators who invest in, and operate, flexible assets like electric boilers, thermal storage, and heat pumps, bear the full financial cost of supplying that flexibility: Increased capital expenditure, grid capacity charges and operational complexity. The potential benefits of activating that flexibility however, accrue elsewhere: to distribution system operators through avoided grid reinforcement, to transmission system operators through freed balancing capacity, and to consumers through potential lower system prices during peak periods. No functional transfer mechanism currently exists to redirect a share of those societal gains back to the DH operators, who carry the financial burden \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}.

The consequence is a structurally sub-optimal equilibrium. Operators face weak or negative incentives to offer the flexibility that the power system increasingly needs. Closing this divide requires understanding both sides of the ledger: the commercial costs and revenues that determine operator behavior, and the socio-economic value that justifies public interest in changing those conditions. This thesis examines both, and analyses what regulatory and market-design changes would be required to align them.

\subsection{Objectives and Research Questions}
\label{1-subsec:1.3 research questions}

\paragraph*{Main Research Question}
To what extent do current market- and regulatory conditions produce a divergence between the commercial and socio-economic value of thermal flexibility in Norwegian district heating, and what mechanisms could reduce that divergence?

\textbf{Sub-Questions:}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item What are the commercial costs and revenues associated with operating thermal flexibility assets in the Oslo district heating system under current market and tariff conditions?
    \item What is the socio-economic value of the same flexibility, and how robust is the triangulated estimate?
    \item What specific regulatory, market-design, and grid-tariff features contribute the most to the divergence between the two perspectives, and which are most amenable to change?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Scope and Limitations}
\label{1-subsec:1.4 scope and limitations}
The thesis takes Oslo's district heating system, operated by Hafslund Celsio, as the primary single case. Trondheim and Bergen are retained as comparative system context only. The flexibility assets in scope are electric boilers, heat pumps and accumulator thermal storage. Small, local heating systems, end-user price elasticity, demand-side response, and detailed technical asset design are out of scope, as is an independent socio-economic cost-benefit analysis. The socio-economic valuation is instead drawn from existing literature and triangulated across those sources rather than constructed anew \cite{ahmed_district_2025}. 

The defence of these scope choices, and the limitations they impose, is deferred to the methodology (\autoref{3-subsec:3.7}) and the discussion (\autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work}).

\subsection{Contribution}
\label{1-subsec:1.5 contribution}
The thesis offers a paired commercial and socio-economic valuation of thermal flexibility for a single Norwegian district heating system, quantifying the gap between the two perspectives rather than presenting them as parallel analyses. The operator-side estimate is produced by a deterministic Python and PuLP dispatch model run against current market and tariff conditions. The system-side estimate triangulates published socio-economic figures across multiple sources. The two closest precedents apply a comparable paired structure elsewhere: \cite{ostergaard_business_2019} to a Danish biomass-based island system, and \cite{moser_socioeconomic_2018} to an Austrian seasonal-storage case. Both are methodologically reusable but not substantively comparable to the hydropower-coupled Norwegian context. To the author's knowledge, a paired commercial and socio-economic valuation has not previously been applied to a Norwegian district heating system at the operator level with explicit quantification of the gap.
