\section{Methodology}
\label{3-sec:3}

This chapter sets out the method that produces the paired business-economic and socio-economic valuation at the centre of the thesis, and states what that method can and cannot establish. The design is a single embedded case study of the Oslo district heating system \autoref{3-subsec:3.1 research design}, and the evidence it rests on is stated before any analytical machinery is introduced \autoref{3-subsec:3.2 evidence base}. A technical flexibility envelope then sizes the physical resource in megawatts and megawatt-hours before any economics enter \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope}, and supplies the physical scale against which both valuation perimeters are read. The operator model is a deterministic hourly linear program of the Oslo heat system \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model}. The counterfactual it is measured against, and the scenario grid crossed with it, are specified together \autoref{3-subsec:3.5 counterfactual and stack}, because a counterfactual and a scenario are different objects and every number in the analysis is misread if the two are confused. The socio-economic side triangulates published unit values onto physical quantities the model emits \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}, and the gap arithmetic divides the socio-economic total between the operator and the parties that receive the remainder, then attributes that division to barrier mechanisms \autoref{3-subsec:3.7 gap and attribution}. Three closing subsections state how the model is tested and what would falsify it \autoref{3-subsec:3.8 model validation}, how the design handles the four standard case-study validity threats \autoref{3-subsec:3.9 validity}, and what the architecture cannot do by construction \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}.

A systematic review of 123 district heating benefit-assessment studies found that flexibility, as a benefit category, has been identified eleven times, quantified four times, and monetised zero times across the corpus \cite{ahmed_district_2025}. The absence of a harmonised method is therefore not a gap this thesis closes by importing an off-the-shelf framework. It is the warrant for building a small bespoke framework and defending it layer by layer, which is what the ordering of this chapter is designed to do.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Research Design}
\label{3-subsec:3.1 research design}

The research design is a single-case study with embedded sub-units, chosen because the research question requires both depth on one regulatory and operator system and analytical generalisation to a class of similar systems.

The case is Oslo's district heating system as a system, not Hafslund Celsio as an organisation. The unit of analysis includes the regulatory context (RME, Statnett, NVE concessions, section 5-5 of the Energy Act), municipal planning, the transmission and distribution grids that bound the operator's flexibility envelope, and the load-side relationships that shape demand. Hafslund Celsio is the operator at the centre of the case, but the case is the system the operator dispatches into.

The design carries four embedded sub-units. Heat production sources cover installed capacity, fuel mix and operational flexibility across the waste-to-energy base load, the heat-pump and electric-boiler peaking stack, and the Haraldrud accumulator. Distribution infrastructure covers network characteristics and temperature regimes. Demand-side relationships cover customer segments and load patterns. Governance arrangements cover ownership, pricing and planning. Each sub-unit is examined for what it contributes to the mechanism behind the value gap, not as an analytical end in itself.

A multi-city design covering Oslo, Trondheim and Bergen was considered and rejected. The reduction to a single primary case rests on three grounds. First, the depth-versus-breadth trade-off is decisive at the modelling resolution required: an hourly dispatch model calibrated to plant-level capacity, published tariff schedules and the operator's own metered production cannot be replicated across three operators within the thesis's time envelope without sacrificing the calibration that gives the model its analytical value. Second, the gap is partly a function of national regulatory context, which is uniform across Norwegian cases, and partly a function of operator-specific cost structure, which is local. The national-regime layer is captured equally well by any single primary case. Third, Oslo is the best-evidenced Norwegian case in the existing literature. A tariff-binding dispatch model has been calibrated on 2012 Hafslund Varme hourly heat-load data and Hafslund Nett tariff rates \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}, which makes Oslo the closest geographic precedent in the corpus for the type of analysis performed here, subject to the data-vintage caveat that it is the institutional structure rather than the data that has carried forward.

The single-case scope is further defensible because Oslo is where the grid constraint bites hardest. At NO1 maximum load, local production covers approximately $10\%$ of demand, and Statnett and Elvia hold around 1,000 MW of new-load applications with only about 200 MW reservable \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. The same source values the grid capacity freed by converting Oslo electric heating to waterborne heat and district heating, a potential Celsio has mapped at 1.5 to 1.9 TWh, at NOK 250 to 430 million per year in avoided grid investment and faster connection of queued load \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. Flexibility is additional to that figure rather than its source: the same report notes that greater use of heat storage and optimised electric-boiler operation frees further capacity on top of the conversion effect, so the socio-economic side of this analysis is anchored on that increment and never on the conversion value itself. Oslo's district heating system serves roughly $20\%$ of the city's heating demand, and district heating could cover about $25\%$ of the city's capacity demand \cite{energikommisjonen_mer_2023}, which makes the case both representative of Norwegian district-heating flexibility potential and bounded enough for tractable analysis.

Trondheim and Bergen remain in the thesis as comparative context, not as primary cases. Each is referenced where Nordic and Norwegian intra-country variation sharpens an argument about the Oslo case, not as a stand-alone unit of analysis. The system characteristics that distinguish them from Oslo are set out in \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics}.

\subsection{What the Analysis Establishes, and on What Evidence}
\label{3-subsec:3.2 evidence base}
\label{3-subsec:3.2 interviews}

Claims in this thesis rest on three kinds of evidence, and they do not carry equal weight. Setting out the division before the machinery is described lets each later number be read against the evidence that produced it, and it is the reason the analysis is built to report differences rather than levels.

The first tier is measured. The technical flexibility envelope, the market environment the operator faces, and the difference between the two grid-tariff regimes available to it are read directly from public and operator records and require no model at all. Licensed and installed capacity comes from the operator's own district heating concession \cite{nve_konsesjonssak_2026}. Hourly prices and reserve volumes come from the Nord Pool and ENTSO-E market records \cite{nord_pool_nord_nodate}, \cite{entsoe_entsoe_nodate}. Tariff rates come from the network operator's published schedules \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}, and tax and levy rates from the annual Storting resolutions on excise duties \cite{stortinget_stortingsvedtak_nodate}. Nothing in this tier depends on an optimisation being right.

The second tier is modelled as a difference. Every capability step in the counterfactual ladder, and every comparison between runs within a delivery year, is the difference between two solutions of the same optimisation problem, run on the same input data and differing in exactly one respect. The year axis is the one deliberate exception, since two years differ in a whole year of weather, prices and activations at once; it is precisely because they differ in so much that the policy parameters are held constant across them, so that at least the statute is not moving as well. Differences of this kind are robust to the model's declared constants, because a constant entering both solutions cancels in the subtraction. Absolute cost levels are not robust in the same way, and the analysis states this rather than implying otherwise. The single largest term in the operator's cost base is the waste incinerator's own marginal cost, and the one-at-a-time sensitivity programme of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} shows that moving it shifts the cost level by roughly two orders of magnitude more than it shifts the flexibility value. That asymmetry is the reason every headline claim in this thesis is a difference between two runs rather than the level of either.

The third tier is triangulated with bands. The socio-economic unit values are not computed here. They are taken from published Norwegian sources and applied to physical quantities the model produces, with the spread across the source methods reported as a band rather than collapsed to a point \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}. A quantity in this tier is never reported as a single number.

The evidence base has four components. Public market and statistical data supply the price, reserve, weather and tax series. The operator's district heating concession supplies the plant inventory site by site \cite{nve_konsesjonssak_2026}. A direct operator channel to Hafslund Celsio, opened in June 2026 through the production-optimisation function under a signed data agreement, supplies the metered hourly heat production series for 2021 to 2025 together with written answers to a list of parameter questions. And one qualitative source supplies mechanism detail that none of the others carries.

That qualitative source is a single semi-structured expert interview, held in person on 25 June 2026 with Hafslund Celsio's head of production optimisation, the function that carries hourly production planning and physical power trading for the Oslo system and therefore the vantage point from which dispatch practice is visible. The interview ran for approximately ninety minutes, was conducted in Norwegian, and was audio-recorded with written consent under the data-handling arrangements approved for the project. It is the only interview realised: requests to Reguleringsmyndigheten for energi and to Energidepartementet went unanswered, and the follow-up with Statnett was not pursued, so no regulator-side or system-operator perspective was obtained at first hand. The processing of personal data is covered by the project's personvernmelding to Sikt, reference 422039. The legal basis is the participant's informed written consent, which covers professional capacity only; recordings are deleted once transcribed; and any quotation is cleared with the participant before use and anonymised unless naming is expressly consented to.

Interview material carries no findings of its own. It is not treated as a separate evidence stream, no coding procedure is applied to it, and no section of this thesis reports it as a result. It supplies background and mechanism detail, meaning how dispatch decisions are actually taken and which constraints the operator experiences as binding, and it is cited inline at the specific claims it bears on in the same way as any other source. A single interview is not evidence that a claim holds generally, and none is asked to carry that weight. Where it is the only support for a statement, the statement is about what this operator does or reports, not about what is generally the case. \autoref{3-subsec:3.9 validity} sets out what that means for the construct-validity claim.

Statements from the operator are cited in the text by organisation and date, distinguishing the 25 June 2026 interview from later written correspondence, and are not entered in the bibliography. They are not retrievable documents, so a reference-list entry would signal an availability that does not exist; the record is held by the author under the consent arrangement above. Personal communications are handled this way under the standard citation conventions.

The four analytical stages those sources feed, and what each stage hands to the next, are drawn in \autoref{fig:3.2 methodology spine}. Two features of that structure are decisions rather than conveniences and are defended where they arise: the technical envelope comes first, so that the physical resource is sized before any price is applied to it, and the socio-economic side receives quantities from the operator model rather than producing its own, so that the two perimeters are always measured on the same dispatch.

\subsection{The Technical Flexibility Envelope}
\label{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope}

The envelope answers a question that precedes all economics: how much flexibility physically exists in the Oslo system, in the two dimensions the power system remunerates separately. Energy is the heat that can be stored and time-shifted, measured in megawatt-hours. Power is the electrical load that can be added or shed at short notice, measured in megawatts. Establishing both before any price enters is what lets the later valuation be read as a share of a known physical resource rather than as an unbounded figure, and it responds directly to the corpus-wide finding that flexibility has been monetised in none of the 123 reviewed district heating studies \cite{ahmed_district_2025}.

The envelope is a static stock-and-headroom estimate, not a dispatch simulation. It quantifies what exists; the linear program in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} determines what current price signals make worth dispatching. Keeping the two separate is what makes assumption A9 in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions} consistent, since that assumption excludes network thermal dynamics from the optimisation while the envelope reports a stock estimate that requires no propagation modelling. The estimate is first-order by construction, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} states what that costs.

The power side resolves into three tiers, and the asymmetry between them is the single most consequential fact about the Oslo case. The first tier is installed electric-boiler capacity: 292.6 MW across the sites whose boilers reach the Oslo district heating network, tallied site by site from the operator's own concession \cite{nve_konsesjonssak_2026}. The second is concurrently dispatchable capacity, approximately 250 MW, which is what the fleet can draw in any single hour (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026). The third is reserve-eligible capacity, approximately 29 MW connected and prequalified today (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026), against a published ceiling of 33 MW for the Oslo and Akershus capacity-area group \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. Roughly a tenth of the installed fleet can be sold into the reserve market, and the distance between the third tier and the volume the operator has applied for is the quantity the access axis of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} brackets.

The constraint that sets the second tier is thermal rather than electrical, and this correction matters because it relocates the barrier. The operator states that the electricity grid does not bind the fleet at all, and that the binding limits are bottlenecks inside the district heating network arising from local heat-transfer limitations (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026). The 250 MW is described as a rule of thumb that varies with network conditions, and it is used here as a rounded figure rather than as a precise limit. It sits between the at least 220 MW of simultaneous draw observed in 2022 \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024} and the 292.6 MW licensed and network-connected, so it is bracketed on both sides by independent evidence.

Because that constraint is thermal, relieving it means debottlenecking the heat network rather than reinforcing the electricity grid, and the two must not be read as the same measure. The connection queue of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} is capacity that has not been built; this is capacity that is already standing and cannot all be used at once. The distinction has a methodological consequence, which is the one change this subsection makes to how the envelope enters the analysis. The second tier is not a fixed input the chapter merely declares. It is swept, by re-solving the whole problem with the concurrent bound moved from the operator's stated 250 MW to the full 292.6 MW the concession licenses and the network already connects, under both tariff structures, as one of the four named sensitivities of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack}. That sweep is what gives the technical barrier an arithmetic row in the attribution of \autoref{3-subsec:3.7 gap and attribution}, where it previously had none. \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} opens by claiming that technical barriers bound what the other three types can be blamed for, and until the bound was priced that claim rested on the literature rather than on this case. \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.1 envelope in use} reports what it is worth.

The energy side is dominated by one asset. The Haraldrud accumulator holds approximately 550 MWh of ideal storage capacity and charges or discharges at up to about 100 MW (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). A hot-water accumulator cannot cycle its full geometric volume, because the top and bottom of the tank carry the thermocline and the return-temperature layer, so the energy that can actually be moved is a fraction of the nameplate; a declared usable fraction of $0.90$ gives 495 MWh, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance} states what that fraction rests on. At full discharge the store therefore runs for just under five hours. Read against the system rather than against the asset, 495 MWh covers roughly forty minutes of delivery at the 763 MW peak heat load measured in 2024. The store is a short-duration, high-power resource, which is where a tank sits in the storage typology of \autoref{2-subsec:2.2.3 thermal storage}.

One component of the energy side is named and not quantified. The water volume inside the supply and return pipes is itself a thermal store, charged and discharged by varying supply temperature and mass flow \cite{mitridati_power_2018}. Sizing it requires the network water volume and the admissible operating temperature band, and the operator did not supply either. No engineering estimate is substituted, because the result would scale near-linearly with two invented inputs and would then propagate into every quantity expressed against the envelope. The network store is therefore reported as a named component of the resource with no number attached, consistent with the low-power, short-duration position \autoref{2-subsec:2.2.3 thermal storage} assigns it, and the envelope's quantified energy figure is the accumulator alone. It is a lower bound on the system's storage stock.

Regulation-ability parameters for the electrical headroom, meaning ramp share per unit time, minimum load and start time, are taken from the Danish Energy Agency technology catalogue for electricity and district heating generation \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}. They confirm that the electric boilers can meet manual reserve activation requirements unaided, and that the heat pumps are constrained by cycling limits rather than by market eligibility; the order of magnitude is corroborated by the Sdr. Felding pilot reported in IEA HPT Annex 57, where a 1.3 MW heat pump records seven minutes to start and four minutes to turn down \cite{v_pedersen_annex_2024}. Every one of these figures resolves below the hourly resolution at which the model settles, so ramp rates are carried as a parameterised bound that does not bind, and reserve capability is imposed structurally instead: committed reserve is backed exclusively by the electric boilers \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.1 model formulation}.

\autoref{tab:3.3 technical envelope} collects the envelope. The heat-side capacities are stated alongside the electrical ones because the two together bound what the model can dispatch, and because the waste incinerator's minimum-load floor is what makes the counterfactual of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder} physically well posed. That floor is a throughput constraint before it is a heat constraint: the Klemetsrud plant is licensed to treat roughly 400{,}000 tonnes of residual waste a year \cite{dsbno_bygging_2026}, and the waste arrives whether or not the heat is wanted.

\subsection{The Operator Model}
\label{3-subsec:3.4 operator model}

The operator model produces the business-economic side of the comparison. It is a tool for the gap analysis rather than the central output of the thesis, and it is built to be small enough that a reader can predict what it can and cannot say. \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.1 model formulation} specifies the optimisation problem, \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance} states where every input comes from and which inputs are declared rather than sourced, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions} states the consequential assumptions with the precedent or the measurement that defends each one. The complete mathematical specification is given in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.1 model formulation}.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Model Formulation}
\label{3-subsec:3.4.1 model formulation}

The model is a deterministic linear program implemented in Python with the PuLP modelling library and the open-source COIN-OR CBC solver. It solves one delivery year at hourly resolution, over the 8,760 hours of an ordinary year and the 8,784 of the 2024 leap year, and it is continuous throughout: there are no binary variables, no unit commitment and no start-up costs. The choice of a linear program rather than mixed-integer linear programming is a deliberate step down from the closest Norwegian methodological precedent, a mixed-integer unit-commitment formulation calibrated on Hafslund Varme 2012 data \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}. Celsio's flexible assets have no binding start-up or minimum-load constraints at the scale relevant to this question, so dropping the unit-commitment binaries removes machinery the analysis does not need while retaining the tariff-binding mechanism that produces the precedent's headline result, a 2 to 17 per cent variation in electric-boiler share across tariff structures \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}. The architecture otherwise descends from a Nordic deterministic-dispatch lineage \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}, \cite{johannsen_incentivising_2021}, \cite{tromborg_flexible_2017}.

The operator is modelled as a cost minimiser under a fixed delivery obligation rather than as a revenue maximiser. Heat demand enters as an exogenous hourly series that the model must meet in every hour, so heat revenue is invariant to dispatch and drops out of the objective. The consequence is that every number the model reports is a cost difference, and no result depends on an assumption about heat pricing.

The ledger the model produces is therefore narrower than the operator's own accounts, and the difference is worth stating so that no figure from it is read as a company result. It contains the variable cost of meeting heat demand and the revenue from selling reserve capacity, and nothing else. Heat sales, capital costs and depreciation, staff and overheads, network operation and maintenance, and the gate fees the operator receives for accepting waste are all outside it. A total from this ledger is a dispatch cost, not an operating cost, and it is a fraction of what the operator actually spends to run the system.

Decision variables are hourly and continuous, and heat demand is inelastic, consistent with the scope decision in \autoref{1-subsec:1.4 scope and limitations} that excludes demand-side response. The notation is collected in \autoref{tab:3.4.1 nomenclature} and the formulation below uses it throughout. Heat quantities are in MW$_{th}$ and electrical quantities in MW$_{el}$, related by the conversion efficiencies in the same table.

The third, $\hat{q}^{\text{eb}}_t$, is the boiler heat actually delivered, which differs from the boiler heat scheduled by whatever an activation takes back out of it:
\begin{align}
    s_t &= \frac{q^{\text{eb}}_t}{\eta^{\text{eb}}} + \frac{q^{\text{hp}}_t}{\text{COP}} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 sched draw} \\
    e_t &= s_t - \alpha^{\uparrow}_t K^{\uparrow}_t + \alpha^{\downarrow}_t K^{\downarrow}_t && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 metered draw} \\
    \hat{q}^{\text{eb}}_t &= q^{\text{eb}}_t - \eta^{\text{eb}} \alpha^{\uparrow}_t K^{\uparrow}_t + \eta^{\text{eb}} \alpha^{\downarrow}_t K^{\downarrow}_t && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 delivered eb}
\end{align}

The objective minimises the net cost of meeting the delivery obligation. The first line is the cost of dispatch, the second is the reserve market entered with a negative sign because it is revenue:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\min \quad \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} & \Big[ p^{\text{spot}}_t s_t + \tau^{\text{elavg}} e_t + c^{\text{wte}} q^{\text{wte}}_t + c^{\text{hp}} q^{\text{hp}}_t + c^{\text{eb}} \hat{q}^{\text{eb}}_t + \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} c^{f} q^{\text{peak},f}_t + c^{\text{cyc}} (\text{ch}_t + \text{dis}_t) \Big] + \Gamma \\[3pt]
& - \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} \Big[ \pi^{\text{cap},\uparrow}_t K^{\uparrow}_t + \pi^{\text{act},\uparrow}_t \alpha^{\uparrow}_t K^{\uparrow}_t + \pi^{\text{cap},\downarrow}_t K^{\downarrow}_t - \pi^{\text{act},\downarrow}_t \alpha^{\downarrow}_t K^{\downarrow}_t \Big]
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:3.4.1 objective}
\end{equation}

The cost line reads as follows. Electricity is bought at the price the day-ahead auction cleared, on the scheduled position. The electricity tax is billed on the metered draw. The next four terms are the variable costs of the four heat sources, each a unit cost multiplied by the heat that source delivers: the waste incinerator, the heat pumps, the electric boilers, and the peak boilers, the last of which is a merit-ordered stack of three fuels indexed by $f$. The final term charges accumulator throughput at a small rate whose purpose is explained in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance}. The grid-tariff term $\Gamma$ is the only term whose form differs between the two tariff structures, and it is given with the tariff rows in \autoref{eq:3.4.1 tariff V} and \autoref{eq:3.4.1 tariff S} below.

The revenue line is the reserve market, in two directions. Capacity is paid at the hourly clearing price whether or not it is called. Activation is settled on the share of committed capacity actually called in that hour, and the sign differs between the directions: an up-regulation activation means the boiler sheds load and the operator is paid for energy it does not consume, while a down-regulation activation means the boiler is told to draw more, so real energy arrives and is paid for at the down-regulation price. The down leg is therefore a purchase below spot rather than a sale, and the compensation is the discount, which is why its activation term enters \autoref{eq:3.4.1 objective} with the opposite sign to the other three. Representing it matters because it is the direction a thermal store is physically built for: an accumulator with charge headroom can absorb an unplanned instruction to consume, whereas an up-regulation call requires the boiler to have been running in the first place and leaves a heat deficit that something else must fill. The Norwegian zone also procures more down-regulation volume than up, so a model without the down leg cannot see the half of the reserve market the asset is best suited to.

Constraints close the formulation in five blocks.

The \textbf{heat balance} equates supply to the exogenous demand in every hour, adjusted for the heat given up or taken on by an activated reserve call:
\begin{equation}
    q^{\text{wte}}_t + q^{\text{hp}}_t + q^{\text{eb}}_t - \eta^{\text{eb}} \alpha^{\uparrow}_t K^{\uparrow}_t + \eta^{\text{eb}} \alpha^{\downarrow}_t K^{\downarrow}_t + q^{\text{peak}}_t + \text{dis}_t - \text{ch}_t - q^{\text{dump}}_t = D_t \qquad \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 heat balance}
\end{equation}
That adjustment is what makes a reserve commitment genuinely cost something: an activated up-regulation removes boiler heat that some other source has to replace at its own marginal cost.

The \textbf{storage recursion} of \autoref{eq:3.4.1 soc} carries the state of charge forward hour by hour with a round-trip efficiency and a standby loss, \autoref{eq:3.4.1 soc bounds} bounds it by usable capacity and bounds charge and discharge by the tank's power rating, and \autoref{eq:3.4.1 soc terminal} requires the terminal state to equal the initial state so that end-of-horizon depletion cannot be booked as a gain:
\begin{align}
    \text{soc}_t &= (1 - \lambda)\,\text{soc}_{t-1} + \eta^{c}\,\text{ch}_t - \frac{\text{dis}_t}{\eta^{d}} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 soc} \\
    \text{soc}_0 &= \text{soc}_T = \sigma \bar{E}^{\text{acc}} \label{eq:3.4.1 soc terminal} \\
    0 \leq \text{soc}_t &\leq \bar{E}^{\text{acc}}, \qquad 0 \leq \text{ch}_t,\ \text{dis}_t \leq \bar{P}^{\text{acc}} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 soc bounds}
\end{align}

\textbf{Capacity bounds} apply per asset and at the fleet level, with \autoref{eq:3.4.1 asset caps} holding the heat pump, the electric boiler and the dump cooler to their installed capacities:
\begin{align}
    \phi A_t \leq &\ q^{\text{wte}}_t \leq A_t && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 wte floor} \\
    0 \leq &\ q^{\text{hp}}_t \leq \bar{Q}^{\text{hp}}, \qquad 0 \leq q^{\text{eb}}_t \leq \eta^{\text{eb}} \bar{Q}^{\text{eb}}, \qquad 0 \leq q^{\text{dump}}_t \leq \bar{Q}^{\text{dump}} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 asset caps} \\
    &\ q^{\text{peak}}_t = \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} q^{\text{peak},f}_t, \qquad 0 \leq q^{\text{peak},f}_t \leq \bar{Q}^{\text{peak},f} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T},\ f \in \mathcal{F} \label{eq:3.4.1 peak stack} \\
    &\ s_t \leq \bar{E}^{\text{conc}} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 concurrent}
\end{align}
Each peak-fuel tranche is capped at its own installed capacity in \autoref{eq:3.4.1 peak stack}, so the merit order among the three fuels is decided by their marginal costs rather than imposed. The waste incinerator carries a minimum-load floor as well as a ceiling in \autoref{eq:3.4.1 wte floor}, because reducing it below roughly half of availability degrades steam production and the turbines (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026). And \autoref{eq:3.4.1 concurrent} caps the scheduled electrical draw at the concurrently dispatchable 250 MW of \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope}, which is a scalar standing in for a network the model does not represent. The bound applies to the scheduled position rather than the metered one, since the fleet has to be running at that level for reserve to be sheddable from it; applying it to the metered draw would let the optimisation schedule above the bound and hide the excess behind an activation.

\textbf{Reserve participation} is bounded in three ways, and each bound encodes a different real constraint. Eligibility and market depth cap what may be committed, in \autoref{eq:3.4.1 eligibility} and \autoref{eq:3.4.1 depth} respectively:
\begin{align}
    K^{\uparrow}_t \leq K^{\text{elig}}, \qquad K^{\downarrow}_t &\leq K^{\text{elig}} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 eligibility} \\
    K^{\uparrow}_t \leq \bar{K}^{\uparrow}_t, \qquad K^{\downarrow}_t &\leq \bar{K}^{\downarrow}_t && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 depth}
\end{align}
Eligibility caps committed capacity at the access level the run is set to, with a separate row for each direction, because the capacity-area regime allocates 3 MW up and 3 MW down per area on total prequalified volume across all reserve products \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. Market depth caps committed capacity in each hour at the volume the NO1 zone actually contracted in that hour, which is a market-size fact rather than a model of bid acceptance. That bound solves one problem and leaves another: it prevents the operator from selling more capacity than the market bought, and it does not cap activated energy, which is computed as the historical zonal activation share applied to the model's own commitment. At high committed volumes the modelled activated energy can therefore exceed what the zone actually consumed, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions} states the consequence for the price-taking declaration.

The third bound is deliverability, which requires that a full call could be honoured. Up-regulation must be backed by boiler output that could actually be shed, \autoref{eq:3.4.1 backed}, and the resulting heat shortfall must be coverable in both power and stored energy, \autoref{eq:3.4.1 up power cover} and \autoref{eq:3.4.1 up energy cover}:
\begin{align}
    K^{\uparrow}_t &\leq \frac{q^{\text{eb}}_t}{\eta^{\text{eb}}} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 backed} \\
    \eta^{\text{eb}} (1 - \alpha^{\uparrow}_t) K^{\uparrow}_t &\leq (\bar{P}^{\text{acc}} - \text{dis}_t) + (\bar{Q}^{\text{hp}} - q^{\text{hp}}_t) + (\bar{Q}^{\text{cov}} - q^{\text{peak}}_t) && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 up power cover} \\
    \eta^{\text{eb}} (1 - \alpha^{\uparrow}_t) K^{\uparrow}_t &\leq \eta^{d}\, \text{soc}_{t-1} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 up energy cover}
\end{align}
Down-regulation is the mirror. It requires electrical headroom under the concurrent bound, \autoref{eq:3.4.1 dn headroom}, and a heat sink with room for an hour of the call in both power and volume, \autoref{eq:3.4.1 dn sink power} and \autoref{eq:3.4.1 dn sink energy}:
\begin{align}
    K^{\downarrow}_t &\leq \bar{E}^{\text{conc}} - s_t && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 dn headroom} \\
    \eta^{\text{eb}} (1 - \alpha^{\downarrow}_t) K^{\downarrow}_t &\leq (\bar{P}^{\text{acc}} - \text{ch}_t) + (\bar{Q}^{\text{dump}} - q^{\text{dump}}_t) && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 dn sink power} \\
    \eta^{c} \eta^{\text{eb}} (1 - \alpha^{\downarrow}_t) K^{\downarrow}_t &\leq \bar{E}^{\text{acc}} - \text{soc}_{t-1} && \forall t \in \mathcal{T} \label{eq:3.4.1 dn sink energy}
\end{align}
The cover capacity $\bar{Q}^{\text{cov}}$ in \autoref{eq:3.4.1 up power cover} is the whole peak fleet, $\bar{Q}^{\text{cov}} = \sum_{f \in \mathcal{F}} \bar{Q}^{\text{peak},f}$, rather than a restricted subset of it; \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions} states what that simplification is worth. The factor $(1 - \alpha_t)$ in the four cover rows is what keeps the requirement honest. Once \autoref{eq:3.4.1 heat balance} nets the expected activation out of the delivered heat, part of the replacement is already being supplied inside the solved dispatch, so requiring the remaining headroom to cover the full call on top of that would charge the activated share twice. What must be coverable is the part not already delivered.

The pairing in the deliverability bound is the standard reserve-procurement idiom and it is worth stating plainly, because it looks inconsistent until it is named. Deliverability is required at the \emph{full} committed call, since the operator must be able to honour a call whether or not it comes. Energy and revenue are accounted at the \emph{expected} activated share, since that is what is delivered and paid for on average. Capacity feasibility at the worst case and energy accounting at the mean is how reserve procurement actually works.

\textbf{Tariff rows} differ by tariff structure, and supply the term $\Gamma$ left open in \autoref{eq:3.4.1 objective}. Under the interruptible arrangement the grid cost is a volumetric charge on the metered draw:
\begin{equation}
    \Gamma^{V} = \tau^{V} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} e_t \label{eq:3.4.1 tariff V}
\end{equation}
Under the standard schedule it is a volumetric energy term plus a capacity charge levied on the single highest hourly draw in each calendar month:
\begin{align}
    \Gamma^{S} &= \tau^{S,e} \sum_{t \in \mathcal{T}} e_t + \sum_{m \in \mathcal{M}} \tau^{S,\text{cap}}_m P_m \label{eq:3.4.1 tariff S} \\
    P_m &\geq e_t + (1 - \alpha^{\downarrow}_t) K^{\downarrow}_t && \forall m \in \mathcal{M},\ t \in \mathcal{T}_m \label{eq:3.4.1 monthly peak}
\end{align}
That single-peak basis is the settlement rule Elvia applies to Celsio's tariff class \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}; the three-peak-average basis applies to the small-customer step tariff and therefore not to this operator, and the regulator permits either \cite{rme_nettleie_2025}. The single-peak encoding makes the capacity charge maximally sensitive to one load-raising hour, which is exactly the mechanism the tariff comparison is built to price. \autoref{eq:3.4.1 monthly peak} exposes the full committed down-regulation to that monthly peak rather than its expected value, because a down call can arrive in any settlement period of the hour and one called quarter sets a peak that is billed for the whole month.

The model represents manual frequency restoration reserve only, and the reason is physical rather than regulatory. Automatic frequency restoration reserve and frequency containment reserve are continuous, automatically activated products whose response windows sit far below the hourly resolution at which this model settles, and they require SCADA-coupled continuous control the fleet does not operate under. A model that dispatches in whole hours cannot honestly claim deliverability against a sub-minute response requirement, so those products are excluded on the resolution of the instrument rather than declared out of scope. The manual reserve, procured in hourly blocks with a fifteen-minute activation window, is representable at this resolution and is also the only product the operator actually bids into (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026).

The information set is the model's most consequential simplification, and it splits into two parts that behave very differently. The first is foresight over time: the model solves one annual problem, so it knows the whole year's prices and demand when it decides any hour. This turns out to be worth almost nothing here, and the reason is a physical property of the asset rather than an artefact. A rolling-horizon reformulation that implements one day at a time and looks ahead only 48 hours reproduces the annual optimum exactly, because a 495 MWh store discharging at 100 MW is about five hours deep and its optimisation horizon is therefore sub-daily. There is nothing for annual foresight to exploit, and the annual formulation is defensible on that measurement rather than on convention. The second part is foresight over outcomes, and it is a real overstatement: the model knows both the reserve capacity price and whether each committed megawatt will be activated, which no operator does, since activation is a real-time system-operator decision. Blinding the model to both, so that it commits on expectations and is settled at realised values, is worth about a third of the baseline run's flexibility value and roughly half the reserve step of the ladder in \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder}. That figure is a lower bound rather than an estimate, because demand is also known perfectly and there is no unit commitment for foresight to plan around. \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} carries it as the model's largest single limitation.

\autoref{fig:3.4.1 lp schematic} sets the formulation out in outline: the input series and parameter sets the program reads, the single cost-minimising objective that consumes all of them at once, and the six quantities it returns. The constraints that stand between them are the numbered equations above, and the nomenclature table names every symbol in them. Two properties of the formulation are worth stating alongside the figure, because both are easy to assume otherwise. The scheduled and the metered draw separate wherever an activation occurs, which is where the two-settlement convention of \autoref{eq:3.4.1 metered draw} enters. And reserve commitment is bounded three times over, by eligibility, by market depth and by deliverability, the third of which is what makes a commitment cost anything.

\subsubsection{Input Data and Provenance}
\label{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance}

Every input is named here with its source, its vintage and its resolution, and the inputs that are declared rather than sourced are named as such. \autoref{tab:3.4.2 model input} is the catalogue; this subsection states what the catalogue does not show, which is where the remaining uncertainty sits.

The heat demand series is the operator's own metered hourly production for 2021 to 2025, supplied under the data agreement (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). It is the single most consequential operational input, because its shape decides every capacity-binding hour in the optimisation. The 2024 series totals 1.92 TWh with a maximum hourly load of 763 MW, a peak-to-mean ratio of 3.49. The annual level is corroborated externally: the operator's net production reported in the national district heating statistics for the same year is within $0.3\%$ of it \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}. One property of the series is unresolved and is declared rather than assumed away: it records plant production, and whether it nets out heat sent to the dump coolers is not stated by the operator. The direction of the error is known and the magnitude is bounded by dump capacity, which is 62.5 MW.

Market data are taken at the resolution the markets settle. Day-ahead prices are the hourly NO1 auction prices from Nord Pool's data portal \cite{nord_pool_nord_nodate}, averaging 487 NOK/MWh across 2024. Reserve capacity prices for both directions come from the ENTSO-E Transparency Platform's contracted-balancing-reserves series, which republishes Statnett's procurement data \cite{entsoe_entsoe_nodate}, and the same series supplies the contracted volume that the market-depth constraint uses. Activation prices and activated volumes come from Nord Pool's balancing-market series \cite{nord_pool_nord_nodate}. The activated share of committed capacity is computed per hour from the fifteen-minute record as activated quarters divided by quarters present, which avoids billing a partially activated hour as a full one; the model remains hourly and takes no decision at sub-hourly resolution. That finer record begins on 17 October 2023, so the fractional treatment is available for the 2024 and 2025 delivery years of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} and not for 2023 or for the earlier years the year bound runs on. Where it is unavailable the model falls back to a binary rule that sets the activated share to one in any hour carrying an activation, and it falls back for the whole window rather than for the uncovered part of it, because mixing two definitions of the same quantity inside one solve is worse than using the cruder one throughout. Which rule each run used is carried on the run's own output rather than inferred, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} states what the split costs the year axis. Currency conversion uses Nord Pool's daily exchange-rate series, averaging 11.63 NOK per EUR across 2024.

Tariff and tax parameters are read from the published instruments. The operator's entire boiler fleet is on the interruptible arrangement (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026), which prices grid use as a volumetric charge of 30 NOK/MWh plus a subscribed-capacity charge that is fixed in subscribed megawatts and therefore moves no dispatch decision \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}. The operator's own all-in figure for the arrangement, roughly 5 øre/kWh (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026), is read in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} as covering the volumetric components alone; on that reading it is consistent with the 30 NOK/MWh rate and excludes the subscribed charge, and the reading is repeated here because every tariff difference the analysis reports rests on both components. The subscribed charge is nonetheless carried in the ledger, because the tariff comparison of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} is meaningless if either tariff's capacity charge is counted at zero; the operator's holdings split 60, 30 and 10 per cent across the three notice variants (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026), so the charge is a blended figure. The standard-tariff counterfactual uses Elvia's published high-voltage business schedule as a calendar-accurate monthly vector \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}. Every parameter in this paragraph is a policy parameter and is therefore read at the 2024 vintage in all three delivery years, on the standardisation \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} defends: both Elvia schedules, the electricity tax, the carbon levy and the reserve market's minimum bid size are held, while demand, day-ahead prices, reserve prices and the activation record are each year's own. The reduced electricity tax for district heating producers is 0.580 øre per kWh at that vintage, set in the annual Storting resolution on excise duties \cite{stortinget_stortingsvedtak_nodate}; eligibility is assessed at entity level on a fuel-mix test, and the electric-boiler share the model produces stays far below the threshold at which the standard rate would apply. The waste-incineration CO$_2$ levy at the same vintage is 882 NOK per tonne for non-quota emissions and 176 NOK for quota-liable emissions \cite{stortinget_stortingsvedtak_nodate}; Klemetsrud is not covered by the emissions trading system in the modelled years (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026), so the non-quota rate applies. The statutory rate was 238 NOK per tonne in 2023 and 908 in 2025, and holding it at 882 across all three is the largest single instance of the standardisation; what that is worth is measured in \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} and bounded in \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}. The levy is charged on an emission factor of 0.2482 tonnes per MWh of heat, and the factor is derived from the operator's own audited accounts rather than from the statute: the annual report gives fossil carbon dioxide from waste incineration separately from biogenic, at 226{,}100 tonnes in the baseline year against 911 GWh of thermal output \cite{hafslund_arsrapport_nodate}. The derivation carries two cross-checks that both hold, a fossil share of 47.5 per cent against the report's own account of roughly half the stream being biogenic, and 1.26 tonnes of carbon dioxide per tonne of waste treated, which is an ordinary figure for mixed municipal waste. The factor is a single annual average applied to every hour, and it is the 2024 vintage's: the same report gives a factor 21 per cent lower for 2025, so the 2025 delivery year is billed throughout at a factor above its own. That is the standardisation working as designed rather than an error left in place, and its effect is the same as the levy rate's: it raises the level of every figure from that year and cancels exactly in every difference taken within it. The resulting marginal cost is the incinerator's entire variable cost in the model, because no gate-fee credit is netted against it. That makes the incinerator's marginal cost an upper bound, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions} states the consequence.

The peak-boiler stack is the part of the cost base that changed most in construction, and it is stated in full because two of its inputs are not sourced. The concession lists the peak fleet as pellet, LNG and bio-oil or oil boilers across ten sites, summing to 534.8 MW, and the operator's own account of the merit order puts pellets first and oil last (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026). Each tranche is therefore priced separately and the model chooses the order itself. \autoref{tab:3.4.2 peak stack} gives every component of every price. The pellet price is a Danish socio-economic price at the heating-plant delivery point, indexed to 2024 and converted to Norwegian kroner \cite{energistyrelsen_dk_samfundsokonomiske_2024}; it is a proxy, and the band spans the delivery point rather than a price forecast. The bio-oil price is decomposed from Norwegian monthly diesel prices by removing value-added tax, road-use duty and the CO$_2$ duty that heating oil does not carry \cite{statistisk_sentralbyra_statbank_nodate}. The LNG price is the one that is not sourced: it rests on an assumed Dutch TTF annual average of 34 EUR/MWh, for which no citable annual figure was obtained, and on a declared small-scale delivery premium of 35 per cent covering liquefaction, shipping, regasification and margin, for which no public reference exists in the corpus. Its band of 450 to 700 NOK/MWh$_{th}$ exists because of those two, and the LNG tranche is the only fossil one, so it alone carries the CO$_2$ levy and it alone sets the fossil share of displaced peaking heat that \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation} prices. Where the LNG price is used, it is used as a band.

Four further conversion constants sit inside those prices and are stated here rather than left inside the arithmetic. Three are boiler thermal efficiencies, which turn a fuel price into a heat price: $0.87$ for the pellet boiler, taken from the technology catalogue's wood-pellet sheet \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}, and $0.95$ and $0.90$ for the gas and oil boilers, both declared. The fourth is the lower heating value of natural gas at 11.0 kWh per standard cubic metre, which converts the levy statute's rate per cubic metre into a rate per MWh; it is the conventional Norwegian figure and is adopted by convention rather than cited. A bio-oil energy content of 10.0 kWh per litre performs the same role on the oil tranche and is likewise declared.

Techno-economic parameters for the flexible assets come from the Danish Energy Agency technology catalogues at central estimates, excluding taxes \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}, \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}. Electric boilers above 10 MW enter at an efficiency of $0.99$ and 0.70 EUR/MWh of variable operating cost, which is the catalogue's total variable figure less its electricity component, since electricity is priced explicitly in the objective and would otherwise be charged twice. Large seawater heat pumps supply the variable operating cost of 11.5 NOK/MWh. The accumulator enters at a full-cycle round-trip efficiency of $0.98$ and a standby loss of $0.186\%$ per day, both read from the large hot-water tank sheet \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}. Three catalogue conventions carry into the assumptions of \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions}. Catalogue costs exclude value-added tax and all duties, so the electricity tax, the grid tariffs and the CO$_2$ levy enter as explicit model terms with no double counting. Heat-pump drive and auxiliary electricity sit inside the coefficient of performance, so the model buys heat-pump electricity at spot plus tariffs. And the Danish electric-boiler capital cost includes grid-connection fees and transformer and distribution-board costs \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}.

One asset parameter is measured rather than taken from a catalogue. The heat pumps' coefficient of performance is computed as 3.19 from national district heating statistics, dividing delivered heat-pump heat by heat-pump electricity consumption across seven years, which also yields its own uncertainty band of 2.90 to 3.48 \cite{statistisk_sentralbyra_statbank_nodate}. Replacing a declared constant with a measurement is worth doing here because the coefficient scales the cost of every heat-pump megawatt-hour.

Three inputs are declared with no source, and each is stated with what it is worth. The accumulator's usable fraction of $0.90$ is a thermocline and return-layer allowance; it is attributed to the technology catalogue but the specific row could not be located, so it is treated as declared, and moving it across its plausible range shifts the annual flexibility value by about $\pm 1.6\%$. The accumulator cycling charge of 1.0 NOK/MWh is a degeneracy tie-break before it is an economic assumption, and saying which it is matters: with two costless heat-disposal paths available the optimum was not unique, so the solver could return an arbitrary vertex with large simultaneous charge and discharge that changed the objective by nothing. The charge removes that indeterminacy wherever the overlap is genuinely arbitrary, and a residue survives in five of the eighteen runs, all of them at partial or applied reserve access. The residue is neither degeneracy nor a solver artefact, and the distinction has to be drawn because the two would be reported differently and only one of them is a property of the system. Measured across every hour in which the overlap appears, the dump coolers stand at their capacity in all of them, the waste incinerator sits exactly on its minimum-load floor in all of them, and a down-regulation commitment is live in between two-thirds and all of them. The optimisation is disposing of must-run heat that has nowhere else to go, by cycling the store and paying the round-trip loss to destroy it: at a round-trip efficiency of $0.98$ that destroys twenty kilowatt-hours for every megawatt-hour cycled. It is a priced dispatch decision under a binding physical constraint, and it appears only where the down-regulation leg pushes the system into heat states the coolers cannot absorb. \autoref{3-subsec:3.8 model validation} tests for it and \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation} reports what the test found, untuned. The cycling charge's own total cost ranges from $0.011\%$ to $0.068\%$ of the objective across the eighteen runs, and forbidding the overlap properly would require a complementarity condition and therefore binary variables, which the formulation excludes. Waste-incinerator availability is flat across the year with no maintenance profile, because the only route to an outage profile is inference from summer dips in aggregate production, which cannot separate a plant outage from a demand trough. That declaration is bounded: imposing a summer derate of 30 to 50 per cent moves the annual flexibility value by at most 2.1 MNOK, and it moves it upward rather than downward, because less must-run heat lets cheaper sources in.

Weather data are used only as a fallback. Where a delivered demand series is unavailable for a year, daily heat volumes are modelled from heating degree days computed from hourly air-temperature observations at Oslo Blindern \cite{norsk_klima_service_senter_observasjoner_nodate}, spread evenly across the hours of the day. The fallback is not used in any run reported here, and its calibration is retained only because a delivery year outside the delivered range would need it.

The data provenance carries one structural constraint on the reliability claim. Nord Pool data, the Statnett balancing series and the operator's own production data are licensed or confidential and cannot be redistributed in an appendix or a public repository. The thesis therefore does not claim reproducibility from public data alone. The reliability claim is procedural replicability, meaning that a researcher with equivalent data access following the formulation, input catalogue and validation programme of this chapter would recover the same architecture and similar findings, and it is stated once here rather than repeated in \autoref{3-subsec:3.9 validity}.

\subsubsection{Assumptions and Simplifications}
\label{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions}

The formulation carries twelve consequential assumptions. Each is stated with the precedent or the measurement that defends it and, where one exists, the alternative considered and not pursued. Simplification is the design intent rather than a concession: the model's job is to size a difference, not to be the central output.

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A1. Cost minimisation under a fixed delivery obligation.} Heat demand is exogenous and must be met in every hour, so the operator minimises cost rather than maximising revenue and heat pricing never enters. The framing is the standard one in the Nordic dispatch lineage \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}, \cite{tromborg_flexible_2017}, and it matches the operator's own description of production planning as a cost-minimising exercise under a delivery obligation (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026).

    \item \textbf{A2. Fixed heat demand profile, no demand-side elasticity.} End-user response is excluded by the scope decision in \autoref{1-subsec:1.4 scope and limitations}. The thesis values flexibility from the operator's supply side. Together with A1 this closes the consumer side of the problem completely, since demand does not respond to price and revenue does not respond to dispatch, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} states what that costs the analysis in reach.

    \item \textbf{A3. Perfect foresight within the year, and it splits in two.} The model solves one annual problem and knows prices, demand and activations in advance. Foresight over \emph{time} is worth nothing here and this is measured rather than asserted: a rolling formulation with a 48-hour lookahead reproduces the annual optimum exactly, because the store is about five hours deep. Foresight over \emph{outcomes} is a genuine overstatement, worth about a third of the baseline run's flexibility value, concentrated entirely in the reserve leg, and measured by re-solving the same problem with the operator blinded to the reserve series and settled at realised values. The richer alternative is multi-stage stochastic programming, applied to Norwegian aggregator bidding in \cite{ottesen_multi_2018}, and two findings in that work support the rejection rather than the adoption. It reports the perfect-information bound at $33\%$ above the stochastic solution, with $84\%$ of that gap concentrated in roughly five unforecastable extreme-price days, so the machinery does not add value proportionally for a gap-quantification question. And it explicitly excludes grid tariffs and capacity charges, which is precisely the constraint this thesis places at the centre of the operator's problem. The measured overstatement here, at a third of the baseline run's flexibility value, sits almost exactly on that study's own figure.

    \item \textbf{A4. Three-tier capacity structure.} Fleet capacity enters as the three tiers of \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope}, each capping a different revenue stream: installed capacity bounds nothing directly because the concurrent bound binds first, the concurrent bound caps what can run in any hour, and reserve eligibility caps what can be sold into the balancing market. The asymmetry between the second and third tiers is a designed result of the scenario stack, not an assumption footnote.

    \item \textbf{A5. Price taking, extended from the power market to the reserve market.} \autoref{1-subsec:1.4 scope and limitations} declares price taking in the power market and, by extension, in the reserve market. In the power market the assumption is uncontroversial for an operator drawing at most 250 MW in a zone of NO1's size. In the reserve market it is an extension rather than an application, because that market is orders of magnitude smaller, and the extension weakens as committed volume rises. At the applied-access run the model supplies more activated up-regulation energy over the year than the whole NO1 zone actually consumed, and its eligible capacity exceeds the volume the zone contracted in the median hour. The operator states directly that entering with the 200 MW applied for would add supply and push reserve prices down (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). No haircut is applied, because a haircut needs a reserve supply curve this model does not hold and inventing one would be worse than declaring the limit. The applied-access results are therefore reported as an upper bound, with this caveat attached wherever they appear.

    \item \textbf{A6. Reliability at the full call, energy at the expected share.} Deliverability constraints require that a full activation could be honoured; energy and revenue are accounted at the expected activated share. The pairing is the standard reserve-procurement idiom, and it is stated because the two look inconsistent if the difference between a feasibility requirement and an accounting convention is not named.

    \item \textbf{A7. Manual reserve only.} Automatic frequency restoration reserve and frequency containment reserve are excluded on the physical ground given in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.1 model formulation}, that their response windows sit below the resolution at which an hourly model can claim deliverability, and that they require continuous automatic control the fleet does not operate under. The published FCR-N unit value that appears in the socio-economic literature is used as a magnitude benchmark only, never as a substitute for modelled reserve revenue \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}.

    \item \textbf{A8. No capital-cost amortisation inside the dispatch objective.} The program minimises operating cost. Investment costs are surfaced separately where the discussion needs them, and no annuity or discount rate enters the arithmetic anywhere in this thesis: both sides of the comparison are annual flows.

    \item \textbf{A9. No network thermal dynamics, and the network as a scalar.} Propagation delays, temperature regimes and distribution losses are abstracted away, and the architecture that would incorporate them is set out in \cite{vandermeulen_controlling_2018}. The district heating network's own transfer limits enter the model as a single number, the concurrently dispatchable 250 MW, which stands in for a network the model does not represent. This is the model's largest structural simplification. Under the interruptible tariff it binds in two to five per cent of hours depending on the delivery year, and relieving it to the licensed fleet moves the annual cost by a couple of million kroner. Under the standard tariff it never binds at all: the monthly capacity charge stops the fleet reaching 250 MW in the first place, so two constraints are stacked and only the outer one does any work. That asymmetry is not a footnote to the assumption, it is what makes the technical barrier separable from the tariff barrier at all, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} prices it with a sensitivity rather than leaving it declared. Its more important consequence is on the socio-economic side and is stated in \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}.

    \item \textbf{A10. Deterministic linear program, no unit commitment.} Binary start-up variables and minimum-load constraints on the flexible assets are dropped from the precedent formulation \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016} on the grounds given in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.1 model formulation}. The waste incinerator does carry a minimum-load floor, which is imposed as a continuous lower bound rather than as a commitment decision. The bi-level strategic-investment alternative \cite{tan_strategic_2022} is rejected at the design level rather than the assumption level: its price-maker formulation does not transfer to a Norwegian operator at this scale.

    \item \textbf{A11. Flat waste-incinerator availability, with a minimum-load floor.} Availability is constant across the year for the reason given in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance}, and the declaration is bounded at 2.1 MNOK per year on the flexibility value. The floor is set at half of availability on the operator's own account of what the boilers tolerate before steam production and turbine operation are compromised (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026). The floor is a rounded verbal answer, it binds in just under half of the baseline year's hours, and it is what makes the reference case of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder} feasible at all.

    \item \textbf{A12. The whole peak fleet counts as reserve cover.} Deliverability of a reserve call may be covered by any spare capacity, including the peak boilers, rather than by a restricted subset. The alternative was tested: requiring the peak fleet to count for nothing at all, so that only the accumulator and spare heat-pump capacity may cover a call, costs 0.098 MNOK per year, which is $0.15\%$ of the annual flexibility value. The simplification is stated with that price rather than left as an open question.
\end{itemize}

One property of the year axis has to be stated alongside these assumptions, because it changes what a reserve result from most of it means. The operator entered the manual reserve market in 2025 (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026), which is the last of the three delivery years. The reserve revenue the model reports for 2023 and 2024 is therefore counterfactual: it is what the operator's existing prequalified capacity would have earned in that year's market had it been offered, not what the operator earned. The counterfactual is the right object for this thesis, since the question is what the flexibility resource is worth under the market conditions the operator faces, and the prices, volumes and activation shares are the realised ones. But it means no reserve figure from those two years can be reconciled against the operator's published accounts for them, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.8 model validation} is designed accordingly.

The incinerator's marginal cost deserves a separate note, because it is the largest single number in the cost base and it is an upper bound rather than an estimate. It is the CO$_2$ levy alone, with no credit for the gate fee the operator receives for accepting waste, and it accounts for roughly $36\%$ of the baseline run's total cost. The fee is published rather than unknown: the operator's price list sets treatment of commercial waste at 2{,}623 NOK per tonne excluding value-added tax, with a CO$_2$ surcharge of 502 NOK per tonne charged on top \cite{hafslund_priser_2024}. That surcharge recovers from the waste customer the levy the operator pays to the state, so the levy remains a real cost to the operator and stays in the model; what the model omits is the revenue side of the same transaction. The incinerator's cost is therefore an upper bound on a known basis rather than an unknown one, and \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} states what else follows from it. Zeroing it moves the annual cost level by 234 MNOK and the annual flexibility value by 2.5, measured by re-solving rather than by arithmetic on one run. That contrast is the clearest single argument for reporting differences rather than levels, and it is why \autoref{3-subsec:3.2 evidence base} places absolute levels in a weaker evidential tier than differences. It is also why the levy is swept in full as one of the sensitivities of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} rather than being asserted to cancel.

One public validation anchor closes the model design and is described in \autoref{3-subsec:3.8 model validation}. Company-level statistics derived from the national district heating register report Hafslund Celsio's annual net heat production and its split across five energy sources \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}, \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fakta_nodate}. The check requires no operator data, so it survives independently of the data agreement.

\subsection{The Counterfactual and the Scenario Stack}
\label{3-subsec:3.5 counterfactual and stack}

Every quantity this thesis reports is a difference, so two things have to be pinned before any of them can be read: what the difference is taken against, and what is varied to produce the set of differences. The first is the counterfactual and the second is the scenario stack. They are specified together because a scenario and a counterfactual are different objects, and mistaking one for the other is the single easiest way to misread the results.

%------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{The Counterfactual: a Capability Ladder}
\label{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder}

The definition of flexibility adopted in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions} is a modification of production or consumption relative to a reference profile, and it does not pin the reference. Three candidates are available and each produces a different headline. The resource can be treated as physically absent, so the comparison is against a system without electric boilers or a store. It can be treated as present but not dispatched on any signal. Or the operator can be treated as dispatching naively, so the comparison is against a worse version of the same behaviour. Choosing one and reporting a single number would hide the choice inside the result.

This thesis refuses the choice and reports a ladder instead. Four rungs are defined, each adding exactly one capability to the one below it, and each value in \autoref{4-sec:4 results} is a difference between adjacent rungs rather than a difference against one arbitrary reference. \autoref{tab:3.5.1 ladder} states them.

Three properties of the ladder must be stated explicitly, because a reader who assumes otherwise will misread every number above the bottom rung.

First, R0 is not a system without flexibility assets. It is a system with the assets and without an operational dispatch signal, which is exactly the structural-capacity-without-an-enabler state that \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions} defines and that the enabler layer of that subsection exists to describe. The boilers and the heat pumps are all physically present at R0, and the plant meets the same demand with the same fleet. What R0 removes is the hourly price signal: the day-ahead series is replaced by its own annual mean, so the level of the electricity price is preserved and its hourly shape is not. The accumulator and reserve-market access are absent at R0 as well, and they are absent at R1 too, which is why the step between those two rungs isolates the price signal alone: each rung switches on exactly one capability and changes nothing else. The merit order between technologies survives, because it lives in their marginal costs rather than in the price shape, and the plant runs on a fixed order. A reader who takes R0 for an asset-free system will read the first step as the value of installing electric boilers, which it is not.

Second, dump cooling is available in every rung, and it is forced rather than chosen. The waste incinerator carries a minimum-load floor, so in a configuration with no accumulator any hour whose heat demand falls below that floor has nowhere to put the incinerator's must-run output, and the problem is infeasible. That is not hypothetical: the counterfactual returned infeasible the first time it was solved with the floor in place. The physics settles it: the coolers exist primarily to keep the incinerator running when the network cannot absorb its heat, with opportunistic use during negative electricity prices only secondary (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). Dump cooling is therefore part of the incinerator's own must-run plumbing rather than a flexibility asset whose absence defines the counterfactual, and switching it off alongside the accumulator conflated the two.

Third, the ladder replaced a single no-flexibility baseline during model construction, and the two are not comparable. The earlier reference still dispatched against hourly prices, so it already contained the largest of the four capabilities. The change is a different denominator rather than a refinement of the old one, and no figure computed against the old reference transfers.

The design has one further warrant worth recording. Two independent model designs were produced for this thesis, each blind to the other and to the existing implementation, and both identified the undefined reference as the central definitional problem and both answered it with a capability ladder rather than with a baseline choice. Convergence between independent designs is weaker evidence than a measurement, but it is stronger than an unexamined default, and it is the reason the ladder is presented as a design decision rather than as a convenience.

The ladder is not the only reference in the analysis, and the second one has to be named here rather than discovered in \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.1 channels}. The ladder measures each capability step against the rung below it, and the bottom of the ladder is the price-blind R0. The gap arithmetic of \autoref{3-subsec:3.7 gap and attribution} measures each run against R1 instead, because the question it answers is what flexibility creates that the operator does not capture \emph{relative to how the plant actually runs today}, and the plant today is price-responsive. Both references are legitimate and they answer different questions, but they are not interchangeable and one channel changes sign between them. Against R1, the flexible operator burns less fossil gas than the reference and the carbon channel is positive. Against R0, the whole ladder burns more, because the first step substitutes gas for electricity in expensive hours, and the carbon channel is negative. \autoref{4-subsec:4.2.1 channels} therefore reports the carbon channel against both references with its sign, and R0 is treated throughout as a decomposition device rather than as a policy alternative: it is a reference against which capabilities can be separated, not a state any regulator could choose.

It carries no values of its own, because those are results.

\subsubsection{The Scenario Stack}
\label{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack}

The scenario stack is the grid of runs the ladder is crossed with. A scenario is two things and only two: a tariff structure and a reserve-access level. Each of the two is an axis because it isolates a mechanism the research question asks about, rather than because it spans a plausible future. The delivery year is not a third axis; it is a vintage that every scenario is solved on, and the distinction carries into \autoref{4-sec:4 results} rather than staying here. A scenario is a regulatory configuration, and re-solving the same configuration on a different year does not make it a different configuration. Reading it as one would present a year's weather and prices as though they were a policy setting.

The \textbf{tariff structure} runs the interruptible arrangement the operator actually holds, the connection-on-condition terms of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, against the standard schedule a firm connection would face, whose capacity charge is the monthly-peak capacity charge that \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} identifies as the economic barrier the corpus cites most often, and as one that acts on the operator's ledger and on what the plant burns at once. This axis is a counterfactual and not a scenario, and the distinction has to be drawn because the two tariffs are not two possible worlds. The operator's entire boiler fleet is on the interruptible terms (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026), so one is what is and the other is what would be. The difference between them is the cleanest quantification of the capacity-charge barrier the thesis can produce, and it prices the arrangement rather than forecasting a tariff change. Because the interruptible arrangement carries a subscribed-capacity charge that the optimisation correctly leaves out of its objective, the comparison is made on a tariff-complete basis that includes it; otherwise one tariff's capacity charge would be counted at zero.

The \textbf{reserve-access} axis runs the volume the operator may sell into the balancing market at three levels: today's connected and prequalified allocation of approximately 29 MW, a partial first-round approval at 100 MW, and the volume applied for at 200 MW. The step lengths are a decision and are defended here in one sentence, because they are not evenly spaced. The published regime ceiling for the Oslo and Akershus capacity-area group and its practically available reduction both sit within a few megawatts of today's allocation, and the answer does not change across that span, so a run spent there would be spent on precision rather than on information. The middle run is placed instead at a scale where the answer does change. The regime ceiling and the practical ceiling are still reported, as facts, carried by a continuous sweep of the access constraint rather than by a run of their own.

One feature of the access regime belongs here rather than in the barriers chapter, because it frames what the axis is measuring. The per-area caps are independent rather than poolable: the group ceiling is reachable only by an actor with prequalified objects in every area of the group \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. A share of each area's cap is additionally reserved for flexibility groups, which puts the practically available ceiling for a single large regulating object below the allocation the incumbent operator already holds, as \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} sets out from the system operator's own implementation notice. That is a finding rather than a parameter, and it is what makes the access axis worth crossing at all: the constraint being priced is not a ceiling the operator is approaching but one it has already passed.

The two axes are crossed completely. Six scenarios run, nothing is dropped, and the completeness is a design requirement rather than thoroughness for its own sake. A tariff comparison taken only at today's access would answer whether the capacity charge is expensive; taken at all three access levels it also answers whether the two barriers are independent of one another or compound, which is a question about the regime rather than about a price. Neither the earlier design nor the existing literature can state that, because both price one barrier at a time. \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.2 tariff barrier} reports what the complete cross found.

Each of the six scenarios is solved on \textbf{three delivery years}, 2023, 2024 and 2025, and each solve uses that year's own metered demand series, day-ahead prices, reserve capacity and activation prices, and activation record. All three are full calendar years, 8,760 hours in 2023 and 2025 and 8,784 in the leap year 2024, so none is annualised or rescaled and a difference taken within one year can be placed beside the same difference taken within another. The purpose is robustness rather than forecasting. The tariff comparison and the access bracket are the two quantities this thesis leans on hardest, and a quantity recovered from a single year is a quantity that might be an artefact of that year's weather, price level and activation pattern; solving every configuration on three years is the cheapest available test of whether it is. 2024 is the baseline year wherever one is needed, because its inputs are the most completely corroborated: it carries the operator's own audited emission factor and an externally confirmed annual production level. The interruptible tariff at today's access, solved on 2024, is accordingly the \emph{baseline run}, and it is what an unqualified reference to a single run means anywhere in this thesis.

Policy parameters are held at the 2024 vintage in every delivery year, and market data are not. This is the single standardisation the design imposes, and both halves of what it does have to be stated because only the second makes the first acceptable. The carbon levy on waste incineration, the reduced electricity tax, both Elvia tariff schedules and the reserve market's minimum bid size are read at their 2024 values whichever year is being solved; demand, day-ahead prices, reserve prices and the activation record are that year's own. What this buys is a clean reading of the year axis: a difference between 2023 and 2025 is then a difference in market conditions rather than a difference in market conditions confounded with three simultaneous changes of statute. What it costs is that \emph{a cost level outside 2024 is a counterfactual quantity}, what that year would have cost under 2024 policy and not what the operator paid, and no absolute cost or levelised cost from 2023 or 2025 may be read as a historical figure. Every \emph{difference} taken within a year is unaffected, because a held parameter sits on both sides of it and cancels exactly.

The size of that trade is measured rather than asserted, and it is the clearest single illustration of why this thesis reports differences. The largest held parameter is the carbon levy, which stood at 238 NOK per tonne in 2023 against the 882 the model bills. Re-solving the reference configuration across the levy's own range moves the annual cost \emph{level} by 169 MNOK between those two rates and the annual flexibility \emph{value} by 1.8, a ratio of roughly ninety to one. The quantity the research question asks about is structurally almost immune to the largest policy input the standardisation touches, because that input is charged on both sides of every difference. \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations} states the consequence for levels, and \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.1 lp sensitivities} reports the full sweep.

One input cannot be standardised the same way, and the year axis carries a definitional seam because of it. The activated share of committed reserve capacity is computed from the fifteen-minute balancing record, which for NO1 begins on 17 October 2023. The 2024 and 2025 solves therefore use the fractional rule described in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance}; the 2023 solve cannot, and falls back to a binary rule that treats any activation within an hour as a full hour. Measured on the years where both can be computed, the binary rule overstates activation duration by a factor of 1.16 in 2024 and 1.51 in 2025, and by 1.15 on the fifth of 2023 that the finer record does cover. The fallback is whole-window rather than partial by design, because mixing two definitions of the same quantity inside one solve is worse than using the cruder one throughout. The consequence is stated wherever it bites: reserve revenue is not measured on one definition across the year axis, so a comparison of reserve revenue between 2023 and a later year carries that seam and a comparison within any one year does not. Every run records which rule it used.

That the delivery years are whole years rather than seasons is a requirement of the method rather than a convenience, and the binding reason is the scarce-hour mask that channel C1 of \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation} rests on. The mask takes the tightest one per cent of hours by heat demand within whatever period it is given, so a period covering part of a year returns the coldest hours of that part and presents them as the year's coincident peak. On the delivered 2025 series the full year identifies 88 such hours, of which 60 fall in January and 22 in February, and the year's maximum load of 578 MW falls on 5 January. A shorter period that did not span midwinter would therefore have selected a different set of hours and priced grid relief against the wrong peak, whatever else it got right.

One property of the later delivery years is a cost the design accepts, and it is stated here rather than left for a reader to notice. The Norgespris retail scheme set out in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers} extended to district heating customers on 1 October 2025, so the last quarter of that year sits under a retail arrangement the earlier years do not. It changes nothing this model computes, for two reasons that have to be given together rather than one at a time. The first is that the scheme enters no term of \autoref{eq:3.4.1 objective} and no constraint: it is carried in the ledger as a memo line outside net cost and appears nowhere else. The second is why it cannot enter, and it is the more important half. Heat demand is exogenous and inelastic under A2 and heat revenue is invariant to dispatch under A1, so a scheme acting on what a household pays for heat has nothing in this model to act on. What that costs the analysis in reach is stated in \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}, and it is a real cost rather than a formality.

A further bound sits underneath the stack rather than inside it, and it extends the year vintages rather than repeating them. The three delivery years are crossed with both axes, which is what separates a finding about an arrangement from a finding about a year; the bound re-solves the reference configuration alone on every delivered demand year, so that a single-year headline can be read against the spread across years rather than presented as a point. The two meet at each of the three vintages, where the reference configuration is both a cell of \autoref{tab:3.5.2 scenario stack} and the bound's own point for that year; it is one problem and it returns one solution, reported in both places. Five years run, back to 2021 and taking in the 2022 energy-crisis vintage whose mean zonal price is roughly four times 2024's. The two earliest predate the fifteen-minute balancing record entirely, so both fall back to the binary activation rule and neither is comparable to the stack on reserve revenue. They are reported as a bound rather than as central cases, and the two that predate the record are reported with that limit attached rather than quietly dropped, because a large figure sitting in an output file with no caveat beside it will be read as a headline by somebody.

The counterfactual the flexibility value is measured against is tariff-specific, and saying so prevents a reading error that would otherwise be invisible. Each scenario is measured against a no-flexibility counterfactual solved on its own tariff structure and its own delivery year: a standard-tariff run against a standard-tariff counterfactual, an interruptible-tariff run against an interruptible-tariff one. Measuring a standard-tariff run against an interruptible-tariff counterfactual would return a number that is part flexibility value and part tariff difference, and under the standard tariff that difference is larger than the flexibility value, so the composite would come out negative and read as flexibility destroying value. The capability ladder of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder} is run at every one of the eighteen scenario-year combinations on the same principle, with its own R0 rung defined within the tariff structure and year it belongs to. The ladder adds no scenarios to the stack: its top rung is the scenario itself, solved once and reported in both places.

Two continuous sweeps sit beside the stack, and both are reported as sensitivities rather than as scenarios. The reserve-access constraint is swept across ten levels between a point below today's allocation and the full licensed fleet, under both tariff structures and in all three delivery years, which yields the marginal value of an additional megawatt of access as well as the bracket the three access runs give. The accumulator's usable energy is swept across ten sizes spanning zero to three times the installed store, at every one of the six scenarios, which answers whether a larger tank would be worth building and, more usefully, whether the commercial and socio-economic returns to storage diverge as it grows. The access sweep is taken at the today-access ceiling in each tariff structure and year, because a finite difference is taken at a run's own ceiling and a sweep started higher would re-trace the same axis from a different point rather than add one; the accumulator sweep is run at every delivery year, so the shape of the return to storage can be compared across vintages rather than inferred at two of them from the ladder's accumulator step alone. Neither sweep adds a cell to \autoref{tab:3.5.2 scenario stack}.

The business-economic sensitivity programme is one-at-a-time and is declared here in full, because \autoref{4-subsec:4.1.4 sensitivity analysis} reports its result and nothing may appear there that this chapter has not introduced. Each model input is moved to the endpoints of its own stated band, one input at a time, and the model is re-solved together with its counterfactual so that both a level and a difference are recovered for every input. Where a parameter has no band, the sweep uses the alternatives its own registry names. Bands are the input's own, never a uniform percentage: an invented $\pm 10\%$ would report the shape of the assumption rather than the shape of the uncertainty.

Four inputs are additionally swept as full re-solves at named points rather than at band endpoints, because for each of them the points are statutory or physical facts rather than an uncertainty range, and each answers a question the chapter has already raised and left open. The \textbf{carbon levy} is swept across eight rates spanning zero to well above any rate in force, which prices the standardisation defended above and shows where the levy begins to reorder the merit order rather than merely to raise its level. The \textbf{electricity tax} is swept across the four statutory 2024 rates, the reduced rate district heating production is billed, the general winter rate, the calendar-day-weighted general blend and the general summer rate, which prices the reduced rate as an arrangement rather than as a number. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.2 policy evolution} reads that rate as a second-order input cost, on the ground that it is about one per cent of the marginal cost of power-to-heat, and treats the tax as first-order only through the heat-price ceiling; charging the general rate instead tests the first of those two readings directly, on the input side where the chapter left it untested. The \textbf{thermal bottleneck} is swept between the operator's stated concurrent limit and the full licensed and network-connected fleet, which is what turns the technical barrier of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} from a declared input into an attributed quantity. And \textbf{market depth} is swept across the whole access axis by removing the constraint that limits committed capacity to the volume the zone actually contracted in that hour, which is the price-taking assumption of A5 in its pure form and prices what that declaration is worth. Each point is a full re-solve of both the run and its counterfactual, never arithmetic on one solution.

Two design features of the programme carry the argument rather than merely describing it. The first is that it reports two columns, a level and a difference, because the two behave completely differently. The largest inputs move the annual cost level by tens of millions of kroner and the annual flexibility value by one or two, and the incinerator's marginal cost is the extreme case, moving the level by two orders of magnitude more than the difference. That contrast is the quantified form of the evidential tiering in \autoref{3-subsec:3.2 evidence base} and it is what justifies a delta-led thesis. The second is that the rows are ordered by measured influence rather than by category, and the ordering is itself a result: the inputs at the top of the list are not the ones the modelling effort went into.

Two things the programme cannot do are stated with it. It is one-at-a-time, so it prices no interaction between inputs; two parameters that are individually small may not be small together. And the model is deterministic, so no row carries a probability. A sensitivity range in this thesis states how far an answer moves if an input is wrong, and never how likely it is that the input is wrong.

The socio-economic side carries its own sensitivity axes, which are specified in \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation} rather than here, because they vary valuation parameters rather than model inputs.

\subsection{Socio-Economic Valuation}
\label{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}

The socio-economic side prices the physical quantities the operator model produces at unit values taken from published Norwegian sources. It does not construct a cost-benefit analysis from scratch, and it does not decompose a published aggregate. This subsection states the framework the valuation runs under, the channels it prices and the channels it deliberately does not, and the sensitivity axes it is reported across.

The socio-economic side is not evaluated on parameters of the thesis's own choosing. Norwegian socio-economic analysis is governed by the finance ministry's circular, which fixes the values such an analysis must use: a risk-adjusted calculation rate of $4.0\%$ for the first forty years, falling to $3.0\%$ and then to $2.0\%$ thereafter; a tax-financing cost of 20 øre per krone on any net effect on public budgets; carbon valued at the ministry's annually updated price paths, with the high and low paths mandatory in sensitivity analysis; and a profitability criterion resting on aggregate unweighted willingness to pay \cite{finansdepartement_prinsipper_2021}. Two of its prohibitions bind this analysis as much as its parameters do: distributional weighting may not be applied inside the analysis, and net ripple effects may not enter it at all, each being reported separately where relevant \cite{finansdepartement_prinsipper_2021}. The carbon valuation follows the current path set, which places the central price at 990 NOK per tonne of CO$_2$ equivalent for emissions inside the European allowance system and 1,639 NOK for emissions under the effort-sharing regulation, against a sensitivity band running from 742 to 2,084 NOK \cite{finansdepartementet_karbonprisbaner_2025}. The effort-sharing path applies here, because the operator states that Klemetsrud is not covered by the emissions trading system in the modelled years (operator communication, Hafslund Celsio, 28 August 2026). Adopting this parameter set is a substantive choice rather than a neutral technical default: the rates and the tax cost are policy parameters that shape which measures pass, and they are Norwegian rather than international practice.

Two of those parameters do not reach the arithmetic, and saying so avoids a false impression of precision. Both sides of the comparison are annual flows stated in kroner per year, so no discounting and no horizon enter anywhere. The concession procedure that the triangulated sources were themselves built against runs on a 25-year lifetime and a $6\%$ real pre-tax discount rate, the parameters of NVE's alternative-analysis spreadsheet for district heating concession applications, which \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024} reports and criticises in its review of that procedure. Naming those parameters matters for commensurability across the source set, but neither is applied here.

The choice to triangulate rather than construct is warranted at corpus scale, by the same review the chapter opens on: of 123 district heating studies, nineteen monetised at least one benefit, ten described the method they used to do it, and the twenty-six monetisation instances between them used six different methods \cite{ahmed_district_2025}. A field that has not converged on a method offers nothing to import, and a bespoke framework built for this thesis would make method choices that are themselves ungrounded. Triangulation across independent published estimates, with the spread reported explicitly, is the field-appropriate response.

The anchor estimate is the THEMA report's headline of NOK 1 to 1.6 billion per year \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}, and what it measures has to be stated precisely because it is not what this thesis measures. The headline is the socio-economic value of \emph{conversion}, meaning the switch of 1.0 to 1.5 TWh of electric heating to waterborne load served by district heating. Its composition is freed grid capacity at NOK 250 to 430 million per year plus a gross freed-electricity value of NOK 750 to 1,200 million per year, the latter reported gross of conversion and production costs \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. The first of those two components shares its numerals with the 250 to 430 MW of capacity it prices, because the report applies a mid unit value of exactly NOK 1 million per MW per year; the interval appears below in both units, and only the unit distinguishes them.

The headline is therefore not decomposed. The anchor report states the value of balancing services separately from that aggregate and in addition to it, and attaches a rough order of magnitude to it: a case exercise on the operator's own electric boilers, valued with the system operator's reserve-market calculator, yields NOK 47,000 per MW for a winter month in FCR-N and NOK 330,000 per MW per year once delivery is assumed across the October to April heating season \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. That figure is carried as context only, since it prices a different reserve product from the mFRR participation modelled here, covers capacity payments alone, and rests on months the same report places 30 to 40 per cent below the annual average, rather than inside it, and it says the same of dispatch: greater use of storage and optimised electric-boiler operation frees capacity on top of the 250 to 430 MW interval \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. There is consequently no flexibility component within the aggregate to extract, and treating the headline as a ceiling on flexibility value would invert the source's own arithmetic, since a value stated as additive makes the aggregate a floor rather than a ceiling. The socio-economic estimate here is instead built upward from the dispatch quantities the model actually produces, each priced at a published unit value and attributed to the party that receives it, with the conversion headline carried alongside as context so that the category difference stays visible. The separation is enforced in the implementation as well as in the prose: the conversion figures are held as context rows that the summing routine refuses to accept.

The sponsor bias on the anchor is acknowledged once, here. The THEMA report was commissioned by Hafslund Celsio. The mitigation is triangulation across sources independent of the operator: national long-term market modelling from NVE \cite{nve_utviklingen_2024}, a building-stock flexibility valuation \cite{rosnes_energifleksible_2023}, a neighbourhood seasonal-storage case study \cite{kauko_assessing_2022}, national building-stock peak-power and system-cost scenarios \cite{SINTEF_energy_2023}, and a national demand-response magnitude with its hydropower income finding \cite{ahang_analysis_2025}. Corroboration independent of the Hafslund commissioning comes from the energy commission's report on national district heating status \cite{energikommisjonen_mer_2023} and from a capacity-balance study prepared for the ministry \cite{thema_consulting_group_har_2022}. Where a source is operator-adjacent grey literature rather than peer-reviewed or public-sector work, it is flagged as such at the point of use and is never a primary anchor.

Channel C1 prices grid relief, and its construction needs stating because the definition of the scarce hours is a real choice. The quantity is the reduction in the operator's metered electricity draw during the tightest one per cent of hours of the year, where tightness is ranked by the operator's own measured heat demand rather than by the electricity price. Ranking by price would make the mask endogenous to the behaviour being measured, since the price signal is exactly what the optimisation arbitrages against; ranking by demand uses a temperature-driven signal that is exogenous to it, and Norwegian grid peaks are temperature-driven. The quantity is reported signed and unconditional, so an hour in which the flexible operator draws \emph{more} than the reference counts against the channel rather than being excluded from it. The unit value is the anchor report's freed-grid-capacity figure of NOK 0.7 to 1.6 million per MW per year, with a midpoint of about 1.0, which spans three different valuation methods and is therefore reported as a band rather than a point \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}.

The change in draw is additionally reported separately for the scarce hours that carry a reserve commitment and those that do not, because the two are opposite mechanisms and their mean describes neither. In an hour with no commitment the flexible operator can draw less than the reference, which is relief, or more, where price-driven dispatch has scheduled the boilers into a scarce hour. In an hour backing a reserve bid the commitment can add draw, because the boiler has to be running for the bid to be sheddable, while the activation shed removes it, and which of the two prevails depends on whether the reference plant would have been running the boilers anyway. Where the backing draw outweighs the shed, the balancing-market and grid-capacity objectives pull against each other, and that is reported as a finding in its own right rather than netted away.

The two mechanisms do not merely widen the band around C1; they can decide its sign, and the analysis is built so that they are allowed to. The channel is reported signed rather than clamped at zero, because a clamped channel cannot distinguish an hour in which flexibility delivered no relief from one in which it made the peak worse, and those are different claims about the same system. Which of the two dominates is not a property of the flexible operator alone. It depends on what the counterfactual would have been doing in the same hours: where the reference plant is already running boilers well above what a commitment needs to back it, the backing requirement adds nothing and the activation shed is pure relief, and where it is not, the commitment is the only reason the boilers are on at all. Because that comparison is made inside whatever set of hours the mask selects, widening the mask or ranking it differently changes which of the two regimes most of the selected hours belong to. \autoref{4-subsec:4.4.2 valuation sensitivities} therefore reports the mask as a sensitivity on the channel's sign and not only on its magnitude, and no figure for C1 or for any total containing it is stated in this thesis without the mask that produced it stated beside it.

C1 carries one limitation that must travel with every figure derived from it. The unit value prices permanently avoided network reinforcement, which is a property of a peak. Under the interruptible tariff the model's own maximum metered draw is identical in every run and in the counterfactual alike, because the concurrently dispatchable bound of \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope} pins it there; under the standard tariff the bound never binds, the monthly capacity charge sets the peak instead, and the flexible run's peak sits above its counterfactual's in every standard-tariff run rather than below it, which is the same reserve-backing mechanism described above showing up at the connection point. Freed capacity at the operator's own connection peak is therefore zero by construction under one tariff and negative under the other, and in neither case is it what C1 measures. C1 measures relief at the \emph{system's} coincident peak rather than released connection capacity. That is also what keeps C5 qualitative: one megawatt of freed headroom is either permanently avoided reinforcement or capacity released to a new load, and it cannot be both, so pricing C5 beside C1 would sell the same megawatt twice.

Channel C2 is the operator's realised reserve revenue at market prices, read directly off the dispatch ledger. It sits inside the business-economic perimeter that \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.1 economic definitions} defines, so it is reported for composition and is never added to the socio-economic side, which would double-count it. The published FCR-N unit value that appears in the anchor report, approximately NOK 0.33 million per MW per year on the electric-boiler fleet \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}, prices a product this model does not trade and is used only as a magnitude check on the modelled figure. It is never added to it.

Channel C3 prices the carbon content of the fossil peaking heat that flexibility displaces, and two properties of its construction matter. It is priced on the \emph{marginal} displaced fuel rather than on an average fossil share of total displacement: only one of the three peak tranches is fossil, it sits in the middle of the merit order, and the change in its own dispatch between two runs is the physical quantity a carbon price should be applied to. The fossil share of displaced peaking heat is consequently a model output rather than an input. The operator declined to supply peak-fuel volumes and prices, so a declared share would have been an invention; splitting the peak fleet into its three licensed fuel tranches and letting the optimisation choose between them means the share is whatever the dispatch produces, and it varies across runs and years for reasons the model can be interrogated about. And it is priced net of the levy, because the operator already pays the CO$_2$ duty inside its own dispatch cost, so only social cost above that duty is un-captured. The same emission factor is used on both sides, so the tonnes the model bills the levy on and the tonnes the valuation prices are the same tonnes. The net-of-levy rule has a consequence worth flagging in advance: because the gas duty and the allowance-system carbon path are within one per cent of each other, the channel is close to zero on that path and non-zero only on the effort-sharing path.

Channels C4, C5 and C6 are reported in words. Lower system prices in scarce hours is a real transfer to electricity consumers, but no unit value exists at the dispatch scale this thesis works at, and the anchor's consumer-saving figures are conversion-scale and belong to a different perimeter. Faster grid connection is excluded for the physical reason given above. Freed hydropower export and reservoir value follows the mechanism that district heating flexibility in a hydropower-dominated system frees generation capacity for export and balancing rather than absorbing renewable surplus \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}, and the scope guardrail rules out building a methodology around it. The rule that governs all three is stated once: a channel with no model-visible quantity is reported in words and never entered in a table as a zero. A zero asserts that the value is absent; an unpriced channel asserts only that this instrument cannot see it, and the difference is exactly what \autoref{5-sec:5 discussion} has to work with.

One further division cuts across the channels and is required by the governing framework rather than chosen. Items in the operator's ledger are either resource effects or transfers. Fuel and variable operating costs are real resources consumed. Grid tariffs, the electricity tax and reserve payments are transfers between parties, and a transfer is not a welfare effect: it changes who holds a krone without changing how many kroner exist. The ledger is split on that basis, and the split is what lets \autoref{5-sec:5 discussion} distinguish an instrument that redistributes value from one that causes more of it to be produced.

Sensitivity on the socio-economic side runs along five axes, and two of them are swept separately rather than combined. The carbon price is varied across the mandatory high and low paths, which is a requirement of the governing framework rather than a choice \cite{finansdepartement_prinsipper_2021}. The fossil share of displaced peaking heat is varied as a bound, because the operator declined to supply fuel volumes and prices, and both ends of that bound are low because three of the four peak fuels are biogenic. The freed-grid unit value is varied across the anchor's own three-method spread, and that spread is what the low, central and high band reported on the socio-economic estimate carries: the band is the grid-capacity method range, not a carbon range, and the two axes are never combined into a single triple. The scarce-hour definition is varied across both ranking methods and across five widths of the tightest-hours mask, at every run, so the reader can see how much of C1 depends on the choice defended above; that axis is the one on which the channel's sign turns rather than merely its size, which is why it is swept exhaustively rather than at two endpoints. And the hydropower channel is varied narratively rather than numerically, in keeping with its qualitative treatment. That price and externality assumptions dominate a socio-economic result of this kind is warranted by the governing framework itself, which makes high and low carbon sensitivity mandatory precisely because they do; the corroborating finding that a plant-level amortisation result swings between fifteen years and infinity on small changes in electricity, gas and CO$_2$ prices sits in that study's business-economic layer and warrants the same sensitivity on the operator side directly \cite{moser_socioeconomic_2018}.

The methodological precedents for pairing a business-economic and a socio-economic assessment of the same district heating asset are set out in \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap}, and neither transfers as a comparator. What this analysis takes from them is structural: the Danish study establishes that the two perimeters can be run on one asset and that their optima diverge \cite{ostergaard_business_2019}, and the Austrian one establishes the pairing as a method while reporting a macroeconomic rather than a welfare layer \cite{moser_socioeconomic_2018}. Neither runs a custom optimisation, and neither quantifies the divergence it reports, which is the cell \autoref{2-subsec:2.6 literature review} leaves empty and \autoref{1-subsec:1.5 contribution} claims.

The externality-coverage checklist closes the framework, and it is carried as a table on \autoref{fig:3.6 so triangulation} rather than restated here, so that what is out of scope is visible beside what is priced rather than absent. \subsection{Gap Arithmetic and Barrier Attribution}
\label{3-subsec:3.7 gap and attribution}

The gap arithmetic brings the two sides together. It is a short piece of accounting, and almost all of its difficulty is in stating what is being subtracted from what, so this subsection is mostly definitions.

The quantity reported is a division within the socio-economic total rather than a difference standing beside it. Socio-economic value in the framing adopted here is the sum of all welfare effects regardless of whose balance sheet they land on \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.1 economic definitions}, so the operator's own flexibility earnings sit inside it. What the analysis measures is how that total divides: the share the operator captures, and the share accruing to grid users, electricity consumers and society at large that reaches the operator through no mechanism at all. Reporting the division rather than a single subtracted figure is what makes the runs comparable, because an instrument that pays the operator for grid relief moves value from the second share to the first while leaving the total where it was.

Two mechanisms are distinguished throughout on that basis, and they answer to different instruments. The first is redistributive: value that is already created and not captured, which a payment route would transfer without changing what is produced. The second is enabling: reserve-market access is not a share of the total but a precondition for the flexible operation that generates it, so an access constraint suppresses value rather than misallocating it. The distinction carries into the discussion, because redistribution alone would be neutral in welfare terms. The argument for it is that changing what the operator earns changes what the operator dispatches and invests in, and it is that response, not the transfer, that moves the total.

That gives two registers, and the rule that governs them is that they are never summed.

The first register is the \textbf{un-captured externality}. It is the socio-economic value created by the dispatch that actually happens and that the operator receives nothing for. It is the sum of the priced channels that lie outside the business-economic perimeter, which is C1 and C3 of \autoref{tab:3.6 channels}, measured for each run against the price-responsive reference R1 of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.1 ladder}. R1 is the right reference for this register because the question is what the plant creates and does not capture relative to how it actually runs, and the plant actually runs against hourly prices.

The register is signed, and it is worth saying plainly that it may come out below zero. Nothing in the arithmetic forbids it: the sum of two signed channels is signed, and the freed-grid channel is signed for the reason \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation} gives. A negative value is not a defect to be clamped away and is not a statement that flexibility destroys welfare; it is the statement that in the hours the mask selects, the dispatch that flexibility produces drew more power than the reference would have, which the socio-economic side prices at the same unit value it would have priced relief. The design refuses the clamp deliberately, because a channel floored at zero reports \emph{no relief} and \emph{relief reversed} as the same number, and the difference between them is exactly the kind of finding a valuation framework exists to surface. Where a run produces one, \autoref{4-subsec:4.3.1 division} reports it with the mechanism rather than with an apology.

The second register is the \textbf{suppressed value}. It is the difference in what the operator can create at all between two regulatory settings, and it is measured between runs rather than between a run and a reference. The access bracket is its principal instance: the difference between the baseline run and the applied-access run is what the current allocation withholds. This register is measured as a difference in the operator's own ledger and is therefore, as measured, a transfer from the system operator rather than a welfare gain. What it \emph{enables} is separate and is reported separately: the physical quantities the socio-economic channels price grow between those same two runs, and it is that growth, not the revenue difference, that is a welfare effect. Reporting one number for both would claim a welfare gain for a transfer. The analysis does not attempt to decompose where the bracket's own surplus lands, because that would require the next-best reserve provider's cost, which this model does not hold.

Summing the two registers would be a category error. The first is a division of a total that exists; the second is the difference between two totals under different rules. They are reported side by side and never added, and no figure in this thesis is the sum of the two.

The primary reporting unit is annual, in kroner per year, on both sides. A per-unit form normalised against the technical flexibility envelope of \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope} would let the two perimeters share a physical denominator, and it is reported only where a denominator covering the same assets as the numerator is available. It is not, here: the numerator spans reserve commitment across the whole electric-boiler fleet, displaced peaking heat and grid relief, while the only available throughput denominator is one accumulator's. Where a defensible common denominator does not exist, the annual figure stands alone rather than being divided by a base that measures something narrower.

Attribution maps the division onto the four barrier types that organise \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}, which are this thesis's collapse of the nine categories of the district energy barrier taxonomy of \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}, mapped category by category in that section. In the resource-and-enabler terms of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions} and \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}, each barrier category is a missing or adverse enabler, so the attributed shares measure the cost of the enablers the Norwegian regime withholds from a flexibility resource that physically exists. The theoretical framing that connects the technical gap to the institutional one comes from the heat-and-electricity coordination literature \cite{mitridati_heat_2020}: district heating functions as virtual electricity storage without a market product to sell that flexibility into. The formal storage-equivalence quantification, a 3.51 per cent cost reduction and a 1.65 per cent wind uplift on a stylised six-bus case, is reported in \cite{mitridati_power_2018} and is cited for the formal result with the caveat that its magnitudes do not transfer.

\autoref{tab:3.7 attribution} sets out the attribution and carries one column that is easy to omit and important to keep. The five attributed quantities are not the same kind of object: one is a transfer, one is a cost difference between two tariff regimes, one is a cost difference between two physical states of the heat network, one is an un-captured externality, and one has no arithmetic row at all. Placing numbers of different kinds in one column invites a reader to rank them, and a ranking across kinds is exactly the category error the channel firewall exists to prevent everywhere else. The column stating the kind is therefore part of the table rather than a footnote to it, and the table is not a ranking.

The technical row is new, and it is the one that changes what the table can be used for. \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} argues that the technical layer bounds what the other three barrier types can be blamed for, and that argument was carried by the literature rather than by this case for as long as the network limit entered the model as a fixed input with no counterfactual beside it. The sensitivity of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} supplies the counterfactual, so the bound can be priced on the same footing as the tariff and access barriers and read against them. One caution travels with it. The relieved state is a heat network debottlenecked to the capacity the concession already licenses and the electricity grid already serves; it is not the connection queue of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers}, which is capacity nobody has built. The two are both technical and they are not the same measure, and a row that was read as the second would price a construction programme rather than a bottleneck.

\subsection{How the Model Is Validated}
\label{3-subsec:3.8 model validation}

A dispatch model of this kind can be internally consistent and still describe a plant that does not exist. This subsection states the tests that would have caught that, what each of them would have falsified, and what tolerance each carries. It reports no outcome: whether the model passed is a result, and results belong to \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation}.

The programme is written as a falsification exercise rather than as a checklist, and the distinction is not stylistic. A checklist asks whether the model behaves; a falsification programme asks what the model would have looked like if a specific part of it were wrong, and then looks for that signature. The tests are grouped by what each could break.

The first group tests the model against the operator's own published behaviour. The national district heating register reports Hafslund Celsio's annual net production split across five energy sources: recovered heat, flexible electricity, ambient heat, bioenergy and fossil gas \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}. The model reproduces all five. It is tested on the flexible-electricity share, within nine percentage points of the published share in each year, a band set against a sixteen-year observed range of 12.5 to 30.7 per cent so that a run falling outside it says something about the year rather than about the tolerance; the shares of the other four sources are not tested against the register, because the register extract the model carries holds the boiler share and the annual total alone. The flexible-electricity share is the demanding one, and it is the one that would falsify the peak-fuel merit order of \autoref{tab:3.4.2 peak stack}: if the pellet, gas and oil prices were wrong relative to the delivered cost of electric heat, the boiler would run at the wrong times and the annual share would miss. The register publishes that split for every year, so the test is run at each of the three delivery years rather than once at a reference year, and a share that holds in one year and fails in another is itself informative about which years the dispatch is trustworthy in. The annual delivered volume is cross-checked against the same register in the same pass, which is what makes the share test interpretable: a share can miss because the dispatch is wrong or because the demand series is wrong, and those call for different responses. Scale is tested separately against national heat and fuel statistics \cite{statistisk_sentralbyra_statbank_nodate}, which would catch a units or a capacity error that the share test could absorb.

The second group tests properties the model was never told to have, which makes them the strongest evidence available. The operator states that essentially everything in the system is flexible up to a heat load of roughly 550 to 600 MW and that flexibility falls away above it (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). That number is not an input to the model at any point. The load level at which the optimisation begins dispatching the peak boilers out of necessity rather than choice is therefore an emergent property, and it should land in that band. If it does not, the capacity frame of \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope} is wrong somewhere. The same group carries a behavioural test: the operator reports abstaining from electric-boiler operation in the highest-price hours without exception (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026), so the model's boiler should draw near zero in those hours. A model that runs the boiler through a price spike has a tariff or a levy term in the wrong place, since it is the sum of price, tariff and tax that decides the switch.

The third group tests the market side against the published literature. Reserve revenue per megawatt of committed capacity is compared against the corpus band for Nordic manual reserve, and storage arbitrage value per megawatt-hour of storage capacity against its own band. Both are order-of-magnitude tests rather than calibrations, and both need a stated reading rule to be interpretable: a revenue figure computed gross of the cost of backing the commitment is not comparable to a net margin estimate, and a band calibrated on a winter-only reserve regime is not comparable to a year in a daily market. Where the model sits above a band for a reason of that kind, the reason is stated and the figure is not adjusted.

The fourth group tests the arithmetic itself, and it is the group that must pass exactly rather than approximately. The ledger reported to the reader must reconstruct the optimisation's own objective value. The hourly heat balance must close. The storage trajectory must respect its bounds and must return to its opening state. Committed reserve must clear the market's minimum bid size or be reported as clamped. The reference runs must commit no reserve at all, since otherwise the reserve channel would not equal its own difference. And feasible-set nesting must hold: raising the access ceiling cannot make the operator worse off.

The fifth group is a single sign test on the newest term in the objective. A down-regulation activation means the operator buys energy, so the metered draw must rise above the day-ahead schedule in every activated down hour, and the additional draw must not exceed the committed capacity. If that sign were reversed the model would be paid for consuming, which is the failure mode the term was written to avoid, and the test exists because the term is new.

Three rules govern how the outcomes are reported, and all three are stated here so that \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation} is bound by them. First, a failing check is reported untuned. No tolerance is widened and no parameter is adjusted in response to a validation result, because adjusting a model to pass its own test converts a check into a calibration and destroys the evidence it was built to produce. Second, a failure is diagnosed before it is described: a numerical residual at the solver's precision and a structural defect are different things, and the report says which one a given miss is rather than reporting a count. Third, a tolerance is scale-relative wherever the quantity it is applied to has a scale, and every arithmetic check reports its worst observed violation whether it passes or fails. The two halves of that rule work together and neither is sufficient alone. An absolute tolerance set tighter than the solver's own feasibility tolerance produces failures that describe the solver rather than the model, and correcting such a tolerance is not the same act as widening one to accommodate a result; printing the residual alongside the verdict is what lets a reader tell the two apart without taking the verdict's word for it. \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation} states the outcome on every test above.

\subsection{Validity and Reliability}
\label{3-subsec:3.9 validity}

Validity is assessed against the four standard case-study tests, construct, internal, external and reliability, summarised in \autoref{tab:3.9 validity tests}. The empirical half of the construct claim is carried by the falsification programme of \autoref{3-subsec:3.8 model validation} and is not repeated here; this subsection covers the design-level threats that no model test can reach.

Two parallel scaffolds support the assessment. The first is the four regulatory criteria of revenue adequacy, economic efficiency, non-discrimination, and simplicity and stability \cite{bergaentzle_electricity_2019}, used as a vocabulary the policy discussion of \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications} can speak in, so that the external-validity claim is sharpened by alignment with language the field already uses. In that source revenue adequacy asks whether the network operator still recovers its costs under the instrument, economic efficiency whether the charge reflects the cost the user's draw imposes on the network, non-discrimination whether like users within a category are charged alike, and simplicity and stability whether the user can understand the charge in advance and plan against it; the source notes that the four compete, so an instrument is assessed on all four rather than scored on one \cite{bergaentzle_electricity_2019}. The second is a categorisation matrix run as a pre-submission coverage check across analytical scope, spatial and temporal resolution, economic perspective, modelling approach and system integration \cite{atabaki_systematic_2025}. Any cell left blank in that matrix is either a stated scope choice, in which case it appears in \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}, or a gap to close. The framing that comes with the matrix is one this thesis adopts for its own results: potential assessments are described there as dependent on assumptions and boundary conditions, and better read as descriptions of likely developments than as decrees \cite{atabaki_systematic_2025}.

\subsection{Limitations by Construction}
\label{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}

The limitations below are constraints the chosen architecture carries, rather than scope decisions about the substantive problem. The distinction matters: \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work} carries the study-level limitations that surface from running the analysis, while this subsection names what the design cannot do however well it is executed. Each is announced, bounded where it can be bounded, and pointed forward.

\textbf{The single case cannot support statistical inference.} One case examined in depth says nothing about the population of Norwegian district heating systems. The counter is that the design pursues analytical generalisation to theory rather than statistical generalisation to a population: the gap is partly a function of national regulatory context, which is uniform across Norwegian cases, and partly of operator cost structure, which is local to Oslo. \autoref{5-subsec:5.4 transferability} develops the conditional transferability of the findings to Trondheim, Bergen and the smaller Nordic operators using the four regulatory criteria of \cite{bergaentzle_electricity_2019} as the scaffold, and the Nordic comparators of \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.3 norwegian own case} strengthen that claim without converting it into statistical generalisation.

\textbf{The socio-economic estimate is triangulated rather than constructed.} The corpus-scale warrant for that choice is given in \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation} and rests on a field that has not converged on a monetisation method \cite{ahmed_district_2025}. The method risk it carries is specific: the anchor's headline measures conversion value rather than flexibility dispatch value, so an analysis that leant on the headline would attribute conversion benefits to flexibility. That risk is addressed structurally by refusing to decompose the headline at all, and the forward-looking direction is the construction of a Norwegian district-heating flexibility valuation framework that codifies what the corpus has not yet converged on \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work}.

\textbf{The operator knows more than any operator does, and the overstatement is now measured.} The model solves one annual problem with full knowledge of prices, demand and reserve activations. Foresight over time turns out to be worth nothing, for the physical reason given in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.1 model formulation}, which makes the annual formulation defensible rather than merely conventional. Foresight over reserve outcomes is a genuine overstatement of about a third of the baseline run's flexibility value, concentrated entirely in the reserve leg, and it is a lower bound rather than an estimate for two reasons: demand is also known perfectly, where in reality it is forecast with error, and there is no unit commitment, so no lumpy decision exists for look-ahead to exploit. The forward-looking direction is not a stochastic reformulation of the whole problem, which \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions} rejects on scope grounds, but a restricted one that blinds the operator to the reserve series alone.

\textbf{The model has no spatial dimension, and the reason is itself evidence.} The fleet is represented as one aggregate node, so no per-site connection voltage, no local network constraint and no site-level billing appears. Per-site operational detail was requested from the operator and refused on power-sensitivity grounds, and that refusal is permanent rather than pending. It is worth recording as more than an inconvenience: a system whose own flexibility resource cannot be described publicly at the resolution at which it is constrained is an instance of the observability problem that \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.4 institutional barriers} develops, and the refusal is citable evidence for it.

\textbf{The district heating network enters as a single number.} The concurrently dispatchable bound of \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope} stands in for the network's hydraulic and thermal transfer limits, which are the constraint the operator actually reports as binding. A scalar cannot represent a bottleneck that is local, directional and load-dependent, and the model therefore cannot say which part of the network binds or what relieving it would be worth. What it can say is what the aggregate limit costs, which the sensitivity of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} measures at a couple of million kroner a year across the range between the operator's stated bound and the licensed fleet, and that under the interruptible tariff the limit is what pins the operator's own connection peak, with the consequence for the grid-relief channel stated in \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation}. Under the standard tariff it pins nothing, because the monthly capacity charge holds the peak below it; the model therefore has nothing to say about the network limit under a tariff that never reaches it, which is a boundary of the finding rather than a null result.

\textbf{There is no own-price feedback.} The operator is a price taker in both markets it participates in. The assumption is uncontroversial in the power market and is an extension in the reserve market, where the model's own committed volume at the applied-access run would move the price the model is paid, on the operator's own account \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.3 assumptions}. No correction is applied, because a correction requires a reserve supply curve this model does not hold, and the applied-access results are reported as an upper bound with the caveat attached.

\textbf{The consumer side of the problem is absent, and that bounds what the analysis can say about retail policy.} Heat demand is exogenous and inelastic and heat revenue is invariant to dispatch, so nothing that acts on what an end user pays or consumes can move a quantity in this model. That covers both of the consumer-support schemes in force across the modelled years, described in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers} and \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.2 policy evolution}: neither enters the objective or any constraint, and neither could be made to without a demand response for it to act on. The consequence runs in two directions and both are worth stating. Nothing in the results is distorted by them, which is what makes a delivery year spanning a change in retail arrangement a legitimate comparator rather than a confound. And nothing in the results bears on them: their effect on end-user demand, their distributional incidence and their cost to the state all sit outside this design, and the analysis is silent on each. \autoref{tab:3.7 attribution} records that silence on the attribution side, where price regulation is the one barrier type carrying no arithmetic row.

\textbf{Absolute cost levels rest on inputs that differences do not.} The single largest term in the cost base is an upper bound, and two components of the peak-fuel merit order are uncited or declared \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance}. A further one sits underneath all of them. The incinerator's CO$_2$ levy enters as a cost per megawatt-hour of heat, so the optimisation can avoid it by burning less waste. Whether that is the right treatment depends on whether annual waste throughput responds to heat demand, and physically it does not: the plant treats roughly 400{,}000 tonnes of residual waste a year \cite{dsbno_bygging_2026}, that waste arrives on Oslo's schedule rather than on the heat system's, and it has to be processed either way, which makes the annual levy closer to a fixed obligation than to a marginal cost. The model does not resolve this, and the direction is knowable, since a fixed obligation cannot be optimised away. Every one of these cancels between two runs that share it, which is why the analysis is reported as differences; none of them cancels in a level. A cost level from this model is therefore an order-of-magnitude statement, and it is never the basis of a claim.

\textbf{A cost level outside the reference policy year is a counterfactual, and it says so rather than being quietly presented as history.} The standardisation of \autoref{3-subsec:3.5.2 stack} holds every policy parameter at its 2024 vintage in all three delivery years, so a net cost or a levelised cost reported for 2023 or 2025 is what that year would have cost under 2024 policy and not what the operator faced. The largest instance is the carbon levy, held at 882 NOK per tonne against a statutory 238 in 2023, which on its own moves the annual cost level by 169 MNOK. The limitation is confined rather than general, and the confinement is what makes the design acceptable: a held parameter sits on both sides of every difference taken within a year and cancels exactly, so the flexibility values, the tariff comparison, the access bracket and every capability step are unaffected, and the same levy move shifts the annual flexibility value by 1.8. What the analysis therefore cannot do is place a cost level from one delivery year beside a published figure for that year, or read the three levels as a cost trajectory. It reads them as three years of market conditions priced under one statute, which is what the year axis was built to be.

\textbf{The model under-dispatches the electric boilers relative to what the operator actually did, and the miss is larger in the high-price year than in the others.} The share test of \autoref{3-subsec:3.8 model validation} is run against the public register in every delivery year, and the outcome is reported in \autoref{4-subsec:4.0.2 validation}; what belongs here is the boundary it draws rather than the result. The annual delivered volume matches the register almost exactly, so the miss is a dispatch miss and not a demand-series miss, and its direction is consistent: a cost-minimising linear program runs the boilers less than the operator does, most of all when power is expensive. Whatever made the operator's behaviour rational in those hours is not represented here, and the candidates are all outside the design rather than mis-parameterised within it: heat-quality and network-temperature requirements, contracted fuel or waste volumes, staffing and operating practice, or a hedged rather than spot-indexed power position. The consequence is a reason to lean on differences taken within a year rather than on levels compared across years, which is what the analysis does regardless; and it is a specific caution against reading the model's account of any single high-price year as a description of what the plant did.

\textbf{The technical envelope is first-order and incomplete on the energy side.} It is a static stock-and-headroom calculation, and its energy figure covers the accumulator alone, because the inputs needed to size the network's own thermal mass were not obtained \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope}. The quantified energy stock is therefore a lower bound on the physical resource. On the power side the envelope is tighter, since all three tiers are either licensed, operator-stated or published. The envelope's job is to bound rather than to resolve, and where operator-confirmed values replaced engineering estimates during construction it tightened accordingly.

One input-side caveat spans the catalogue-sourced parameters. Danish Energy Agency cost data for immature technologies carries real between-edition revision risk: a recent edition doubled the capital cost of the 20 MW seawater heat pump and raised geothermal levelised heat costs by 14 to 18 per cent against the previous sheets, on the agency's own admission that the then-young technologies had unreliable data \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}. The tank figures used here are drawn from the energy-storage catalogue, which classes large-scale hot water tanks as a mature and proven category 4 commercial technology \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}; the electric-boiler figures come from the separate electricity and district heating catalogue, which places electric boilers between category 3 and 4 and assesses their uncertainty as low \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}.
