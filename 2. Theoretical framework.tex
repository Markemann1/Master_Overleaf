\subsection{Energy System Flexibility: Concepts and Frameworks}
\label{2-subsec:2.1 concepts}

The field offers no single agreed definition of energy flexibility, and unstated definitional choices would otherwise leak into the valuation: what counts as flexibility decides what gets valued. This section therefore does the definitional groundwork for the rest of the thesis. \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions} states the thesis's working definition and separates the structural and operational layers of a flexibility resource from the enablers that convert dispatch into value. \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power} introduces the bidding-zone structure and the reserve products through which that flexibility earns revenue in the Norwegian power system, and \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.3 sector coupling} establishes power-to-heat as the specific sector coupling this thesis models. The resource-and-enabler split carries the connections forward: \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} catalogues the enablers that are missing or adverse in Norway, \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context} shows why the Norwegian enabler set is distinct, and \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} formalizes the value gap that opens when a resource exists but its enablers do not.

\avklar{FORMAT: key-point box rendered as the plain \textbf{.}-led paragraph below; alternative is a tcolorbox (title "Key point"), which requires adding /usepackage(tcolorbox) - not added silently.} \textbf{Key point.} A flexibility \textit{resource} (structural capacity plus operational dispatch) yields no value to its owner without an \textit{enabler}: a market, reserve product, or tariff that rewards its activation. Norwegian district heating holds the resource; this thesis quantifies what the missing or adverse enablers cost.


\subsubsection{Definitions and Frameworks}
\label{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}

No single definition of energy flexibility commands agreement across the heating and power-system literature, and the variation is consequential because each definition draws a different boundary around what counts as flexibility. Two recent reviews document the fragmentation directly: A SINTEF synthesis grades $16$ prior definitions against three criteria, finds none that satisfies all, and proposes a unifying one: the ability of an asset or system to modify its routine operation for a limited duration in response to an external signal \cite{degefa_comprehensive_2021}. 

A systematic review of $85$ building-flexibility studies finds that roughly $42\%$ of them give no formal definition of flexibility, and that the rest rely on $61$ distinct quantification metrics \cite{li_energy_2021}. Three broad traditions recur. The first treats flexibility as a physical property of the system, grounded in inertia and in the coordinated operation of coupled electricity, heat and gas grids, where flexibility is the ability to speed up or delay the injection or extraction of energy relative to the reference profile, requiring only that the system possess inertia \cite{vandermeulen_controlling_2018}, \cite{lund_smart_2017}.
The second defines flexibility operationally, as a response to an external signal, namely the managed shifting of demand and generation to meet grid and local objectives, quantified through market-tier participation or performance indicators such as the "Flexibility Function" rather than in absolute energy terms \cite{li_ten_2022}, \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}. 
The third, sociological, tradition argues that both framings take the timing of demand as given, and so risk stabilising rather than providing a genuinely flexible system \cite{blue_conceptualising_2020}, a caveat this thesis acknowledges while remaining within the engineering and economic frameworks. The traditions converge on flexibility as the temporal shifting of energy injection or extraction enabled by inertia, and diverge mainly on whether it is treated as a system property, a market-tradable response, or a control problem. This thesis adopts a definition that sits in the first two traditions and suits a district heating operator facing the power market. Energy flexibility is the capacity of an asset or system to modify its rate of production or consumption relative to a reference profile in response to a price- or system signal, within technical and commercial constraints \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}, \cite{degefa_comprehensive_2021}. 

A second distinction separates the physical capacity for flexibility from its operation, and this split organizes how that capacity is described throughout this thesis. The \textit{structural} layer is the embedded physical capability that makes deviation from the reference profile possible. In a district heating system it comprises the three sources of thermal inertia identified by \cite{vandermeulen_controlling_2018} -- the network heat carrier, dedicated thermal energy storage such as an accumulator tank, and the thermal mass of connected buildings -- together with the installed capacity of the electric boilers. The \textit{operational} layer is the dispatch decision that activates that physical capacity in response to a signal. These are two utilisation layers of the same technology: an electric boiler is structural in its installed capacity, but the choice to up- or down-regulate its dispatch is operational. Structural capacity without an operational dispatch signal therefore yields no flexibility. 

Structural capacity and operational dispatch together constitute a \textit{flexibility resource}, and whether that resource provides any value to the operator depends on a third layer, the \textit{enablers}. Markets, reserve products and network tariffs are the enablers, the external preconditions required to activate the value of a flexibility resource \cite{degefa_comprehensive_2021}, \cite{saele_understanding_2023}. A district heating operator can hold ample structural flexibility capacity and even dispatch it correctly, yet capture no value if no reserve product, bilateral contract or market mechanism exists. How the absence of these enablers creates a value gap between the socio-economic benefits and the commercial value of the same physical flexibility is formalized in \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}.

Operational flexibility is exercised in two modes. The first is load-shifting: advancing or deferring consumption relative to the reference profile without altering the total energy delivered, which repositions the time at which the same amount of consumption occurs. The second, and the principal operational lever in district heating with electric boilers installed, is directional regulation, the increase or decrease of electrical consumption in response to system conditions. The Nordic flexibility framing of the Flex4RES programme defines flexibility in these directional terms: the ability to regulate the increase or decrease of generation or consumption in response to market signals \cite{skytte_flexible_2019}. 
The two directions map onto opposite system needs. When electricity is in surplus and prices are low, a power-to-heat (P2H) asset up-regulates, increasing electrical consumption to convert surplus power into heat and charging the accumulator tank for later use. When the grid is stressed and prices are high, the same system down-regulates, reducing electric boiler draw and instead discharging stored heat from the tank, which relieves the peak load on the grid while still serving demand.

The power flexibility of a district heating system with a controllable electric boiler is central to this thesis. Golmohamadi distinguishes power flexibility, a response in the system's electricity demand, from heat-side flexibility, and names the electrically operated assets as the source of the greatest direct power-flexibility potential in district heating systems \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}. Bloeß and Schill frame the same flexibility as the \textit{flexible} use of electricity for heating, the timing of the conversion adjusted to system conditions rather than electricity simply substituting for fuel \cite{bloes_power--heat_2018}. 

How far that power flexibility can be shifted in time depends on the thermal buffer into which the converted heat can be stored, and later drawn from. Golmohamadi groups three such buffers into two kinds \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Passive thermal inertia:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item The heat carrier in the network pipes (FHC, real-time flexibility/intraday)
    \item The thermal mass of connected buildings (FTI, real-time flexibility/intraday)
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Active thermal inertia:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Dedicated thermal storage (FTS): An accumulator tank that scales from day-ahead arbitrage to seasonal shifting depending on its size.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
For a district heating operator working from the supply side, the relevant buffers are the network heat carrier (FHC) and the accumulator (FTS). Building thermal inertia (FTI) is a demand-side source outside the scope of this thesis. The buffer's parameters, namely its heat storage capacity, the sustainable deviation duration, and the ramp rate at which changes can be applied, determine which products the power-flexibility action can bid into \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}. Two revenue pathways appear. The first is reserve products, procured by the transmission system operator and graded by activation speed, which pay for capacity held ready and for energy activated on a system signal. The second is spot-market arbitrage, which rewards shifting consumption across the day-ahead price profile and requires a price spread between charging and discharging windows wide enough to cover the cost of flexible operation. Each pathway faces different eligibility barriers, activates on a different signal, and forms a separate component of the commercial value of thermal flexibility.

Just as the definition of flexibility differs, so does the estimation of its value. The term is applied to everything from reduced system operating cost or welfare gain in \cite{mitridati_heat_2020}, to the operating profit a district heating operator gains from stacking reserve-market participation onto its heat dispatch in \cite{javanshir_operation_2022}, to the uplift in the market value of variable renewable generation through offsetting the merit-order effect \cite{kirkerud_power--heat_2017}, as well as building or network performance indicators such as peak-to-average load ratio \cite{li_ten_2022}. These framings apply different system boundaries, count different actors as beneficiaries, and produce results in incomparable units. This thesis adopts a perspective-explicit working definition: Flexibility value is the monetary or welfare benefit attributed to a flexibility action, conditional on the system boundary and the actor whose ledger is being measured. \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} formalizes this as the commercial interest and socio-economic value perimeters.

\subsubsection{Flexibility in Power Systems}
\label{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power}

District heating flexibility earns its revenue in the power system, so the structure of that system decides which revenue pathways exist and what they pay. The Norwegian power system is organised into five bidding zones, NO1 through NO5, which clear separately through Nord Pool, each with its own spot price. The zones reflect physical transmission constraints between regions and are also a regulatory instrument for managing congestion: when transfer limits are reached, each zone clears its own price, which signals local scarcity or surplus \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}. Hydropower supplies close to $90\%$ of national generation, making it the dominant source in every pricing zone. NO2, in the south-west, is the largest producer and runs a substantial surplus, together with the western (NO5) and northern (NO4) zones, which also generate above local demand. NO1, the south-eastern grid including Oslo, is the demand centre of the country and is generally a net importer of power. 
\utsett{concrete figures around import vs export vs production type in the different regions - Electricity consumption and generation by price area (StatBank table 14092 \& 14093 SSB: September 2025, for instance, it generated about 1.6 TWh against 2.1 TWh of consumption, with roughly 94\% of its own output was hydropower, whereas NO2 produced about 3.8 TWh against 2.5 TWh of demand}.

The southern zones (NO1, NO2, and NO5) are strongly coupled and frequently clear at a single price. The sharper price divide runs between the south and the north (NO3 and NO4), not between the southern neighbour-zones \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}. Wind generation is concentrated in NO2, NO3, and NO4, whereas NO5 remains hydropower-dominated. Because NO1 is closely coupled to NO2, a wind-driven low-price surplus in the south propagates much of its market signal to the Oslo zone. The arbitrage value available to a power-to-heat operator in NO1 is therefore governed less by local conditions and more by southern wind and system coupling to continental Europe, and by congestion in the connector to the northern zones.

The resulting price-spread distribution sets the ceiling of the commercial arbitrage value available to Oslo-based DH operators before any market-access barriers are taken into account.

Norway's hydropower dominance reshapes the value logic of thermal flexibility relative to fossil-heavy or wind-heavy power systems \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023}. The combination of dispatchable hydropower and price-responsive industrial demand makes Norway the Nordic region's primary provider of system flexibility \cite{skytte_flexible_2019}. In the terms of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}, a hydropower reservoir is itself a structural flexibility asset at system scale. In a fossil-dominated system, a load that absorbs off-peak electricity displaces marginal thermal generation, reducing fuel combustion and the associated emissions. In Norway, where the marginal unit originates from hydropower, no fuel is displaced and the system-level benefit runs instead through dispatchable capacity. Modeling of the Norwegian case finds that displacing direct electric heating with district heating lowers the peak load on dammed hydropower and frees generation capacity for potential export \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. The primary system-level benefit of sector coupling in Norway is therefore freed generation capacity rather than saved fossil fuel cost and consumption. In model simulations of a 2030 Nordic system, power-to-heat in the district heating mix concentrates in the spring, summer and autumn rather than during peak winter demand, because the high electricity price during winter hours makes other heating fuels more competitive than electric conversion \cite{kirkerud_power--heat_2017}. 

Statnett procures balancing services through a three-tier product hierarchy that cascades through increased time-frames, from automated to manual activation. Frequency Containment Reserves (FCR) are activated continuously and symmetrically in response to real-time frequency deviations. Automatic Frequency Restoration Reserves (aFRR) restore nominal frequency automatically within seconds to minutes following a disturbance. Manual Frequency Restoration Reserves (mFRR) are dispatched manually on a 15-minute window and constitute the product tier most accessible to large thermal demand-side assets such as district heating electric boilers. Until December 2025 the minimum bid size for mFRR participation was $10 MW$ in NO2, NO4 and NO5 and $5 MW$ in the NO1 and NO3 bidding zones \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}, a threshold that excluded all but the largest aggregated demand-side assets. Two changes then arrived separately. The Nordic mFRR Energy Activation Market went live on 4 March 2025, moving activation to automated 15-minute units \cite{solberg_manedsrapport_nodate}. The minimum bid was cut from 10 MW to $1 MW$ with effect from 2 December 2025, approved by RME under Article 18 of the Electricity Balancing Guideline as a precondition for the European MARI platform \cite{statnett_1_2025} - a change Statnett had signaled in advance as part of its transition to automated balancing \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}. The bid-size floor therefore no longer functions as a structural exclusion mechanism for district heating, where a single electric boiler site now clears this threshold. For the sub-MW fringe of the fleet, qualification cost and operational and metering complexity still bind. For a large qualified actor, the binding constraint has instead moved to the administrative rationing of reserve access: Statnett caps the reserve volume that can prequalify per capacity area in grids below 110 kV, and new connections queue for grid capacity \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The consumption capacity charge remains a conditional barrier: binding in its standard form, largely neutralized for the incumbent's interruptible connections, as \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} develops. These barriers are quantified in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap}. The remaining barriers are further examined in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, and quantified in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap}.

\subsubsection{Sector Coupling and Thermal Systems}
\label{2-subsec:2.1.3 sector coupling}

Sector coupling denotes the intentional integration of distinct energy-carrier systems through conversion technologies that let energy flow in parallel across historically separate supply chains \cite{lund_smart_2017}. The operative coupling in this thesis is power-to-heat: the conversion of electrical energy to thermal energy through electric boilers and heat pumps, or the substitution of individual electric heating with district heating. Electric boilers and heat pumps create a controllable load on the power system and a corresponding heat source within the district heating network, making the network an active interface between the two carriers rather than a passive heat consumer. For both technologies the coupling is unidirectional: electricity is consumed to produce heat, with no reverse conversion within the asset. Combined heat and power (CHP), including waste incineration plants, couples the same two carriers but in the opposite direction, generating electricity alongside heat from fuel. Its flexibility comes from adjusting fuel consumption or the heat-to-power ratio. In the Oslo system it serves as base-load rather than as a P2H flexibility asset. Realising the value of the P2H coupling depends on policy and market design as much as on the conversion technology. A scoping review of sector-coupling research finds that policy integration and sector coupling are studied largely in parallel, with district heating the explicit focus of only one of the $25$ reviewed studies, leaving the coordinated policy conditions for P2H flexibility under-examined \cite{vanhanen_energy_2024}.

The structural flexibility of a district heating system, its thermal inertia, is what makes this interface relevant at meaningful scale. Network piping volume and dedicated accumulator tanks can absorb or release heat independently of instantaneous consumer demand, giving the operator a thermal buffer across which electrical load can be advanced or deferred. The structural capacity of that buffer, namely its volume, temperature range, and charge and discharge rate, sets the envelope within which operational load-shifting is feasible. When electricity prices are low, a P2H asset converts surplus power to heat and stores it in that buffer, to be released during high-price or high-demand periods, which can help free up grid capacity and attenuate price spikes and congestion. This coordination is framed as a virtual electricity storage function, in which the district heating system provides energy storage-equivalent services to the power system by synchronising its electrical load with system conditions, without a dedicated power-storage asset \cite{mitridati_heat_2020}. The same work shows that sequential heat-then-electricity market clearing is short-sighted, so an operator optimising within its own heat-market perimeter forgoes the system value that coordinated clearing would realise. The storage-equivalence itself is quantified in \cite{mitridati_power_2018}, which models district heating pipeline storage as a power-system resource on a stylised six-bus, three-node case. It reports a $3.51\%$ reduction in total cost and a $1.65\%$ uplift in wind utilisation when the virtual storage capability is unlocked. These magnitudes are case-specific, but the results indicate that heat storage-equivalence has merit, and that district heating thermal inertia carries quantifiable system benefit when the market design permits coordinated clearing. The critical qualification is that this value must be accessible to the operator who provides it. This accessibility hinges on an enabler: a reserve-market product, a flexibility contract, or market and fee structures that reward coordinated dispatch. Without such an enabler, the value accrues only to the grid operators and consumers, while the operator bears the full cost of flexible operation. This asymmetry between where value is created and where cost falls is what the thesis quantifies and seeks to reduce. 

The Norwegian consequence of this coupling value differs from other European nations in one important aspect. In systems with high shares of variable renewable generation (VRE), the primary system-level benefit of P2H is the absorption of surplus wind and solar energy which would otherwise be curtailed, thus raising the achievable penetration of renewable generation \cite{geabermudez_role_2021}. In Norway, where current curtailment is structurally low and the dominating marginal power unit is hydropower reservoirs, the corresponding benefit runs through dispatchable capacity rather than curtailment avoidance: substituting district heating for direct electric heating lowers the winter peak on dammed hydropower and frees generation capacity \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. The same work cautions that the demand reduction also increases forced export from reservoir draining, which it reads as a sign of system inflexibility rather than a gain \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. The coupling mechanism is identical across both contexts, but the value pathway differs. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context} develops the Norwegian system characteristics that produce these dynamics, and \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} places it within the commercial DH and socio-economic valuation perimeters.

\subsection{District Heating as a Flexibility Resource}
\label{2-subsec:2.2 DH as flexibility}

This section covers the technology stack through which district heating supplies the flexibility defined in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1 concepts}: the European and Nordic system context, the power-to-heat conversion assets, and the thermal storage that shifts their output in time. The material is covered because the dispatch model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 DH operator model} is parameterized directly on these technology characteristics; ramp rates, minimum loads, efficiencies and storage capacity decide which markets each asset can physically serve. The section is used to establish the merit-order roles the model encodes: waste-to-energy as inflexible base-load, heat pumps as slow mid-merit assets, electrode boilers as the fast flexibility asset, and the accumulator as the buffer that decouples their dispatch from instantaneous heat demand. It connects forward to \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}, which explains why most of a physically capable fleet remains outside the reserve markets, and to the Norwegian system description in \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context}.

\subsubsection{European and Nordic Overview}
\label{2-subsec:2.2.1 eu and nordic overview}

District heating covers a significant, but unevenly distributed, share of building heat demand across Europe. Throughout this thesis, the penetration metric is the share of national building heat demand met by district heating. The most comprehensive cross-country baseline on this metric is close to a decade old, but because district heating penetration is governed by long-lived network infrastructure, it shifts slowly. According to that study, some countries have almost no district heating, while implementation rates around and above $50\%$ appear in Iceland, Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland and Russia; across the European Union as a whole district heating supplied roughly $13\%$ of building heat use in 2014, and roughly $8\%$ worldwide \cite{werner_international_2017}. A more recent review of the Danish sector puts district heating at $56\%$ of Danish building heat demand at the end of the 2010s \cite{johansen_something_2022}. The sector is undergoing a generational transition towards lower supply temperatures and greater integration of renewable and waste-incineration sources. The fourth-generation (4GDH) concept that frames this shift was defined over a decade ago \cite{lund_4th_2014}. A 2024 comprehensive survey extends the trajectory to a fifth generation (5GDH) of near-ambient-temperature networks, which is still evolving alongside 4GDH rather than replacing it, and remains confined to early pilot projects \cite{dang_fifth_2024}. This thesis concerns existing large-scale DH infrastructure operating at conventional supply temperatures; 4GDH and 5GDH developments are noted as background context but do not form part of the analysis. 

Denmark occupies a disproportionately large share of the DH flexibility literature relative to its system scale, reflecting its early large-scale P2H deployment and a long tradition of integrating district heating with wind power. The Danish sector rests on statutory advantages that Norway does not share: mandatory connection for new development, a non-profit pricing principle, and long-term municipal financing and ownership that align operator incentives with broader energy-system objectives \cite{johansen_something_2022}. Greater Copenhagen, where large-scale heat pumps have been integrated into the district heating network, has become a recurring technical benchmark for DH-integrated P2H flexibility \cite{bach_integration_2016}. Many of the barrier typologies and analytical frameworks applied in this chapter were developed in the Danish context, and are transferred here with adjustment for Norwegian structural conditions. The Danish fleet scale keeps the comparison honest: 136 electric boilers totaling 1,860 MW in 2024, with installed capacity roughly doubled between 2019 and 2023 \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}.

Sweden represents a comparatively under-examined Nordic case. Swedish district heating covered around $50\%$ or more of national building heat use as of 2014, with Stockholm among the world's major district heating systems \cite{werner_international_2017}. The SthlmFlex project demonstrated a local flexibility market architecture within the Stockholm system, initiated jointly by Svenska kraftnät and the regional DSOs Vattenfall Eldistribution and Ellevio to address congestion during the winter season \cite{nyman_how_2026}. The pilot operated from winter 2020/2021 onward and has since concluded with very low market liquidity, with Ellevio stating that short-term local flexibility markets are not a suitable congestion-management tool for Stockholm in the near term \cite{scrocca_local_2026}. It nevertheless remains the closest Nordic institutional reference for multi-DSO/TSO flexibility coordination. A recent sector-coupling optimisation for the Swedish DH sector is structurally analogous to this thesis's Norwegian question \cite{atabaki_optimising_2026}, and the only available qualitative study of Swedish DH-flexibility actors finds that weak institutional incentives and profit-oriented ownership structures limit flexibility provision despite adequate technical capacity \cite{fernqvist_district_2023}. Sweden's combination of large urban DH systems, growing wind integration and emerging local flexibility market structures makes it the more instructive Nordic comparator for the analysis in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.5 cross-country comparison}, whereas Denmark provides the primary technical benchmark throughout \autoref{2-subsec:2.2 DH as flexibility} and \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}.

Norway presents a structurally distinct profile within the Nordic group. National district heating penetration is approximately $10\%$ of national building heat demand \cite{SINTEF_energy_2023}, significantly lower than Danish or Swedish levels \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. The sector is concentrated in three cities with three separate operators: Oslo (Hafslund Celsio), Trondheim (Statkraft Varme), and Bergen (BKK Varme).

The paper attributes this gap to historical lock-in rather than to differences in operator profitability: Norway's legacy of abundant, historically cheap electricity and a direct-electric heating culture left it with few water-borne distribution systems, so the country now combines the lowest overall DH penetration in the Nordic region with the highest power-to-heat share of district heating generation \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. Norwegian DH operators must consequently compete against a low-cost electricity baseline and lack the scale, cooperative ownership structures, and policy support that sustain Danish and Swedish operators. This structural position shapes both the barrier analysis in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} and the case-selection rationale in \autoref{3-subsec:3.1 research design}.

\subsubsection{Power-to-Heat Technologies}
\label{2-subsec:2.2.2 P2H technologies}

Power-to-heat (P2H) technologies are the controllable electrical loads that couple the electricity system to the district heating network: electric boilers and large-scale compression heat pumps convert purchased electricity into heat and can therefore be dispatched against electricity price signals and reserve-market activation windows \cite{bloes_power--heat_2018}, \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}. A third heat source, combined heat and power (CHP) plants, including waste-to-energy (WtE) incineration facilities, couples the two carriers in reverse, generating electricity as a by-product of heat production rather than consuming electricity \cite{beiron_combined_2020}. Cogeneration is nonetheless rare in Norway: the CHP share of Norwegian district heat production was 1.5 percent in 2013, against 73 percent in Denmark and Finland \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}.

Large-scale compression heat pumps extract thermal energy from a low-grade source, such as seawater, groundwater, or wastewater, and upgrade it to district heating supply temperature. The lower electrical input per unit of thermal output, relative to electrode boilers, reduces the heat pump's price exposure. Its contribution to flexibility is constrained more by the economics and mechanical limits of cycling than by ramp rate: as the temperature lift between source and supply widens, COP deteriorates, and frequent start-stop operation accelerates compressor wear. So while electrode boilers can provide instantaneous up- and down-regulation, large-scale heat pumps have generally not been designed for frequent flexible regulation \cite{david_heat_2017}. A European survey of large-scale district heating heat pumps found the newest units running continuously at 7000 to 8000 hours per year and supplying part of the base load, others operating seasonally at 4000 to 7000 hours, and very few dispatching intermittently on the electricity price, though the survey notes that operating-hour data was sparse and the role of these units in their networks could not be clearly established \cite{david_heat_2017}. Electric boilers respond instantaneously, within 30 seconds, whereas large-scale heat pumps carry some lead time before becoming fully operational, so the two technologies suit different segments of the reserve markets rather than competing for the same products \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}. The IEA HPT Annex 57 final report quantifies this tiering through the Sdr. Felding pilot in Denmark, in which a $1.3 MW$ heat pump records a start-up time of approximately seven minutes and a turn-down time of around four minutes, placing heat-pump participation in the aFRR window rather than the faster, seconds-scale windows accessible to electrode boilers, namely the Frequency Containment Reserve (FCR) and Fast Frequency Reserve (FFR) \cite{v_pedersen_annex_2024}.

Combined heat and power plants (CHP), including waste-to-energy (WtE) incineration facilities, occupy a structurally different position within the DH flexibility stack. Rather than being dispatchable against an electricity price signal, they operate in a heat-led mode. Thermal output is the primary production target, driven by waste throughput requirements or heat demand contracts, and electricity is generated as a by-product at a fixed or minimally adjustable heat-to-power ratio \cite{beiron_combined_2020}. Flexibility provision from CHP and WtE is accordingly indirect, achieved through two mechanisms: adjusting the heat-to-power extraction ratio within the operating range of the turbine cycle, and buffering variable heat production against demand using thermal storage to decouple generation from consumption in time \cite{wang_investigation_2019}. Catalogue minimum loads bound the inflexibility: $20\%$ for waste-fired CHP and $70\%$ for waste-fired heat-only boilers, with warm and cold starts of 8 and 12 hours respectively \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}. 

Waste incineration supplies roughly half of all Norwegian district heating, the highest share amongst the Nordic countries \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. In Oslo, Hafslund Celsio's Klemetsrud WtE plant represents the dominant base-load source in the system merit order, operating continuously with thermal output driven by waste-incineration throughput rather than electricity market signals. This heat-led dispatch logic means that WtE capacity constrains the flexibility stack rather than contributing to it directly. Its inflexibility is compensated by the electrode boiler, heat-pump capacity and accumulator-tank storage.

\subsubsection{Thermal Energy Storage}
\label{2-subsec:2.2.3 thermal storage}

The dedicated thermal storage identified in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions} as the supply-side FTS buffer is what converts the power-flexibility action into a resource that can be shifted across time. It spans a wide envelope of power and duration, from short-duration accumulators suited to day-ahead arbitrage to seasonal stores that decouple summer collection from winter demand. Where an asset sits on that envelope fixes which flexibility products it can bid into, following the buffer-parameter logic of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}.

\utfyll{What is the daily heat delivery of Oslo's district heating compared to the accumulation tank size aka How long can the tank substitute for heat production?}

Thermal accumulator tanks store pressurised hot water at district heating supply temperature inside insulated vessels. They are inexpensive among energy storage options: hot-water tanks cost between 50 and 100 times less per unit of stored energy than electrical storage \cite{hennessy_flexibility_2019}. Their round-trip thermal efficiency is high: the Danish Energy Agency technology catalogue reports a full-cycle round-trip efficiency of $98\%$ (uncertainty band $96-99\%$) and a standby loss of about $0.2\%$ per day for large-scale steel accumulator tanks \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}. 

As short-duration, high-power assets, accumulators charge and discharge at rates approaching the connected P2H plant capacity over timescales of hours, making them directly usable for spot-market arbitrage and for buffering the heat produced or displaced during an mFRR activation. The connected electrode boiler can deliver across the full activation window rather than only as long as the heat demand profile permits. Oslo's Haraldrud facility, commissioned in October 2023, is among Norway's largest operational DH accumulators, with an ideal storage capacity of approximately $550 MWh$ and a momentary charge and discharge rate of up to about $100 MW$ (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026)\avklar{confirm: 550 MWh / 100 MW against the operator dataset; open: whether the Ulven 350 MWh day-store reported elsewhere is a second, separate storage}. It is not alone at this scale: Statkraft's Heimdal plant in Trondheim operates a tank of roughly $350 MWh$ \cite{SINTEF_energy_2023}.

Accumulator tanks are common in Danish, Finnish, and Swedish district heating but have historically been more scarce in Norway \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. The Danish tank fleet, roughly 875,000 m\textsuperscript{3} across 284 plants with about 50 GWh usable, also illustrates how enablers drive storage adoption: much of it was originally sized for operation against a since-abolished three-part electricity tariff, and the fleet's role shifted when that enabler changed \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}. Outside Oslo, large-scale accumulator capacity in the Norwegian systems is limited, a technical asymmetry treated as a scope boundary in \autoref{3-subsec:3.7}.

Seasonal thermal energy storage technologies, principally pit thermal energy storage (PTES) and borehole thermal energy storage (BTES), operate on charge-discharge cycles of weeks to months, enabling the temporal decoupling of summer heat collection or waste-heat availability from winter heating demand. The four canonical underground storage typologies (tank, pit, borehole and aquifer) differ substantially in energy density: tank TES holds roughly $60-80\frac{kWh}{m^3}$, gravel-water PTES roughly $30-50\frac{kWh}{m^3}$, BTES around $15-30\frac{kWh}{m^3}$, and ATES around $30-40\frac{kWh}{m^3}$ \cite{dahash_advances_2019}. Round-trip efficiencies are operationally lower than for short-duration accumulators and depend strongly on the storage utilisation cycle: a five-year-average measured storage efficiency of $90.1\%$ has been reported for the Dronninglund PTES at a high utilisation cycle of 2.16, against approximately $66\%$ for the Marstal PTES operated at a cycle close to 1.0 \cite{pan_long-term_2022}. Power ratings are modest relative to storage volume, limiting participation in balancing markets. Denmark has deployed large-scale PTES extensively, with solar-coupled installations such as Vojens ($200\,000\ m^3$) and Dronninglund ($60\,000\ m^3$) \cite{dahash_advances_2019}, \cite{pan_long-term_2022}.

The levelised cost of stored heat at the $100\,000\ m^3$ scale runs from about $130\frac{\euro}{MWh}$ for an accumulation tank to about $160\frac{\euro}{MWh}$ for the shallow pit, under European cost conditions \cite{dahash_techno-economic_2021}. In Norway, seasonal DH storage remains primarily at the demonstration stage. A neighbourhood-scale case study at Furuset in Oslo finds that district heating alone reduces peak electricity demand by approximately $28\%$ relative to direct electric heating, while adding borehole seasonal thermal storage reduces peak district heating demand by a further $31\%$ and cuts $CO_2$ emissions by $20\%$ below the direct-electric-heating baseline \cite{kauko_assessing_2022}. The seasonal-storage configuration is not cost-saving under baseline conditions, however: it carries the lowest operational cost but the highest capital cost, leaving total system cost approximately $3\%$ above the electric-heating reference. It becomes competitive only when winter electricity prices double or when available grid capacity is reduced by more than $9 MW$ below the site's baseline of $22.6 MW$, a capacity the authors describe as unusually high and present as a special case rather than a representative neighborhood. These figures are therefore indicative at neighborhood scale and do not transfer directly to system scale. 

The commercial case for seasonal storage splits by value channel. Through the market channels available in the NO1 price environment the constraint stands: unlike accumulators, PTES and BTES assets cannot recover capital cost through mFRR activation or short-horizon arbitrage alone, positioning them primarily as socio-economic assets in the valuation framework of \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}. A Norwegian residential study quantifies this dynamic under the Norwegian monthly-peak grid tariff structure: co-optimizing seasonal thermal storage with the monthly-peak capacity tariff achieves a $4.3\%$ total-cost reduction relative to a tariff-only baseline, whereas a storage-only strategy that ignores the tariff signal produces a $69\%$ higher annual cost than the co-optimized case \cite{thorvaldsen_long-term_2024}. The mechanism transfers in direction to the DH operator setting: tariff-unaware storage operation systematically under-performs, and the value of storage depends on accessible flexibility products and tariff signals rather than on stored capacity alone. Through the fuel-displacement channel the direction reverses: a cost-minimizing analysis of seasonal storage in a Norwegian district heating system, charging on otherwise-vented excess heat and displacing expensive peaking fuel, finds positive net present value with discounted paybacks of 5 to 20 years across price scenarios \cite{tim_maarten_riesen_techno-economic_2025}. The direction transfers; the magnitudes do not, since that analysis contains no operator perimeter, no market-access terms and no capacity charge. Its labeling of a system-cost saving as (business-economically profitable) is itself an instance of the perspective conflation that \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} formalizes.

\illu{Storage typology plot (power in MW on y-axis, duration in time on x-axis) - accumulator tank, BTES, PTES, pipeline storage.}

The value attributed to any thermal storage asset is bounded by the flexibility products available for the stored energy to be sold into, the enabler layer of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}. A review of power-to-heat and thermal storage participation in electricity markets, focused on the Finnish system, finds that the benefit a thermal store draws from the day-ahead and intraday markets rests largely on charging at times of lower electricity prices and discharging when prices are higher, and concludes that power-to-heat coupled with thermal storage needs additional income, for example from ancillary service markets, to secure profitable operation \cite{jaanto_thermal_2023}. This finding applies directly to the Norwegian DH context. Without a flexibility product that compensates the district heating operator for system services beyond mFRR capacity payments, the commercial value of storage investment is anchored on NO1 spot-price variance. \utsett{Price-spread measurement/calculation in Section 2.1.2. - from Nordpool spot price analysis} The societal value of the same storage assets, in the form of avoided grid reinforcement, freed balancing capacity, and system-price suppression, accrues to Elvia, Statnett, and consumers rather than to the operator. The relationship between storage value and market design architecture is therefore a structural feature of the commercial-/socio-economic gap, not a transient market condition.

\subsection{Barriers to Flexibility in District Heating}
\label{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}

This section explains why the flexibility resource documented in \autoref{2-subsec:2.2 DH as flexibility} earns so little of the value it creates: it catalogues the barriers that separate the operator's economic return on flexibility provision from its system-level societal value. The catalogue is used twice downstream. The dispatch model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 DH operator model} encodes the economic barriers as tariff and market-access terms, and the gap attribution in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} assigns the measured gap across the barrier categories established here. The corpus on district heating flexibility converges on a recurring set of mechanisms spanning tariff design, market-product gaps, regulatory frictions, and institutional arrangements. The most systematic ordering comes from \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}, which categorises them into a taxonomy of forty barriers grouped into nine categories: operational signalling, investment, permitting, ownership, district-energy technology conditions, grid access, physical environment, bounded rationality, and acceptance. This thesis collapses those nine categories into the four barrier types that organise the subsections below, mapping district-energy technology conditions, physical environment, and grid access onto technical barriers, \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers}, operational signalling and investment onto economic and market barriers, \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, permitting and ownership onto regulatory and policy barriers, \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}, and bounded rationality and acceptance onto institutional barriers, \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.4 institutional barriers}. The four-way collapse is this thesis's own analytical construction and not a grouping \cite{sneum_barriers_2021} proposes: that study's contribution is the forty barriers and the nine categories holding them, and its conclusion argues explicitly for a finer sub-division of categories than the regulation-and-economy split common in the literature it reviews. One of the four assignments carries a qualification. The grid access category is internally heterogeneous, containing a grid-capacity barrier that is technical, a grid-connection-cost barrier that is economic in substance, and a grid-code barrier that is regulatory in substance. It is placed under technical barriers here because network capacity, rather than connection pricing or connection rules, is the binding constraint in the Oslo case; the cost and rule dimensions of the category are acknowledged but not developed separately below. \utsett{Grid-access cost (Sneum \#27) and grid-code (\#28) content is acknowledged in \S2.3 but not treated in \S2.3.2/\S2.3.3 - decide whether to add a sentence to each or leave the narrowing as stated.} \cite{sneum_barriers_2021} identifies the absence of a signal-providing scheme as the single barrier sufficient on its own to suppress flexibility, and finds that barriers of national origin dominate the taxonomy, which places the primary responsibility for removing them with national policy-makers rather than with the individual operators. In the resource-and-enabler terms of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}, every barrier in this taxonomy is a missing or adverse \textit{enabler}. The flexibility resource exists physically in the operator's electrode boilers, heat pumps, and accumulator, but markets, reserve products, and network tariffs either fail to reward its activation or actively penalize it. Degefa et al. and Sæle et al. make this split explicit, classifying markets, regulation, and tariffs as enablers separate from the flexibility resource itself \cite{degefa_comprehensive_2021}, \cite{saele_understanding_2023}. The four barrier types below are therefore a catalogue of the enablers that are absent or misaligned in the Norwegian case. \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.5 cross-country comparison} then sets the Norwegian condition under each barrier type against Denmark and Sweden, the two Nordic systems against which Norwegian district heating is most often measured.

%--------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Technical Barriers}
\label{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers}

The most visible technical constraint on district heating flexibility provision in Norway is the limited deployment of large-scale thermal storage. The Haraldrud accumulator, commissioned in October 2023, is Oslo's principal dedicated DH storage asset. Large-scale accumulator capacity elsewhere in the Norwegian system remains limited, confining many operators to within-hour load modulation rather than the multi-hour to day-ahead dispatch optimisation that storage enables \cite{hennessy_flexibility_2019}, \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. Hafslund Celsio's Oslo system, with both a large P2H fleet and dedicated storage, represents a ceiling of Norwegian DH flexibility potential rather than a median, so findings from the Oslo case do not transfer directly onto other Norwegian operators without adjustments in their physical flexibility assets. 

New P2H capability additions face grid connection constraints that delay deployment and cap installed capacity below the economically optimal level. The broad electrification push across sectors has loaded distribution-grid networks and expanded connection queues. In Oslo, applications for roughly $1000MW$ of new electricity load stand against only about $200MW$ of reserved grid capacity, so a DH operator seeking to add electrode boiler capacity may face multi-year connection timelines and capacity restrictions \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. This caps the P2H capacity an operator can install below economically optimal levels, independent of the downstream market-access question.
The queue impact on power-to-heat is operator-confirmed: new electric-boiler connections above 5 MW route into the connection queue, the operator installs boilers from 10 MW upward, and its smallest planned boiler is deliberately below 5 MW to stay outside the queue (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). The statutory machinery is the maturity assessment for connection requests of 1 MW and above, with reserved capacity for smaller customers and a unilateral downgrade provision, in the network-regulation code \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2019}.

Network thermal dynamics, including supply temperature frameworks, pipe thermal inertia, and distribution-side control constraints, impose physical limits on the ramp rates achievable through network-level flexibility activation. These are noted as a boundary condition of the economic feasibility in \autoref{3-subsec:3.7}, but are not subject to detailed analysis in this thesis \cite{vandermeulen_controlling_2018}, \cite{guelpa_demand_2021}.

Across the literature, technical barriers are consistently treated as the most tractable of the four barrier types. Of the forty barriers categorized by \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}, only two concern thermal storage, which the author characterises as a "no-regrets technology", often seen as the default go-to investment to increase system performance. The bulk of the gap lies in the economic, regulatory, and institutional categories that follow \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}.


%-------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Economic and Market Barriers}
\label{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}

The single most cited economic barrier in the Norwegian DH flexibility corpus is the capacity charge, the monthly peak-load capacity component of Elvia's distribution grid tariff. For the business classes with annual consumption above 100{,}000 kWh, in which a district heating operator of Hafslund Celsio's scale sits, the capacity charge is assessed on the single highest measured power draw within each calendar month, regardless of when that peak occurs or what system conditions prevailed at the time \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}. The regulation permits either a single-maximum or an averaged-peaks settlement basis, and the large-customer schedules use the single-maximum form \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}, \cite{langset_national_2021}, \utsett{(RME fact sheets - RME - 2020/2022)}. The regulator itself has acknowledged that this settlement basis can yield a marginal network cost that does not reflect actual network costs, when the monthly peak falls in hours with available capacity \cite{eriksen_proposed_2022}, \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}.  The Norwegian Energy Regulatory Authority (RME) put that basis in question while consulting on a capacity-based tariff for low-voltage customers: it proposed a daily rather than a monthly peak for that class, suggested that larger commercial customers also be provided with a daily-peak basis from 2021, and asked respondents whether the monthly peak should still be used in settlement for certain customers at all \cite{eriksen_proposed_2022}. The consultation's remit was the low-voltage distribution grid, so it is not authority for the high-voltage business schedule under which a district heating operator of Hafslund Celsio's scale is billed, where the monthly-peak basis set out above still applies. \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}, \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}. For a district heating operator that uses electric boilers to store thermal energy during low-price hours, any load increase triggers a new monthly peak and incurs a capacity charge proportional to its magnitude. This holds even when the same load increase reduces the aggregate system peak and defers grid reinforcement at the network level. The tariff thereby penalizes precisely the flexibility behavior it should incentivize. The observation is not an operator-side complaint alone: Statnett's own 2018 Nordic flexibility outlook names the mechanism, noting that the capacity charge can prevent electric boilers from consuming even at very low power prices \cite{holmefjord_fleksibilitet_2018}, seven years before the operator-commissioned THEMA assessment. For a Norwegian DH plant modelled on Oslo load data, the electric-boiler share of annual heat generation varies from $7\%$ to $17\%$ under a hydropower-dominated price pattern and from $2\%$ to $13\%$ under a thermal-dominated one, driven by the grid tariff structure applied, before any market-access barrier is considered \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}. The mechanism generalizes beyond Norway, though the Norwegian form is unusually severe. For the Danish system, conventional grid tariffs can "obliterate the price signals from the wholesale electricity market" \cite{skytte_design_2017}. In that 2014 Danish case, an electric boiler is the cheapest heat source in only about $6\%$ of annual hours, a share that rises to roughly $74\%$ under a flat capacity tariff. The Norwegian capacity charge is a monthly-peak capacity charge rather than the volumetric per-kWh structure analyzed there. The mechanism therefore differs in form and is arguably stronger: the monthly-peak settlement injects intra-month decision uncertainty on top of the constant wedge a volumetric tariff imposes. The direction of the distortion is nonetheless identical. Across the Nordic system, volumetric grid tariffs suppress a substantial volume of cross-sector flexibility investment: a revenue-neutral capacity tariff would unlock on the order of a $20\%$ increase in power-to-heat capacity and a $7\%$ increase in storage \cite{bergaentzle_cross-sector_2022}. Hafslund Celsio is connected at distribution level and faces Elvia's capacity charge on top of the transmission tariff modeled in that study, so the suppression of Oslo-specific flexibility value may be larger still. A comparable conclusion holds at plant level: a capacity-component tariff effectively eliminates electric-boiler operation in Norway and Finland, because a few low-price boiler hours trigger a monthly charge that outweighs the fuel saving \cite{sneum_economic_2018}. 

The interruptible (utkoblbar) tariff is the standard escape from the capacity charge, but it is not a regulated entitlement. The dedicated utkoblbar chapter of the tariff regulation was deleted on 1 July 2012, and the discount has since been a discretionary bilateral arrangement the DSO may offer where network conditions warrant it \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}, \cite{langset_national_2021}, \utsett{(RME fact sheets - RME - 2020/2022)}. In practice it is the sector norm: most Norwegian district heating companies run their electric boilers on interruptible terms \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}, \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fakta_nodate}, and the Oslo incumbent's entire boiler fleet operates on such terms at an effective volumetric rate of approximately 5 øre/kWh, with the disconnection option barely exercised over five years because price-based self-curtailment moves the boilers off the grid before the DSO needs to call (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). The capacity-charge barrier is therefore conditional rather than absolute. It binds firm-connection consumers and operators without interruptible terms \cite{sneum_economic_2018}, and it binds capacity additions, which face the successor regime of connection on condition (tilknytning på vilkår), where curtailment is a condition of connection and carries no compensation \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2019}. For the incumbent's legacy interruptible connections it is largely neutralized. Danish distribution system operators institutionalise the same arrangement from the network side, offering electric boilers limited grid access with a negotiable minimum load \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}.

This does not make the capacity charge a barrier simply to be abolished. Capacity-charge designs are not inherently barrier-forming. Removing the capacity component while retaining a dynamic energy signal lets assets with high thermal mass pre-heat ahead of high-price periods, which simply shifts peaks rather than reducing them \cite{wilczynski_assessment_2023}. Some capacity signal is therefore needed for grid-peak suppression. The barrier lies in the specific peak-based monthly structure of the Norwegian capacity charge, not in capacity pricing. The design flaw is in which peak the tariff targets. Norway already runs a capacity tariff designed not to distort at another voltage level: the transmission and regional-network capacity tariff settles on reference hours that, by design, shall so far as possible not be predictable to the customer, which removes the incentive to shave the settlement peak itself \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}, \cite{langset_national_2021}, \utsett{(RME fact sheets - RME - 2020/2022)}. The contrast sharpens the critique: the distortion is a distribution-level design choice, not an inherent property of capacity pricing. On a real Oslo case, a capacity-based tariff alone incentivizes individual peak reduction without coordinating the coincident peak that actually drives grid capacity needs \cite{askeland_activating_2021}. Closing the gap between individual and coincident peak behavior therefore requires a complementary local capacity-trading mechanism that the Norwegian system currently lacks. Oslo demand-response data corroborate this: the individual-peak, ex-post settlement of the capacity charge performs worst for substation-level peak reduction, even as it performs best at the individual-customer level \cite{hofmann_grid_2025}. The tariff nonetheless materially governs storage-coupled dispatch. Co-optimizing a Norwegian building's seasonal-storage operation against the monthly measured-peak tariff, by absorbing cheap summer electricity to lower the costlier winter peak charge, reduces annual operating cost by $4.3\%$ and the monthly-peak tariff bill by $23.9\%$ relative to operation without storage \cite{thorvaldsen_long-term_2024}. The residential scale of these cases does not transfer numerically to the district heating operator. The mechanism, however, applies directly to Hafslund Celsio's electrode-boiler and accumulator dispatch under the capacity charge. Finally, the capacity charge does not bind all assets equally. A monthly-maximum load-demand tariff of the Oslo type is the worst tariff for electric-boiler deployment but the best for heat pumps: base load heat-pump operation spreads the capacity charge across many hours, whereas intermittent boiler dispatch concentrates it into a few. Tariff reform therefore releases more flexibility from electrode boilers than from heat pumps, the latter remaining base load regardless of tariff design unless a competing low-cost heat source emerges \cite{sandberg_energy_2019}, \cite{johannsen_incentivising_2021}.

Until December 2025 the mFRR minimum bid threshold functioned as a structural exclusion mechanism, converting the cost asymmetry above into a permanent revenue gap. At $10 MW$ in NO2, NO4 and NO5 and $5 MW$ in NO1 and NO3 \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}, the floor placed reserve-market revenue out of reach for all but the largest aggregated capacity. As established in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power}, the cut of the mFRR bid floor to $1 MW$ with effect from 2 December 2025 removes the bid-size barrier as such\avklar{Statnett primary for the 2 December 2025 cut pending}. A single Hafslund Celsio electrode-boiler site now qualifies on scale alone. What remains is a revenue gap of a different origin. That gap is not about bid size. Modelling of power-to-heat bidding into a reserve capacity market finds the economics governed by the composition of the competing heat production: the maximum average bidding price an operator can offer before business-as-usual operation is cheaper falls from about USD 150 per MW of electrical capacity in a heat-only-boiler system to between USD 60 and USD 80 where a biomass CHP unit supplies base load, while the balancing potential itself stays bounded by the installed power-to-heat capacity and the production strategy of the system; the study concludes that policies rewarding power-balancing services, alongside investment support for power-to-heat capacity and heat storage, are needed to overcome those limits \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}. Norwegian practitioner accounts point the same way, attributing under-participation to a complex flexibility value chain, capacity-charge exposure, and a competence and digitalisation gap as trading roles emerge in district heating control rooms \cite{gronli_hvor_2025} \avklar{(operator-adjacent commentary, not peer-reviewed). The prequalification-cost, metering/baseline and aggregation-framework barriers previously asserted here need a source that actually makes them --- neither Monie (2022) nor Grønli (2025) does. Statnett's mFRR prequalification requirements or an RME/NVE document would be the right anchor; restore the claim only once one is in hand.} 
The reserve products also differ in how well district heating assets match them, so participation turns on asset capability rather than scale alone. Fast-ramping electrode boilers can serve the frequency-containment products, whereas the slower heat-pump units are confined to the automatic restoration (aFRR) and mFRR windows that respond on a timescale of minutes \cite{javanshir_operation_2022}, \cite{boldrini_role_2022}. For the large incumbent, the barrier has moved from bid size to allocated access. Statnett announced on 6 March 2026, and implemented on 23 April 2026, a cap on the volume of regulating capacity that may be prequalified in grids below 110 kV \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. Norway is divided into 92 capacity areas (kapasitetsområder), each capped at 3 MW of upward and 3 MW of downward regulation counted across FCR, aFRR and mFRR combined \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The cap covers the regional grid below 110 kV and the distribution grid connected beneath it, and it binds already-prequalified units as well as new applications. Statnett gives observability as the rationale: most of the grid below 110 kV lies outside its network model, leaving the system operator without a view of flow, capacity, outages or operating state, and therefore unable to assess congestion risk from reserves it cannot see. Until 31 December 2026, 1 MW of each area's 3 MW is reserved for flexibility groups, aggregations of sub-1 MW resources bidding as units of 1 to 5 MW. NO1 holds 25 capacity areas and 75 MW in total, of which the Oslo and Akershus group covers 11 areas and 33 MW \cite{statnett_innforing_2026} - the ceiling that binds the Oslo district heating system. Against it the operator reports approximately 29 MW of connected reserve capacity and more than 200 MW applied for (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). Statnett processes applications in the order received, treats clearance with the relevant distribution system operator as part of each case, and states that processing must be expected to run beyond normal deadlines \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The cap arrived while the entry threshold fell to 1 MW, so the regime widened eligibility and rationed allocation at the same time.

The reserve product exclusion is compounded by the absence of any market instrument through which district heating operators can be compensated for system services that fall outside the mFRR activation window. This is a missing-market problem. The heat market clears first and is myopic to its interactions with the electricity market, so the inter-temporal flexibility of a district heating network coupled with CHPs and heat pumps could be modelled as a virtual electricity storage. Generalising financial and physical storage rights, so that heat systems can quantify and offer that flexibility to the power system, is identified as a promising direction for market design rather than an existing product \cite{mitridati_heat_2020}. Without a bilateral flexibility contract or a purpose-designed capacity product, these services go effectively uncompensated. One narrow capacity payment exists: RKOM, the seasonal balancing-capacity option market for mFRR, pays for a guarantee of upward bids in the October to April season, and district heating participation in it is negligible on current evidence \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}, \cite{langset_national_2021}, 
\utsett{Source for RME fact sheets - RME - 2020/2022} 
Beyond that seasonal upward-bid guarantee, no Norwegian market mechanism offers a capacity payment for DH-side load flexibility besides the mFRR activation payment itself. Finnish work on thermal storage reaches the same conclusion for its own market, noting that remuneration for providing flexibility, or additional compensation for the network-balancing service that power-to-heat delivers, would have to be introduced as a new incentive \cite{jaanto_thermal_2023}. The THEMA report attributes the gap to specific regulatory-design choices rather than to a textbook market failure: district heating receives no payment for the grid-service value it provides, the regulated price cap is computed monthly rather than hourly so hourly flexibility revenue is blunted, and operators in effect self-finance the electricity price support (strømstøtte) scheme for their customers \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. THEMA's headline estimate of NOK 1-1.6 billion per year is the societal value of converting heat supply to district heating, that is, fuel substitution and peak reduction. This is a broader quantity than the flexibility-specific value this thesis isolates. The flexibility subset is correspondingly smaller, and it is the quantity \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} attributes across the barrier mechanisms.

The NO1 spot price structure sets a ceiling on the arbitrage revenue available to Oslo-based operators, independently of market-access barriers. As established in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power}, NO1 exhibits structurally lower price-duration variance than NO2 and NO5, which compresses the price spread that drives P2H dispatch value. Capacity-based tariffs of the Norwegian type reduce effective electrode-boiler utilization to low levels \cite{sandberg_energy_2019}. Combined with the compressed NO1 price spread established in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power}, this means the flexibility investment cannot recover on commercial returns alone in the Norwegian context. That reinforces the case for complementary reserve-market access rather than sole reliance on spot-arbitrage revenue.

Beyond the regulated price ceiling itself, the price models through which Norwegian operators bill heat do not transmit flexibility value to customers. Conventional volume-based district heating price models are inadequate for a flexible supply-and-demand system, and price-model design directly governs an operator's ability to capture flexibility value \cite{lygnerud_capturing_2024}. The time-differentiated models that have supported flexibility investment elsewhere in the Nordic market are foreclosed in Norway, both by this price-model convention and by the alternative-cost ceiling in the Energy Act §5-5 examined in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}. An operator that reduces system peak load and contributes to lower electricity prices therefore cannot recover that value through the heat price, because the ceiling adjusts downward with the electricity price it helped reduce.

\subsubsection{Regulatory and Policy Barriers}
\label{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}

District heating prices in Norway are governed under §5-5 of the Energy Act. The wording originates in the 1986 District Heating Act and was carried forward into the 1991 Energy Act, so there is no separate law governing district heating \cite{nve_nves_2024}. The provision caps the district heating price at the price of electric heating in the customer's supply area ("prisen for elektrisk oppvarming i vedkommende forsyningsområde"); the statute fixes the price level, not the tariff structure, and specifies no computation period, the monthly settlement being administrative practice \cite{energidepartementet_lov_1991}. The wording has stood unchanged since 2008, while the effektledd sits in secondary legislation, an asymmetry in reform path that \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications} returns to. This cap-style mechanism is designed to protect customers from monopoly pricing, but it structurally suppresses any flexibility-value signal that would otherwise flow through the heat price. The THEMA report identifies this regulatory framework as a primary source of misaligned incentives \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. An operator who invests in flexibility assets that reduce system peak load and enable lower electricity prices at the system level cannot recover that investment through higher heat prices, because the price ceiling adjusts downward with the electricity price the operator helped reduce. The 2024 statistics show the coupling in operation: household district heating averaged about 119 øre/kWh including VAT against an electricity reference of 127.5 øre/kWh, the cap binding at roughly $93\%$ of the benchmark, and sector revenue fell $12\%$ year on year as heat prices tracked electricity prices down \cite{boeng_mindre_2025}. A comparative review of European district heating price-regulation regimes finds that fixing the DH price to an external benchmark may produce heat-sale revenues that fail to cover true costs, threatening operation, maintenance and investment in new technologies, and that a price cap set too low may threaten a system's long-term economic sustainability \cite{odgaarda_review_2020}. The closest Danish quantification is consumer-side rather than operator-side: in Aalborg's 1961-1972 single-family stock the privately optimal level of heat savings falls short of the economically optimal level under the existing tariff design, and a modeled shift to a fully variable tariff narrows that shortfall without closing it \cite{djorup_district_2020}.

The electricity tax applied to P2H consumption constitutes a cost component that reduces the operating margin of electric boilers and heat pumps. The reduced Norwegian rate for district heating producers is 0.60 øre/kWh in both 2025 and 2026, against a standard rate of 7.13 øre/kWh in 2026; eligibility attaches to the legal entity through a fuel-mix test requiring at least half waste, bio, surplus heat and heat pumps, and electric boilers are not a qualifying source \cite{the_norwegian_tax_administration_arsrundskriv_nodate}. The Danish industrial rate is reported at approximately 0.80 øre/kWh
\avklar{the Danish rate and its currency basis (DKK øre vs NOK øre) against a Danish primary source such as tax administration or the DEA technology catalogue. THEMA 2024 contains no Danish material at all, and the corpus' only Nordic electricity-tax tabulation (Sneum and Sandberg 2018, Table 3, 2016 levels: DK 28.9 vs NO 0.5 EUR/MWh) predates the Danish electricity-tax reform, so neither source can carry a comparability claim}. 
The already-low absolute level of the Norwegian reduced rate means that further tax reduction has limited scope as a flexibility policy lever, independently of how the two countries compare. Relative to the market-access architecture of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, the electricity tax is a second-order barrier as an input cost, treated in the commercial model as a fixed operating-cost component rather than a primary gap mechanism. A modelling precedent underscores the point. In a Nordic wind-integration study, the Danish tax on heat produced by electricity, 67.4 €/MWh, was assumed away entirely in order to improve the feasibility of using electric boilers or heat pumps, with the fuel taxes on competing heat production zeroed alongside it so that the removal would not create a tax-driven advantage \cite{meibom_value_2007}. The assumption itself is the evidence: an input-side cost wedge on power-to-heat can be large enough that removing it is treated as a precondition for assessing the technology at all. In the Norwegian case the input-side wedge is small, on the order of one percent of the marginal cost of power-to-heat; the binding constraints sit in the market-access architecture analysed in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}. The tax matters instead through a second channel: successive cuts in the standard rate, from 16.13 øre/kWh in 2020 to 7.13 in 2026, lower the price of electric heating and thereby mechanically lower the §5-5 ceiling on heat revenue; the industry association puts the sector cost near NOK 500 million per year, a figure to be read as an interested party's position \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fakta_nodate}, \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}. The elavgift is second-order as an input cost and first-order as ceiling risk.

A Norway-specific regulatory distortion is the $CO_2$ levy applied to waste incineration. For 2025 the levy is set at NOK 908 per tonne of $CO_2$ for non-quota-liable emissions and NOK 182 per tonne for quota-liable (EU ETS) emissions. For 2026 the non-quota rate falls to NOK 848 per tonne, while ETS-covered plants are exempted \cite{noauthor_stortingsvedtak_2024}. This levy has no direct counterpart in Denmark or Sweden. It directly affects the operating economics of WtE-anchored DH systems such as Hafslund Celsio's Klemetsrud plant, by raising the marginal cost of the dominant heat source in the merit order. The applicable rate depends on Klemetsrud's quota status, and the planned carbon capture at the plant (operational from about 2029) would exempt the captured share entirely. A rising WtE cost shifts the dispatch balance toward electric boilers and heat pumps, potentially increasing the economic attractiveness of P2H flexibility. It simultaneously compresses operator margins and may reduce the financial headroom for the capital investment that flexibility requires. The net effect on the commercial/socio-economic gap through this channel is directionally ambiguous, and is flagged as an open analytical question in \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work}.

The extension of the Norway Price (Norgespris) scheme to district heating customers represents a compounding policy barrier. The Norgespris programme, originally designed to cap residential electricity bills, was extended to district heating customers with effect from 1 October 2025 and is state-funded from that date; the statutory reference price is 40 øre/kWh excluding VAT (50 øre/kWh including VAT), settled as a two-way contract for difference against the monthly zonal spot price, for households only and mutually exclusive with strømstøtte \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2025}, \cite{nve_norgespris_2026}. The monthly settlement basis means that no hourly price signal reaches district heating bills, by construction. Prior to the extension the operator self-financed the strømstøtte equivalent for its customers. Hafslund Celsio's audited accounts record a district heating result after tax of NOK $-52$ million in 2025 and, once the accounting gain on the sale of Hafslund Fiber AS is stripped out, NOK $-133$ million in 2024 \cite{hafslund_arsrapport_nodate}. The revenue coupling that drives the exposure is documented independently of operator funding: across the sector's roughly fifty concession companies, returns on invested capital correlate with power prices at 77 to 85\%, and sector returns have run near the cost of capital in recent years \cite{jonas_erraia_lonnsomheten_2023}. The loss figures therefore support a value-capture claim, not an unprofitability claim: the gap this thesis quantifies concerns flexibility value the operator cannot capture, not district heating being loss-making. The regime change therefore shifts the burden from the operator's profit \& loss to the state budget. The price-ceiling mechanism in the Energy Act §5-5 \cite{energidepartementet_lov_1991} continues to apply, now indexed to a politically set retail electricity price rather than to a market price. The THEMA NOK 1-1.6 billion conversion-value figure is based on pre-Norgespris conditions. The post-October 2025 regulatory configuration removes one operator-side cost channel while preserving the underlying flexibility-value-capture gap, since the §5-5 ceiling still suppresses any flexibility-driven margin uplift the operator might otherwise realize.

\subsubsection{Institutional Barriers}
\label{2-subsec:2.3.4 institutional barriers}

The most consequential institutional gap is the absence of a formal aggregation framework through which multiple sub-threshold DH operators could pool flexibility capacity to clear mFRR minimum bids collectively \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. The Swedish SthlmFlex pilot, run jointly by Svenska kraftnät, Vattenfall Eldistribution, and Ellevio from winter 2020/2021, demonstrated the technical and institutional architecture of multi-operator local flexibility coordination. It included a reduced mFRR minimum bid of 1 MW for SthlmFlex-linked bids, against the standard Swedish 5 MW threshold \cite{nyman_how_2026}. The pilot has since concluded with very low market liquidity. Ellevio's own assessment is that short-term LFMs are not a viable congestion-management instrument for Stockholm in the near term \cite{scrocca_local_2026}. The Swedish experience therefore demonstrates both the institutional template and its commercial fragility. Even with reduced bid thresholds and explicit TSO-DSO coordination, sellers found revenue insufficient to justify participation; one aggregator estimated LFM income at approximately $10\%$ of ancillary-market income for equivalent effort \cite{nyman_how_2026}. The Danish EcoGrid 2.0 field trial (800 residential customers on Bornholm, 36 real activations) provides complementary peer-reviewed evidence that the LFM mechanism is operationally feasible at scale \cite{heinrich_ecogrid_2020}. The qualification is that flexibility services act as an insurance product against rare-but-consequential overloading events, rather than as a continuous revenue stream. Twenty-five interviews across the parallel CoordiNet Uppland and Skåne LFM pilots corroborate this barrier inventory, finding that the main barriers were the difficulty of understanding LFM design and the extensive market knowledge participation demanded, with existing regulation, manual and time-consuming processes, and insufficient profitability following as secondary barriers, while monetary incentives proved less important as a driver than expected \cite{palm_drivers_2023}. A Norwegian DSO interview study reaches the same verdict from the buyer side. Distribution companies rate local flexibility markets below the prominence they receive in the academic literature, and report that qualitative concerns, most prominently availability, reliability, predictability, and controllability, together with the operational risks that the associated uncertainties create, weigh more heavily in their adoption decisions than cost-benefit calculations \cite{saele_understanding_2023}. In Norway, no equivalent framework exists. What is institutionally locked in is no longer a bid-size threshold but the access regime documented in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, and two features define it. The first is an information asymmetry between the transmission system operator and the grid beneath it: Statnett states that most of the regional grid is not part of its network model, that it has no view of flow, capacity, outages or operating state there, and that the volume caps exist to limit overload risk in a grid it does not monitor \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The second is queue length: applications are processed in the order received, network clearance with the relevant distribution system operator forms part of each case, and Statnett states that processing must be expected to run beyond normal deadlines; the Oslo operator reports applications pending well past that point (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). The two accounts describe one constraint from opposite ends, a system operator managing risk it cannot observe and an asset owner holding capacity it cannot monetise, and the public record does not adjudicate between them. The regulatory-design layer sits underneath with the regulator itself: RME's national reporting concedes that the revenue-cap cost-norm model can favour investment over operational or flexibility measures, and industry consultation rejected the 2019 neutrality fix \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}, \cite{langset_national_2021}, \utsett{(RME fact sheets - RME - 2020/2022)}. The direction of travel points toward earlier system-operator coordination rather than toward a market: Statnett has signaled its intention to merge the prequalification and grid-connection processes, so that network conditions including capacity-area headroom are settled when connection is applied for \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. A systematic review of 38 studies of flexibility in the district energy sector finds that the barriers to exploiting flexibility-enabling technologies in district energy are mostly not technical, and reports proposals for new intermediary roles, such as aggregators pooling small heat pumps to a scale at which flexibility becomes valuable, as part of the remedy \cite{selvakkumaran_how_2021}. Norway's position, where no such intermediary exists, exemplifies the wider pattern.

A second institutional gap is the structural disconnect between the distribution system operator (DSO) and the transmission system operator (TSO) in the attribution of flexibility value. Flexibility provided by Hafslund Celsio's electric boilers reduces peak load on Elvia's distribution network, deferring grid reinforcement investment and accruing value to Elvia and ultimately to grid users through lower tariffs. Simultaneously, mFRR activation of the same assets provides balancing services to Statnett at the transmission level. Neither value flow reaches the district heating operator through any existing mechanism. No Norwegian local flexibility market comparable to the SthlmFlex pilot bridges the distribution-level gap, and at the transmission level the mFRR market remunerates activated balancing energy only, not the distribution-network value that the same dispatch creates, as set out in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}. The result is that district heating flexibility assets operate in a dual-benefit landscape where neither institutional beneficiary compensates the provider.

A third institutional condition specific to the Oslo case is Hafslund Celsio's position within the Hafslund group, which holds assets across hydropower generation and district heating and a regional grid stake (Elvia), under City of Oslo ownership \cite{hafslund_interim_nodate}. In principle, vertical integration of this type could align incentives for flexibility by internalizing the grid value that DH flexibility creates within the same corporate perimeter. In practice, Swedish evidence points to a deep-rooted focus on firm-level profits rather than benefits for the energy system at large \cite{fernqvist_district_2023}, and within a diversified group that logic would operate through group-level investment priorities that need not track the marginal socio-economic value of district heating flexibility. This is presented as a structural condition of the Oslo case study, not as an evaluative judgement on Hafslund's decision-making. Its practical significance for the flexibility gap is an open question that the operator interview in \autoref{3-subsec:3.5 interviews} is positioned to explore.

The comparison reveals a consistent structural pattern. Norway has not updated its regulatory and market architecture to reflect the changed system conditions in which district heating flexibility has become system-valuable. Denmark and Sweden have taken different institutional pathways, and the Nordic comparison shows grid tariff design to be decisive: energy-component-based tariffs increase electric-boiler operation, where capacity-component-based tariffs can eliminate it altogether \cite{sneum_economic_2018}. The Norwegian distance from the comparators on any individual dimension is moderate. The grid tariff is more restrictive than Danish tariff structures but not qualitatively different from Swedish capacity charges, and the mFRR bid floor is now identical across the Nordic markets, although the capacity-area caps on reserve prequalification have no Danish or Swedish counterpart. The distinctive feature of the Norwegian case is that the remaining dimensions stack simultaneously in the direction that suppresses commercial flexibility value, producing a compounding barrier geometry that neither comparator replicates. The Nordic framework-conditions literature identifies Norway as the outlier on district heating penetration rather than on flexibility conditions, explaining that gap mainly by stronger competition from alternative heating options and the low share of installed hydronic systems in Norwegian buildings \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. \subsection{The Norwegian Context}
\label{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context}

Norwegian district heating cannot be modeled as a scaled-down Danish or Swedish case; this section assembles the system characteristics that set it apart. Both valuation perimeters run through these Norwegian specifics. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics} shows how the hydropower water-value pathway reroutes where the socio-economic value of flexibility lands. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.2 policy evolution} traces a decade of incremental regulatory change that has adjusted individual conditions without resolving the mismatch between where flexibility value arises and where its cost lands. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.3 norwegian own case} condenses both threads into the four-condition stack that makes the case irreducible to a Nordic transfer. The section is used to parameterize the model's price and demand environment (\autoref{3-subsec:3.3.2 input data sources}) and to ground the case-selection argument of \autoref{3-subsec:3.1 research design}. It connects the barrier catalogue of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} to the valuation framework of \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} by establishing which barriers bind, and how hard, in the specific system the thesis quantifies.


\subsubsection{Energy System Characteristics}
\label{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics}

Norway's electricity system is dominated by hydropower to a degree unmatched in any other IEA member country, with hydropower accounting for approximately $90\%$ of annual electricity generation and the remainder drawn primarily from wind, supplemented by gas turbines and a small thermal reserve \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023}, \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}. This structural condition creates a marginal generation unit that behaves differently from the thermal or wind-based units that set the price in most European systems. The marginal cost of a Norwegian hydropower reservoir is not a fuel price but an opportunity cost: the shadow value of stored water, determined by expected future scarcity, cross-border export prices, and the scarcity of balancing reserves rather than any single commodity input. As established in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.3 sector coupling}, district heating electric boilers that respond to low spot prices in this system do not primarily displace fuel combustion. They conserve reservoir capacity for higher-value export windows or reserve provision. The socio-economic value pathway, formalized as one of the two valuation perimeters in \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}, runs through hydro reservoir economics and cross-border market design, a characteristic with no direct parallel in fossil-heavy or wind-dominated European systems. The technical envelope of this pathway has been modelled directly \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. In the reference scenario, roughly 149 hours of the year see Norwegian hydropower generation capacity, rather than the interconnector, bind the limit on electricity exported for European balancing. A shift of approximately $25\%$ of individual direct electric heating to district heating removes that binding constraint, leaving free hydropower generation capacity in every hour of the year and making the interconnector the limiting factor throughout. The same shift carries a trade-off, reducing Norway's capacity to absorb surplus European renewable generation, so the net system value is conditional rather than unambiguous. That study is an EnergyPLAN analysis of technical potential: it takes neither investment costs nor the electricity market into account, so it establishes the physical envelope of the pathway rather than its value. Independently of the operator-funded literature, NVE's long-term market analysis identifies district heating electric-boiler coupling as a long-duration flexibility resource, more effective than batteries for multi-hour and multi-day surplus \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023}. The same analysis projects the Norwegian capacity balance tightening toward a deficit in the most stressed hours by 2040.

The Norwegian power grid is divided into five bidding zones, NO1 through NO5, introduced in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power}, within which a single spot price clears each hour via Nord Pool. Transmission constraints between zones produce systematic price differentials during periods of high cross-border flow or intra-Norwegian congestion. Norwegian spot prices exhibit comparatively low price-duration variance, which the Flex4RES assessment attributes to the system's dispatchable hydropower dominance, the property that makes Norway the most price-stable area in the Nordic region \cite{skytte_flexible_2019}. Oslo and the surrounding eastern grid fall within NO1, a zone with negligible local wind or other variable-renewable generation, so its hourly price profile is shaped by hydropower dispatch, interconnector schedules, and demand variation rather than by intermittent renewable production. For district heating operators based in Oslo, the direct consequence is a compressed arbitrage window: the spread between off-peak and on-peak spot prices is narrow, which reduces the commercial return on power-to-heat flexibility before any market-access barrier is applied. This compression is not a contingent feature of current market conditions but a structural property of the zone's generation mix, reinforcing the gap between commercial and socio-economic value on the revenue side. The same compression measured against Danish and Swedish reference zones is set out in the cross-country comparison in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.5 cross-country comparison}.

Norwegian heating demand is structurally concentrated in winter \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023} and skewed toward direct electric resistance heating, a legacy of the Norwegian hydropower build-out between 1950 and 1980, which laid the groundwork for an electricity-based heating resource, and of the relatively low electricity prices that followed and are identified as a main reason for the low penetration of district heating in Norway relative to the other Nordic countries \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. Norwegian electricity demand is correspondingly highly price-inelastic at the short horizon. For southern Norway (the NO1, NO2 and NO5 zones), the short-run price elasticity is estimated at approximately $-0.09$ over 2013--2023, a period that includes a more-than-fourfold increase in average spot prices, and it becomes statistically indistinguishable from zero in the coldest, highest-price periods \cite{idso_short-term_2024}. Temperature explains the bulk of demand variation. The price signal that drives operator-side flexibility decisions does not pass through to consumer demand in any short-run manner relevant to the commercial/socio-economic gap framing. This direct electric heating stock creates a sharp winter peak in electricity demand that intensifies both congestion on the NO1 grid and the scarcity value of generation capacity during peak periods. District heating networks can in principle reduce this peak by converting electricity to stored thermal mass during off-peak hours and supplying building heat from storage during morning and evening demand peaks, a function that corresponds to the virtual electricity storage concept introduced in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.3 sector coupling}. The system-level scale of the available resource has been quantified in a stochastic TIMES-Norway model run to 2050: residential space heating accounts for $83-90\%$ of all cost-efficient demand-response operation, with residential hot water contributing $6-14\%$ and industry only $3-5\%$ \cite{ahang_analysis_2025}. The same model shows demand response smoothing Norwegian electricity prices and reducing regulated-hydropower income by $0-4\%$ across the five price zones, which locates a meaningful share of the socio-economic benefit on actors other than the operator that provides the flexibility. That framing is residential-side and this thesis values flexibility from the operator-supply side, but the dispatch pattern is the same: thermal flexibility shifts winter electric heating load away from peak. In a Balmorel analysis of the future renewable northern European energy system, power-to-heat in flexible district heating and increased transmission line capacity appear to have a larger economic potential than demand response from households, industry and the tertiary sector \cite{kirkerud_role_2021}. The extent to which this peak-shaving function is commercially accessible to district heating operators depends on the reserve products and tariff structures available to them, a question addressed directly in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} and \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 DH operator model}.

The Norwegian district heating sector is small by Nordic standards but geographically concentrated. Aggregate national net production was approximately $7568 GWh$ in 2024, of which about $6810 GWh$ was delivered to consumers \cite{boeng_mindre_2025}. By energy source, 2024 net production was roughly $38\%$ waste incineration, $29\%$ solid biofuel, $12\%$ electric boilers and $11\%$ heat pumps, giving Norway the highest power-to-heat share in the Nordic district heating group \cite{boeng_mindre_2025}, \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}.

\subsubsection{Policy Evolution 2015-2025}
\label{2-subsec:2.4.2 policy evolution}

The policy environment governing Norwegian district heating flexibility has shifted materially over the decade from 2015 to 2025. It accumulated institutional changes that individually addressed specific inefficiencies without resolving the market-design mismatch that is the thesis's central object of study. Tracing this evolution situates the quantitative modeling in \autoref{3-sec:3} against a specific regulatory timeline, and frames the barrier analysis in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} as a snapshot of a system in incremental transition rather than a static condition.

Grid tariff reform entered the regulatory agenda in 2015, when NVE opened its first consultation on replacing the then-current distribution-network tariff with a capacity-based element intended to reduce peak congestion and improve price signals for flexible consumers \cite{andersen_horing_2015}. These consultations ultimately produced the capacity charge structure. For business customers with annual consumption above 100{,}000 kWh, the charge is levied on the single highest hourly power draw within each calendar month; the averaged form, the mean of the three highest daily maxima in the month, applies to the step tariff for business customers below that annual threshold and therefore not to a district heating operator of Hafslund Celsio's scale \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}. As documented in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, this regime functions as a commercial barrier to the specific flexibility behavior it was ostensibly designed to encourage. A district heating operator that raises its electrical load during an mFRR activation or a spot-arbitrage event risks setting a new monthly peak that persists for the full billing period, which makes the net financial case for activation ambiguous even when the spot-price signal is favorable \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}.

A Nordic-incentives analysis covering Norwegian district heating was published in 2018 \cite{sneum_economic_2018}. The Flex4RES consortium's summary report followed in 2019, a Nordic-Baltic cross-country assessment of how sector coupling across heat, gas and transport, together with electricity transmission and generation, can supply flexibility for variable renewables, and of the regulatory barriers standing in its way; district heating is one of its central flexibility routes and Norway one of the countries assessed \cite{skytte_flexible_2019}. The barrier taxonomy later adopted as the scaffolding for \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} is the 2021 taxonomy study \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. These documents pre-date the Haraldrud commissioning and the 2025 Norgespris extension, establishing a reference baseline against which subsequent regulatory developments can be compared.

The institutional capacity to reform these conditions consolidated over the same period. The Storting adopted the Energy Act amendments transposing the European Union's Third Energy Market Package on 22 March 2018, establishing a statutorily independent regulatory authority that cannot be instructed in the exercise of its delegated powers or in the disposition of its allocated budget \cite{stortinget_vedtak_2019}; RME was operationalization as that authority from 1 November 2019, separating market regulation from NVE's broader resource-administration mandate. \avklar{add a bibliography entry for FOR-2019-10-24-1413, which is what actually sets the 1 November 2019 date. Separately, the references.bib entry for stortinget\_vedtak\_2019 carries year 2019 and month nov, whereas the lovvedtak was adopted 22 March 2018}

The 2022 International Energy Agency review of Norwegian energy policy observed that Norwegian demand-side flexibility options remain in the pilot phase and recommended that the government provide incentives for introducing sector-wide demand-side flexibility through network tariffs and flexibility services \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}.

The year 2023 brought parallel advances in the evidence base and in the physical system. The SINTEF ZEN research program's "Report 47" modeled the Norwegian building stock to 2050, quantifying how energy efficiency combined with district heating and heat-pump deployment could lower national peak electricity demand and reduce system energy costs \cite{SINTEF_energy_2023}. Vista Analyse published a cost-benefit analysis of energy-flexible heating systems at the building level, assessing the private and socio-economic consequences of possible changes to the TEK17 building-code flexibility requirements \cite{rosnes_energifleksible_2023}. In October 2023, the Haraldrud accumulator was commissioned, materially changing the technical basis of Oslo system flexibility.

\subsubsection{Why Norway Is Its Own Case}
\label{2-subsec:2.4.3 norwegian own case}

The preceding subsections establish two structural claims. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics} documents that the Norwegian power system routes the socio-economic value of power-to-heat flexibility through hydro reservoir economics and cross-border export pricing, a value pathway absent from the European literature that developed primarily around fossil displacement and VRE curtailment avoidance. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.2 policy evolution} documents that a decade of incremental regulatory activity has modified specific elements of the market environment without resolving the core mismatch between where flexibility value is created and where its cost is borne. This subsection addresses whether these two features, in combination, make Norwegian district heating flexibility analytically distinct from any Nordic comparator, or whether it constitutes a scaled-down version of the Danish or Swedish case that can be modeled by adaptation.

The hydro value pathway is necessary but not sufficient to explain the structural character of the Norwegian case. If it were the only distinguishing feature, the analytical gap could in principle be addressed by adapting a Danish or Swedish sector-coupling optimization and substituting the NO1 price signal for DK1 or SE3. What prevents this reduction is the co-occurrence of four conditions that individually appear elsewhere in the Nordic group but do not stack simultaneously in Denmark or Sweden (see \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.5 cross-country comparison}): the hydropower water-value pathway of \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics}, which routes the system value of flexibility through reservoir economics rather than fossil displacement; a substitution-price cap on heat revenues under the Energy Act §5-5, under which the heat price may not exceed the price of electric heating in the supply area \cite{energidepartementet_lov_1991}  and is therefore now indexed to a politically set retail electricity price since the state-funded Norgespris extension took effect for district heating customers on 1 October 2025 \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2025}; a market-access architecture that penalizes load-raising flexibility under the standard capacity-charge tariff, offers escape only through interruptible terms that presuppose disconnectability, and administratively caps reserve-market access per capacity area even for prequalified actors; and a $CO_2$ levy trajectory that raises the operating cost of the waste-to-energy base-load anchoring the Oslo merit order. No single Nordic comparator faces all four simultaneously, and the compounding interactions between them, in particular the joint effect of the price cap and the market-access architecture on the operator's net margin from a flexibility event, are not captured by any of the single-condition barrier studies reviewed in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}.

The consequence for the valuation framework is that the gap between commercial and socio-economic value of district heating flexibility in Norway is not merely a magnitude difference relative to Danish or Swedish equivalents: it is a structural outcome produced by a specific combination of barriers that interact and reinforce one another within a system whose marginal value mechanism is already fundamentally different. Quantifying the gap at the operator level therefore requires a Norway-specific model rather than a transferred Nordic case study, and the institutional explanation of why the gap persists requires accounting for each of the four compounding conditions rather than isolating a dominant single cause. \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} formalizes the two valuation perimeters that structure the quantification, and \autoref{3-sec:3} specifies the model built to measure the gap they define.

\subsection{Commercial and Socio-Economic Value}
\label{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}

This section defines the two valuation perimeters the thesis measures against each other: the business-economic (bedriftsøkonomisk) perimeter of the operator's own ledger, and the socio-economic (samfunnsøkonomisk) perimeter of all welfare effects regardless of whose balance sheet they land on. The definitions are covered because the research question is a comparison between the two, and any ambiguity in what each perimeter counts would surface as a spurious gap. They are used as the accounting rules of the quantitative work: the dispatch model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 DH operator model} computes the first perimeter, the triangulation in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 socio-economic valuation} the second, and the gap arithmetic in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} their difference. \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.1 economic definitions} draws the two perimeters item by item; \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap} then states the four-step mechanism through which the gap between them arises. The section connects backward to the enabler catalogue of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}, whose missing enablers are what hold the two perimeters apart, and forward to the policy discussion in \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications}.

%-------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Defining the Two Perspectives}
\label{2-subsec:2.5.1 economic definitions}

The commercial valuation perimeter encompasses the cash flows that accrue to the district heating operator, and only those flows. On the revenue side, this perimeter includes spot-market arbitrage revenue from purchasing low-price electricity for power-to-heat conversion and displacing more expensive backup boiler dispatch during high-price hours; reserve-market payments for mFRR capacity and, where the asset qualifies technically, aFRR capacity, comprising both the capacity component paid regardless of activation and the activation payment received when the reserve is called, a revenue line bounded in practice by administratively capped access rather than by asset scale (\autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}); and avoided peak-load charges under the standard effektledd regime, a revenue line that is marginal for assets on interruptible terms. On the cost side, the perimeter includes electricity purchased at the prevailing spot price, the network tariff, which for the incumbent's interruptible connections is an effective volumetric charge of approximately 5 øre/kWh rather than the standard effektledd \avklar{effective vilkår rate}, capital servicing on power-to-heat and storage assets, and variable operating costs including maintenance and staffing. The perimeter explicitly excludes the value that flexible operation creates for the grid operator, for other market participants, and for the broader energy system: these belong to the complementary perimeter. 

The socio-economic valuation perimeter encompasses all welfare effects attributable to district heating flexibility provision, regardless of which actor's balance sheet they appear on. In addition to the flows counted in the bedriftsøkonomisk perimeter, it includes avoided grid reinforcement costs, which accrue to the distribution system operator Elvia and ultimately to network tariff payers through a lower regulatory asset base; freed grid connection capacity that enables faster electrification of new loads, with welfare value captured by the connecting parties rather than the district heating operator; system price effects, whereby withdrawing power-to-heat demand from high-price hours clears the electricity market against a lower-cost marginal unit and redistributes consumer surplus across the bidding zone; displaced fossil or high-cost peaking generation and the corresponding reduction in $CO_2$ emissions; and, in the Norway-specific case, reduced peak load on dammed hydropower, which frees generation capacity for potential export \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}, though the same analysis finds that lower domestic demand also raises forced export to drain reservoirs and reduces the import the system can absorb, and it is a technical simulation that excludes investment costs and the electricity market. The value of freed export capacity accrues primarily to hydropower producers and indirectly to export market participants rather than to the district heating operator. The perimeter also incorporates an optionality component: the resilience value of thermal storage as a hedge against grid supply interruptions, a system reliability benefit with a diffuse beneficiary set. The conceptual basis for the socio-economic perimeter draws on the standard Norwegian framework for socio-economic analysis of public investments, adapted here to a private infrastructure operator context in which the externalities accrue to identifiable institutional actors rather than to anonymous social welfare.

These two perimeters are not two measurements of the same value. They are two accounting boundaries applied to the same physical asset and the same flexibility action. A single mFRR activation that reduces peak demand on the NO1 grid yields one value when assessed against the operator's income statement, and a larger value when assessed against total welfare. The difference is not a measurement error but a structural consequence of the institutional arrangements that determine which actor captures which cash flow. Anchoring the scale of that difference against prior work is difficult, because the field lacks a shared method. A systematic review of 123 district heating benefit and economic-assessment studies finds that flexibility, although identified as a benefit in fifteen of them, is monetized in none \cite{ahmed_district_2025}. The same review finds that welfare-level societal benefits are the least frequently assessed benefit category, and that no harmonized framework for valuing district heating benefits exists across the corpus. The choice of valuation perimeter is, consequently, not a presentational detail but the decision that fixes what the resulting figure means.

Two studies in the Nordic corpus apply paired perspectives to the same district heating asset and are the closest methodological precedents for the approach taken here. The first pairs the two perspectives across two models: EnergyPLAN on the whole Samsø energy system from a socio-economic perspective, and energyPRO on the business-economic case of the Ballen-Brundby plant within it. Optimising the plant's investment configuration puts the thermal storage optimum at roughly 10 to 15 MWh, which the authors note is far less than what was of interest from the island perspective, and they describe the resulting misalignment between what is relevant from a societal perspective and from a plant-operator perspective as one that needs to be overcome \cite{ostergaard_business_2019}. The second combines a heat-merit-order business-economic analysis with a socioeconomic assessment of seasonal storage in Linz, and likewise finds that storage value depends on multi-function dispatch integration rather than on raw capacity \cite{moser_socioeconomic_2018}. Its societal layer rests partly on input-output multipliers rather than welfare externalities, however, so it transfers as a method rather than as a comparator. The Norwegian institutional conditions documented in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} and \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context} (the grid tariff structure, the Energy Act §5-5 price cap, and the hydro-dominated value pathway) make direct transfer of either study's parameter values inadvisable, so the definitions applied here are constructed from the ground up for the Norwegian case. To the best of the author's knowledge, no paired bedriftsøkonomisk and samfunnsøkonomisk valuation of thermal flexibility has been conducted for Norwegian district heating at the firm level, a gap the methodology in \autoref{3-sec:3} is designed to fill.

\subsubsection{The Value Gap Mechanism}

\label{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap}

In the resource-and-enabler terms of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}, the value gap this thesis quantifies is what opens when a flexibility resource carries samfunnsøkonomisk value that no enabler converts into a bedriftsøkonomisk return. It operates through a four-step structural mechanism. First, thermal flexibility provision by a district heating operator reduces costs elsewhere in the energy system: avoided grid reinforcement at the distribution level, freed balancing capacity at the transmission level, and suppressed peak clearing prices that reduce consumer expenditure during high-demand events, each of which constitutes a real welfare gain attributable to the flexibility action \cite{mitridati_heat_2020}, \cite{mitridati_power_2018}, \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. Second, these cost reductions accrue primarily to Elvia as avoided distribution-grid capital expenditure, to Statnett as freed reserve capacity available for redeployment, and to electricity consumers as lower peak clearing prices. None of these actors are obligated under current market design to compensate the district heating operator for the service that generates them. This is the split-incentive configuration, in which the benefits of an action do not fall on the stakeholder performing it \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. The same divergence appears for Norwegian network companies: flexibility resources can create value for other actors in the flexibility value chain, such as the transmission system operator, that may not be accounted for in the DSOs' own cost-benefit analyses, and can carry wider societal benefits beyond the power system altogether \cite{saele_understanding_2023}. The authors raise it as a limitation of their DSO-only perspective rather than as a measured finding. Third, the operator bears the full cost of flexible operation: electricity purchased at the prevailing spot price, effektledd peak charges that, for standard connections, may rise rather than fall when a load-raising flexibility event sets a new monthly maximum, a channel largely defused for the incumbent's interruptible connections (\autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}), capital servicing on power-to-heat and storage assets, and the opportunity cost of heat not sold at the regulated price during arbitrage windows \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}, \cite{djorup_district_2020}. Fourth, no transfer mechanism currently bridges the gap between the samfunnsøkonomisk value generated and the bedriftsøkonomisk cost borne. The mFRR bid floor was cut from 10 MW to 1 MW with effect from 2 December 2025 \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}. The cut matters less for district heating than its framing suggests: at 5 MW in NO1 the floor already sat below the scale of the larger boiler installations, so bid size was not what kept them out of the reserve market (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). What binds is network-side prequalification - clearance with the distribution system operator and approval by the transmission system operator - and, since April 2026, the per-area volume caps of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The floor cut widened formal eligibility for the sub-threshold fringe; it did not open access for the incumbent, which had cleared the floor throughout. Threshold access is not the same as a reward, however: modelling of power-to-heat bidding into a reserve capacity market finds the economics bounded by the installed power-to-heat capacity and by the composition of the competing heat production, and concludes that policies rewarding power-balancing services, alongside investment support for power-to-heat capacity and heat storage, are needed to overcome those limits \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}. For the largest operator the constraint inverts: reserve access is administratively capped under the capacity-area regime even where scale, automation, and prequalification are all satisfied, so the missing enabler is not eligibility but allocation \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The Danish contrast shows the same enabler working: electric-boiler deployment in Danish district heating is driven primarily by the demand for ancillary services rather than the demand for heat, historically by the profit chance in the FCR market, where access is not administratively rationed \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}. No distribution-level product compensates peak-shaving, and district heating receives no grid-benefit payment for the congestion it relieves \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. The effektledd structure does not exempt flexible loads from capacity charges during reserve activations, even when those activations reduce net system congestion. Finally, price-model redesign can transmit flexibility value, but only along the operator-to-customer axis: Swedish case evidence finds that the price models best capturing flexibility are the power-plus-fee and marginal-pricing models, with marginal pricing delivering the highest reductions in both system and customer costs, so that route moves value between the operator and its building-owner customers and leaves the transfer from the wider power system unaddressed \cite{lygnerud_capturing_2024}. The boundary this mechanism draws is now articulated in government primary text: the proposition amending the Energy Act on surplus-heat utilization states that the benefits at issue are mainly societal while the decisions rest on business-economic criteria, that requirements imposing costs beyond an actor's own business-economic benefit constitute a disproportionate burden, and that the proposed utilization duty binds only where business-economically profitable \cite{det_kongelige_energidepartement_endringer_2026}. The result is a structural underinvestment equilibrium in which the operator's privately rational decision, to build out flexibility capacity and operate it actively, is attenuated by rules that assign the costs of flexibility to the actor who provides it while distributing its benefits to actors who bear none of the cost. This is the same divergence between operator-optimal and socially-optimal flexibility provision found in a Danish study that paired a socio-economic model of a whole island energy system with a business-economic model of one plant within it, which is evidence that the gap is structural rather than hypothetical \cite{ostergaard_business_2019}. System-level Nordic modeling reaches the same split from the other direction: demand-response deployment described as no-regret from a societal perspective yields private arbitrage value of only about 1.2 to 3.6 EUR/MWh, with the explicit conclusion that subsidies may be needed and a call for producer-perspective revenue modeling of the kind this thesis performs \cite{nagel_european_2023}. A 2020 review of Nordic sector coupling had already reached the antecedent conclusion: the integrated whole was blocked by a missing market and compensation model, at least in Norway \cite{verbeeten_state--art_2020}. The equilibrium is not a market failure in the textbook sense of an externality that no institutional arrangement has yet recognized. It is a regulatory-design outcome that made sense under the conditions prevailing when the relevant rules were written, and that has not been updated to reflect the changed system conditions produced by the electrification transition, the growth of variable renewable generation in adjacent bidding zones, and the grid-constraint trajectory documented for the Norwegian system in \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context}. \utsett{FORMAT: key-point box rendered as the plain \textbf{.}-led paragraph below; alternative is a tcolorbox (title "Key point") - requires /usepackage{tcolorbox}, not added silently.} \textbf{Key point.} The gap mechanism runs in four steps: (1) flexibility provision creates value elsewhere in the energy system; (2) that value accrues to Elvia, Statnett and electricity consumers; (3) the operator bears the full cost of providing it; (4) no general transfer mechanism routes the remaining value back, and for the largest operator reserve access is capped by allocation rather than eligibility. Each step is evidenced separately above.

The mechanism above describes the dominant direction of the gap: the samfunnsøkonomisk value of district heating flexibility exceeds the bedriftsøkonomisk value the operator can capture. This is the direction the Norwegian institutional arrangements predict and the pattern documented across the Nordic precedents. The directional claim warrants one qualification. Stylized Swedish district heating configurations with high power-to-heat penetration show that operator-optimized dispatch of stored power-to-heat can displace electricity co-generation from a competing biomass-CHP unit \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}. In that waste-to-energy configuration, biomass-CHP electricity output falls from $115 GWh$ to zero as power-to-heat capacity scales to 60 MW, with a corresponding loss of system balancing capacity that the operator's profit and loss does not register. The implication is not that the gap can run negative in the Norwegian case at current power-to-heat penetration, where Hafslund Celsio's co-generation comes from waste incineration dispatched as inflexible base-load rather than from a merit-order-displaceable biomass-CHP unit. Rather, at sufficiently high power-to-heat build-out, the assumption that samfunnsøkonomisk value always weakly exceeds bedriftsøkonomisk value requires re-examination. The analysis in \autoref{4-sec:4 results} operates within the dominant-direction regime. The boundary condition under which the gap could reverse is flagged for the future work in \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work}.

\subsection{Literature Review: Field Overview and Research Gap}

The preceding five sections have assembled the conceptual and empirical foundations this thesis operationalizes: the vocabulary of flexibility (\autoref{2-subsec:2.1 concepts}), the technology stack that supplies it (\autoref{2-subsec:2.2 DH as flexibility}), the barrier taxonomy that constrains it (\autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}), the Norwegian system characteristics that make it a distinct case (\autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context}), and the paired valuation perimeters that define what is being measured (\autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}). It is not a full systematic review; its function is narrower: to make explicit how the source base was assembled, to map that base onto the dimensions that define the thesis's contribution, and to state precisely which region of that map remains unoccupied. The research-gap matrix in \autoref{2-subsec:2.6.2 research gap matrix} anchors this positioning, while the surrounding prose sets it up and reads it off. \subsubsection{How the Field Was Mapped}
\label{2-subsec:2.6.1 how field mapped}

The corpus underpinning this chapter was assembled through an informational rather than a structural review process. The starting point was a seed set of supervisor-supplied papers and the author's own keyword seeding. This was expanded in Research Rabbit and Consensus by snowballing across citation and co-citation links, and supplemented by targeted Scopus searches on the themes where the seed set was thin: grid tariff design, balancing-market access for demand-side assets, and Norwegian district heating specifically. 

This is an informational review, and is presented as such. Across 123 studies, the field has no harmonized method for monetizing district heating benefits \cite{ahmed_district_2025}, so a protocol-driven review would impose a comparability the underlying literature does not possess. A published systematic-methodology checklist is instead used as a coverage-hygiene instrument \cite{atabaki_systematic_2025}, to confirm that the assembled set spans the temporal, spatial, economic, and sector-coupling dimensions a flexibility valuation must address, rather than as the review method itself. The assembled corpus is, by composition, overwhelmingly Danish- and Nordic-led. The Norwegian contributions are largely case-applications of frameworks developed for the Danish and wider Nordic systems rather than independent methodological work. Even at the field level, the intersection of district heating with sector-coupling policy is thin, the exclusive focus of only one of the 25 studies in a recent sector-coupling scoping review \cite{vanhanen_energy_2024}. The research-gap matrix encodes this geographic skew directly.

\subsubsection{Where This Thesis Fits and What It Adds}
\label{2-subsec:2.6.3 where this thesis fits}

This thesis occupies the firm-level $\times$ paired cell of \utsett{ref research gap matrix}, applied to the Norwegian district heating case: a single operator's flexibility assessed against both the business economic and the socio-economic perimeter on consistent scenarios. It draws on the two nearest populated regions of the map. From the single-perspective Norwegian work it takes the operator-ledger modeling precedent \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016} and the welfare-side institutional diagnosis of the THEMA report \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. From the paired Nordic work it takes the methodological template of the Danish Samsø study \cite{ostergaard_business_2019}, adopted for its two-perimeter structure rather than for its biomass-island results. The precise claim the matrix supports is therefore not that the value of thermal flexibility is unstudied, but that, to the best of the author's knowledge, no paired business economic and socio-economic valuation of thermal flexibility has been conducted for Norwegian district heating at the firm level. What makes the cell more than a geographic blank is the regulatory detail that defines it: although generic grid-tariff effects on power-to-heat are well studied, the Norwegian capacity tariff central to the operator-side cost in  2.3.2 has been characterized in policy and operator-facing analysis rather than analyzed as a flexibility barrier in its own right. The thesis also quantifies before it monetizes: a technical flexibility envelope (Section 3.2) sizes the physical resource in MWh and MW and supplies the common denominator for the paired valuation, directly addressing the field-wide finding that flexibility has been monetized in none of the 123 reviewed studies \cite{ahmed_district_2025}. The methodology developed in Chapter 3 is constructed specifically to fill that cell.

\subsubsection{Adjacent Gaps Left for Future Work}
\label{2-subsec:2.6.4 future work}

Naming the cell the thesis fills also sharpens the adjacent cells it does not. Three are worth flagging, both to anchor the future-work discussion in  5.5 and to keep the contribution claim precise.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multi-system paired valuation.} The thesis takes Oslo as its primary case; extending the paired BØ/SØ assessment across Trondheim, Bergen, and smaller municipal systems is a distinct research area, not a marginal extension, because the storage and capacity asymmetries documented in sections 2.3.1 and  2.4.1 break the single-case parameterization.
  \item \textbf{End-user welfare and distributional effects.} The welfare consequences of flexibility activation for the heat customer fall outside the scope set in  1.4, and remain almost entirely unmonetized in the field: end-user and distributional benefits are valued in only a handful of the 123 studies in the systematic review \cite{ahmed_district_2025}.
  \item \textbf{Business-model and instrument design.} This thesis names the missing transfer mechanism ( 2.5.2) but does not design it. The concrete shape of a capacity-payment product, an aggregation contract, or a bilateral DSO flexibility agreement remains open. The closest precedent for that design question is a systematic review of district-energy business models \cite{selvakkumaran_how_2021}.
\end{itemize}
