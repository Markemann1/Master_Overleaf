\section{Theoretical Framework}
\label{2-sec:2}

This chapter equips the reader with the conceptual toolkit the analysis depends on, and closes with the field map that locates the thesis's contribution. \autoref{2-subsec:2.1 concepts} fixes the flexibility vocabulary and the resource-and-enabler frame; \autoref{2-subsec:2.2 DH as flexibility} describes the technology stack through which district heating supplies flexibility; \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} catalogues the barriers, each a missing or adverse enabler, that block its value capture in Norway; \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context} establishes why the Norwegian system is its own case; \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} defines the business-economic and socio-economic valuation perimeters whose difference the thesis measures; and \autoref{2-subsec:2.6 literature review} maps the literature onto those dimensions and names the empty cell this thesis fills. 

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Energy System Flexibility: Concepts and Frameworks}
\label{2-subsec:2.1 concepts}

The field offers no single agreed definition of energy flexibility, and unstated definitional choices would otherwise leak into the valuation: what counts as flexibility decides what gets valued. This section therefore does the definitional groundwork for the rest of the thesis. \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions} states the thesis's working definition and separates the structural and operational layers of a flexibility resource from the enablers that convert dispatch into value. \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power} introduces the bidding-zone structure and the reserve products through which that flexibility earns revenue in the Norwegian power system, and \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.3 sector coupling} establishes power-to-heat as the specific sector coupling this thesis models. The resource-and-enabler split carries the connections forward: \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} catalogues the enablers that are missing or adverse in Norway, \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context} shows why the Norwegian enabler set is distinct, and \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} formalises the value gap that opens when a resource exists but its enablers do not.

\begin{keypoint}[title=In short]
A flexibility \textit{resource} (structural capacity plus operational dispatch) yields no value to its owner without an \textit{enabler}: a market, reserve product, or tariff that rewards its activation. Norwegian district heating holds the resource; this thesis quantifies what the missing or adverse enablers cost. 
\end{keypoint}


\subsubsection{Definitions and Frameworks}
\label{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}

This subsection does the definitional work first because every later quantity inherits it: the working definition adopted here bounds what the dispatch model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} treats as flexibility, and the structural, operational and enabler layers it separates are the frame the barrier catalogue of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} and the valuation perimeters of \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} are built on. No single definition of energy flexibility commands agreement across the heating and power-system literature, and the variation is consequential because each definition draws a different boundary around what counts as flexibility. Two recent reviews document the fragmentation directly: A SINTEF synthesis grades $16$ prior definitions against three criteria, finds none that satisfies all, and proposes a unifying one: the ability of an asset or system to modify its routine operation for a limited duration in response to an external signal \cite{degefa_comprehensive_2021}. 

A systematic review of $85$ building-flexibility studies finds that roughly $42\%$ of them give no formal definition of flexibility, and that the rest rely on $61$ distinct quantification metrics \cite{li_residential_2021}. Three broad traditions recur. The first treats flexibility as a physical property of the system, grounded in inertia and in the coordinated operation of coupled electricity, heat and gas grids, where flexibility is the ability to speed up or delay the injection or extraction of energy relative to the reference profile, requiring only that the system possess inertia \cite{vandermeulen_controlling_2018}, \cite{lund_smart_2017}.
The second defines flexibility operationally, as a response to an external signal, namely the managed shifting of demand and generation to meet grid and local objectives, quantified through market-tier participation or performance indicators such as the "Flexibility Function" rather than in absolute energy terms \cite{li_ten_2022}, \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}. 
The third, sociological, tradition argues that both framings take the timing of demand as given, and so risk stabilising rather than providing a genuinely flexible system \cite{blue_conceptualising_2020}, a caveat this thesis acknowledges while remaining within the engineering and economic frameworks. The traditions converge on flexibility as the temporal shifting of energy injection or extraction enabled by inertia, and diverge mainly on whether it is treated as a system property, a market-tradable response, or a control problem. This thesis adopts a definition that sits in the first two traditions and suits a district heating operator facing the power market. Energy flexibility is the capacity of an asset or system to modify its rate of production or consumption relative to a reference profile in response to a price- or system signal, within technical and commercial constraints \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}, \cite{degefa_comprehensive_2021}. 

A second distinction separates the physical capacity for flexibility from its operation, and this split organises how that capacity is described throughout this thesis. The \textit{structural} layer is the embedded physical capability that makes deviation from the reference profile possible. In a district heating system it comprises the three sources of thermal inertia identified by \cite{vandermeulen_controlling_2018} -- the network heat carrier, dedicated thermal energy storage such as an accumulator tank, and the thermal mass of connected buildings -- together with the installed capacity of the electric boilers. The \textit{operational} layer is the dispatch decision that activates that physical capacity in response to a signal. These are two utilisation layers of the same technology: an electric boiler is structural in its installed capacity, but the choice to up- or down-regulate its dispatch is operational. Structural capacity without an operational dispatch signal therefore yields no flexibility. 

Structural capacity and operational dispatch together constitute a \textit{flexibility resource}, and whether that resource provides any value to the operator depends on a third layer, the \textit{enablers}. Markets, reserve products and network tariffs are the enablers, the external preconditions required to activate the value of a flexibility resource \cite{degefa_comprehensive_2021}, \cite{saele_understanding_2023}. A district heating operator can hold ample structural flexibility capacity and even dispatch it correctly, yet capture no value if no reserve product, bilateral contract or market mechanism exists. How the absence of these enablers creates a value gap between the socio-economic benefits and the commercial value of the same physical flexibility is formalised in \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}.

Operational flexibility is exercised in two modes. The first is load-shifting: advancing or deferring consumption relative to the reference profile without altering the total energy delivered, which repositions the time at which the same amount of consumption occurs. The second, and the principal operational lever in district heating with electric boilers installed, is directional regulation, the increase or decrease of electrical consumption in response to system conditions. The Nordic flexibility framing of the Flex4RES programme defines flexibility in these directional terms: the ability to regulate the increase or decrease of generation or consumption in response to market signals \cite{skytte_flexible_2019}. 
The two directions map onto opposite system needs. When electricity is in surplus and prices are low, a power-to-heat (P2H) asset up-regulates, increasing electrical consumption to convert surplus power into heat and charging the accumulator tank for later use. When the grid is stressed and prices are high, the same system down-regulates, reducing electric boiler draw and instead discharging stored heat from the tank, which shaves peak demand on the power system while still serving heat demand \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}.

The power flexibility of a district heating system with a controllable electric boiler is central to this thesis. Golmohamadi distinguishes power flexibility, a response in the system's electricity demand, from heat-side flexibility, and names the electrically operated assets as the source of the greatest direct power-flexibility potential in district heating systems \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}. Bloeß and Schill frame the same flexibility as the \textit{flexible} use of electricity for heating, the timing of the conversion adjusted to system conditions rather than electricity simply substituting for fuel \cite{bloes_power--heat_2018}. 

How far that power flexibility can be shifted in time depends on the thermal buffer into which the converted heat can be stored, and later drawn from. Golmohamadi groups three such buffers into two kinds \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item Passive thermal inertia:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item The heat carrier in the network pipes (FHC, real-time flexibility/intraday)
    \item The thermal mass of connected buildings (FTI, real-time flexibility/intraday)
  \end{enumerate}
  \item Active thermal inertia:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Dedicated thermal storage (FTS): An accumulator tank that scales from day-ahead arbitrage to seasonal shifting depending on its size.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{itemize}
For a district heating operator working from the supply side, the relevant buffers are the network heat carrier (FHC) and the accumulator (FTS). Building thermal inertia (FTI) is a demand-side source outside the scope of this thesis. The buffer's parameters, namely its heat storage capacity, the sustainable deviation duration, and the ramp rate at which changes can be applied, determine which products the power-flexibility action can bid into \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}. Two revenue pathways appear. The first is reserve products, procured by the transmission system operator and graded by activation speed, which pay for capacity held ready and for energy activated on a system signal \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}. The second is spot-market arbitrage, which rewards shifting consumption across the day-ahead price profile and requires a price spread between charging and discharging windows wide enough to cover the cost of flexible operation. Each pathway faces different eligibility barriers, activates on a different signal, and forms a separate component of the commercial value of thermal flexibility.

Just as the definition of flexibility differs, so does the estimation of its value. The term is applied to everything from reduced system operating cost or welfare gain in \cite{mitridati_heat_2020}, to the operating profit a district heating operator gains from stacking reserve-market participation onto its heat dispatch in \cite{javanshir_operation_2022}, to the uplift in the market value of variable renewable generation through offsetting the merit-order effect \cite{kirkerud_power--heat_2017}, as well as building or network performance indicators such as peak-to-average load ratio \cite{li_ten_2022}. These framings apply different system boundaries, count different actors as beneficiaries, and produce results in incomparable units. This thesis adopts a perspective-explicit working definition: Flexibility value is the monetary or welfare benefit attributed to a flexibility action, conditional on the system boundary and the actor whose ledger is being measured. \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} formalises this as the commercial interest and socio-economic value perimeters.

\subsubsection{Flexibility in Power Systems}
\label{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power}

District heating flexibility earns its revenue in the power system, so the structure of that system decides which revenue pathways exist and what they pay. The Norwegian power system is organised into five bidding zones, NO1 through NO5, which clear separately through Nord Pool, each with its own spot price. The zones reflect physical transmission constraints between regions and are also a regulatory instrument for managing congestion: when transfer limits are reached, each zone clears its own price, which signals local scarcity or surplus \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}. Hydropower supplied $91.8\%$ of national generation in 2020, or $141.6$ TWh, making it by far the dominant generation source \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}. NO2, in the south-west, is the largest producer and runs a substantial surplus, together with the western (NO5) and northern (NO4) zones, which also generate above local demand. NO1, the south-eastern grid including Oslo, is the demand centre of the country and is generally a net importer of power. In 2024 NO1 generated $21.6$ TWh against $33.4$ TWh of consumption, covering $65\%$ of its own demand, whereas NO2 generated $55.6$ TWh against $34.1$ TWh and NO5 $32.5$ TWh against $16.2$ TWh \cite{statistisk_sentralbyra_statbank_nodate}. The deficit is structural rather than incidental: NO1 generated below its own consumption in every year from 2020 to 2025, covering between $51\%$ and $65\%$ of it. What NO1 does generate is almost entirely hydropower, at $94\%$ of its own output in 2024, so the zone pairs a hydropower-dominated supply mix with a persistent import requirement. 

The southern zones (NO1, NO2, and NO5) are strongly coupled and frequently clear at a single price: measured on 2024 day-ahead prices, NO1 cleared within $5$ NOK/MWh of NO5 in $74\%$ of hours and of NO2 in $51\%$ \cite{nord_pool_nord_nodate}. The sharper price divide runs between the south and the north (NO3 and NO4), not between the southern neighbour-zones \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}: the same measurement places NO1 within $5$ NOK/MWh of NO3 in only $21\%$ of hours and of NO4 in $15\%$, and NO1 clears above the two northern zones in two-thirds and three-quarters of hours respectively, by an annual average of $161$ and $217$ NOK/MWh. Wind generation is concentrated in NO2, NO3, and NO4, which together held $94\%$ of national wind generation in 2024, whereas NO5 remains hydropower-dominated and generated no wind power at all \cite{statistisk_sentralbyra_statbank_nodate}. The Oslo zone is thus coupled most tightly to a zone that carries no wind, and reaches the wind-driven surplus through its looser link to NO2; that link, however, tightens precisely in the hours that matter for power-to-heat. In the decile of hours when NO2 is cheapest, NO1 cleared within $5$ NOK/MWh of it in $82\%$ of hours, rising to $99\%$ in the hours when NO2 priced below zero, against $51\%$ across the year as a whole \cite{nord_pool_nord_nodate}. A wind-driven low-price surplus in the south therefore propagates almost fully to the Oslo zone in the hours a power-to-heat operator would charge. The arbitrage value available to a power-to-heat operator in NO1 is therefore governed less by local conditions and more by southern wind and system coupling to continental Europe, and by congestion in the connector to the northern zones. 

The resulting price-spread distribution sets the ceiling of the commercial arbitrage value available to Oslo-based DH operators before any market-access barriers are taken into account. For a day-cycle thermal store the binding measure is the spread available within the day rather than across the year. Matched to a store of roughly five to six hours' duration, the mean daily difference between the six dearest and the six cheapest hours in NO1 was $314$ NOK/MWh in 2024, with a median of $231$ NOK/MWh, and on $30$ of $366$ days it fell below $50$ NOK/MWh \cite{nord_pool_nord_nodate}. The same measure gives $809$ NOK/MWh for DK1, so the window an Oslo operator trades against is well under half that of a thermally-coupled Danish reference zone.

Norway's hydropower dominance reshapes the value logic of thermal flexibility relative to fossil-heavy or wind-heavy power systems \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023}. The combination of dispatchable hydropower and price-responsive industrial demand makes Norway the Nordic region's primary provider of system flexibility \cite{skytte_flexible_2019}. In the terms of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}, a hydropower reservoir is itself a structural flexibility asset at system scale. In a fossil-dominated system, a load that absorbs off-peak electricity displaces marginal thermal generation, reducing fuel combustion and the associated emissions. In Norway, where the marginal unit originates from hydropower, no fuel is displaced and the system-level benefit runs instead through dispatchable capacity. Modelling of the Norwegian case finds that displacing direct electric heating with district heating lowers the peak load on dammed hydropower and frees generation capacity for potential export \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. The primary system-level benefit of sector coupling in Norway is therefore freed generation capacity rather than saved fossil fuel cost and consumption. In model simulations of a 2030 Nordic system, power-to-heat in the district heating mix concentrates in the spring, summer and autumn rather than during peak winter demand, because the high electricity price during winter hours makes other heating fuels more competitive than electric conversion \cite{kirkerud_power--heat_2017}. 

Statnett procures balancing services through a three-tier product hierarchy that cascades through increased time-frames, from automated to manual activation. Frequency Containment Reserves (FCR) arrest frequency deviations and stabilise the frequency at a new level, are governed by local automatic functions, and split into a symmetric normal-operation product (FCR-N, which activates across the $49.9$-$50.1 Hz$ normal band and is fully activated within three minutes) and separate up- and down-regulating disturbance products (FCR-D, $50\%$ activated within five seconds and $100\%$ within $30$ seconds) \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}. Automatic Frequency Restoration Reserves (aFRR) bring the frequency back toward the $49.9$-$50.1 Hz$ normal band after an imbalance, activate automatically on control signals from Statnett within two minutes, and require the resource to be connected to a control centre over SCADA \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}. Manual Frequency Restoration Reserves (mFRR) are activated on an electronic order from Statnett to replace the faster reserves and hold the balance until a new balance is reached in the energy market, under a response-time requirement of under $15$ minutes in the legacy regulating-power market and under $12.5$ minutes in the mFRR Energy Activation Market \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}, and they constitute the product tier most accessible to large thermal demand-side assets such as district heating electric boilers. Until December 2025 the minimum bid size for mFRR participation was $10 MW$ in NO2, NO4 and NO5 and $5 MW$ in the NO1 and NO3 bidding zones \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}, a threshold that excluded all but the largest aggregated demand-side assets. Two changes then arrived separately. The Nordic mFRR Energy Activation Market went live on 4 March 2025, moving activation to automated 15-minute units \cite{solberg_manedsrapport_nodate}. The minimum bid was cut to $1 MW$ with effect from 2 December 2025 - from the $10 MW$ headline floor Statnett quotes, and from the $5 MW$ that had applied in NO1 and NO3 \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate} - requested and approved under Article 18 of the Electricity Balancing Guideline as a precondition for the European MARI platform \cite{statnett_1_2025, statnett_forslag_2025} - a change Statnett had signalled in advance as part of its transition to automated balancing \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}. The bid-size floor therefore no longer functions as a structural exclusion mechanism for district heating, where a single electric boiler site now clears this threshold. For the sub-MW fringe of the fleet, qualification cost and operational and metering complexity still bind. For a large qualified actor, the binding constraint has instead moved to the administrative rationing of reserve access: Statnett caps the reserve volume that can prequalify per capacity area in grids below 110 kV, and prequalification applications are processed in the order received, with case handling expected to run beyond normal deadlines \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The consumption capacity charge remains a conditional barrier: binding in its standard form, largely neutralised for the incumbent's interruptible connections, as \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} develops. These barriers are quantified in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap}. The remaining barriers are further examined in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, and quantified in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap}.

\subsubsection{Sector Coupling and Thermal Systems}
\label{2-subsec:2.1.3 sector coupling}

Sector coupling comes last in the conceptual groundwork because power-to-heat is the specific coupling this thesis prices: this subsection fixes the coupling's direction in the Norwegian configuration and introduces the storage-equivalence argument that \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} formalises and the model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} operationalises. Sector coupling denotes the intentional integration of distinct energy-carrier systems through conversion technologies that let energy flow in parallel across historically separate supply chains \cite{lund_smart_2017}. The operative coupling in this thesis is power-to-heat: the conversion of electrical energy to thermal energy through electric boilers and heat pumps, or the substitution of individual electric heating with district heating. Electric boilers and heat pumps create a controllable load on the power system and a corresponding heat source within the district heating network, making the network an active interface between the two carriers rather than a passive heat consumer. For both technologies the coupling is unidirectional: electricity is consumed to produce heat, with no reverse conversion within the asset. Combined heat and power (CHP), including waste incineration plants, couples the same two carriers but in the opposite direction, generating electricity alongside heat from fuel, and its flexibility comes from adjusting the boiler load or the heat-to-power ratio \cite{beiron_combined_2020}. That reverse coupling is the Danish, Swedish and Finnish configuration rather than the Norwegian one, combined heat and power contributing between $41\%$ and $73\%$ of district heating heat supply in the six Nordic and Baltic countries with large district heating sectors \cite{skytte_flexible_2019}: mainland Norway's hydropower access meant co-generation was never needed for electricity adequacy, and electricity from Norwegian waste-to-energy plants amounts to under one percent of national power generation \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fakta_nodate}. In the Oslo system the technology is heat-led and serves as base-load rather than as a P2H flexibility asset (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). Realising the value of the P2H coupling depends on policy and market design as much as on the conversion technology. A scoping review of sector-coupling research finds that policy integration and sector coupling are studied largely in parallel, with district heating the explicit focus of only one of the $25$ reviewed studies, leaving the coordinated policy conditions for P2H flexibility under-examined \cite{vanhanen_energy_2024}.

The structural flexibility of a district heating system, its thermal inertia, is what makes this interface relevant at meaningful scale. Network piping volume and dedicated accumulator tanks can absorb or release heat independently of instantaneous consumer demand, giving the operator a thermal buffer across which electrical load can be advanced or deferred. The structural capacity of that buffer, namely its volume, temperature range, and charge and discharge rate, sets the envelope within which operational load-shifting is feasible. When electricity prices are low, a P2H asset converts surplus power to heat and stores it in that buffer, to be released during high-price or high-demand periods, which shaves peak demand and contributes to congestion management on the power system \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}, and where the heat sector carries enough such buffering capacity, the electrical demand that power-to-heat adds need not push prices to extremes \cite{bloes_power--heat_2018}. This coordination is framed as a virtual electricity storage function, in which the district heating system provides energy storage-equivalent services to the power system by synchronising its electrical load with system conditions, without a dedicated power-storage asset \cite{mitridati_heat_2020}. The same work shows that sequential heat-then-electricity market clearing is short-sighted, so an operator optimising within its own heat-market perimeter forgoes the system value that coordinated clearing would realise. The storage-equivalence itself is quantified in \cite{mitridati_power_2018}, which models district heating pipeline storage as a power-system resource on a stylised six-bus, three-node case. It reports a $3.51\%$ reduction in total cost and a $1.65\%$ uplift in wind utilisation when the virtual storage capability is unlocked. These magnitudes are case-specific, but the results indicate that heat storage-equivalence has merit, and that district heating thermal inertia carries quantifiable system benefit when the market design permits coordinated clearing. The critical qualification is that this value must be accessible to the operator who provides it. This accessibility hinges on an enabler: a reserve-market product, a flexibility contract, or market and fee structures that reward coordinated dispatch. Without such an enabler, the value accrues only to the grid operators and consumers, while the operator bears the full cost of flexible operation. This asymmetry between where value is created and where cost falls is what the thesis quantifies and seeks to reduce. 

The Norwegian consequence of this coupling value differs from other European nations in one important aspect. In systems with high shares of variable renewable generation (VRE), the primary system-level benefit of P2H is the absorption of surplus wind and solar energy which would otherwise be curtailed, thus raising the achievable penetration of renewable generation \cite{geabermudez_role_2021}. In Norway, where the dominating marginal power unit is dispatchable hydropower reservoirs rather than non-dispatchable variable renewables, the corresponding benefit runs through dispatchable capacity rather than curtailment avoidance: substituting district heating for direct electric heating lowers the winter peak on dammed hydropower and frees generation capacity \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. The same work cautions that the demand reduction also increases forced export from reservoir draining, which it reads as a sign of system inflexibility rather than a gain \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. The coupling mechanism is identical across both contexts, but the value pathway differs. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context} develops the Norwegian system characteristics that produce these dynamics, and \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} places it within the commercial DH and socio-economic valuation perimeters.

\subsection{District Heating as a Flexibility Resource}
\label{2-subsec:2.2 DH as flexibility}

This section covers the technology stack through which district heating supplies the flexibility defined in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1 concepts}: the European and Nordic system context (\autoref{2-subsec:2.2.1 eu and nordic overview}), the power-to-heat conversion assets (\autoref{2-subsec:2.2.2 P2H technologies}), and the thermal storage that shifts their output in time (\autoref{2-subsec:2.2.3 thermal storage}). The material is covered because the dispatch model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} is parameterised directly on these technology characteristics; ramp rates, minimum loads, efficiencies and storage capacity decide which markets each asset can physically serve. The section is used to establish the merit-order roles the model encodes: waste-to-energy as inflexible base-load, heat pumps as slow mid-merit assets, electrode boilers as the fast flexibility asset, and the accumulator as the buffer that decouples their dispatch from instantaneous heat demand.

%-----------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{European and Nordic Overview}
\label{2-subsec:2.2.1 eu and nordic overview}

The European and Nordic baseline is established first because the barrier frameworks and technical benchmarks this chapter borrows are Danish and Swedish in origin: how far each transfers depends on how the Norwegian sector differs from its neighbours, which is the comparison \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.5 cross-country comparison} and \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context} then build on. District heating covers a significant, but unevenly distributed, share of building heat demand across Europe. Throughout this thesis, the penetration metric is the share of national building heat demand met by district heating. The most comprehensive cross-country baseline on this metric is close to a decade old, but the sector moves slowly: total district heat supply in the European Union has been almost constant since 1990, with disconnections in the former planned economies offset by expansions elsewhere, so the sector's heat deliveries are described as rather stable \cite{werner_international_2017}. According to that study, some countries have almost no district heating, while implementation rates around and above $50\%$ appear in Iceland, Denmark, Sweden, Finland, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania, Poland and Russia; across the European Union as a whole district heating supplied roughly $13\%$ of building heat use in 2014, and roughly $8\%$ worldwide \cite{werner_international_2017}. A more recent review of the Danish sector puts district heating at $56\%$ of Danish building heat demand at the end of the 2010s \cite{johansen_something_2022}. The sector is undergoing a generational transition toward lower supply temperatures and greater integration of renewable and waste-incineration sources. The fourth-generation (4GDH) concept that frames this shift was defined over a decade ago \cite{lund_4th_2014}. A 2024 comprehensive survey extends the trajectory to a fifth generation (5GDH) of near-ambient-temperature networks, which is still evolving alongside 4GDH rather than replacing it, and remains confined to early pilot projects \cite{dang_fifth_2024}. This thesis concerns existing large-scale DH infrastructure operating at conventional supply temperatures; 4GDH and 5GDH developments are noted as background context but do not form part of the analysis. 

Denmark occupies a disproportionately large share of the DH flexibility literature relative to its system scale, reflecting a position at the forefront of development and implementation in both wind energy and district heating that makes the exploitation of sector-coupling synergies a live question in the Danish setting \cite{sipos_power_heat_2025}. The Danish sector rests on statutory advantages that Norway does not share: mandatory connection for new development, a non-profit pricing principle, and long-term municipal financing and ownership that align operator incentives with broader energy-system objectives \cite{johansen_something_2022}. Greater Copenhagen, where the integration of a large-scale heat-pump fleet has been modelled in detail against the existing plant mix, has become a recurring technical benchmark for DH-integrated P2H flexibility \cite{bach_integration_2016}. Many of the barrier typologies and analytical frameworks applied in this chapter were developed in the Danish context, and are transferred here with adjustment for Norwegian structural conditions. The Danish fleet scale keeps the comparison honest: 136 electric boilers totalling 1,860 MW in 2024, with installed capacity roughly doubled between 2019 and 2023 \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}.

Sweden represents a comparatively under-examined Nordic case. Swedish district heating covered around $50\%$ or more of national building heat use as of 2014, with Stockholm among the world's major district heating systems \cite{werner_international_2017}. The SthlmFlex project demonstrated a local flexibility market architecture within the Stockholm system, initiated jointly by Svenska kraftnät and the regional DSOs Vattenfall Eldistribution and Ellevio to address congestion during the winter season \cite{nyman_how_2026}. The pilot operated from winter 2020/2021 onward and has since concluded with very low market liquidity, with Ellevio stating that short-term local flexibility markets are not a suitable congestion-management tool for Stockholm in the near term \cite{scrocca_local_2026}. It nevertheless remains the closest Nordic institutional reference for multi-DSO/TSO flexibility coordination. A recent sector-coupling optimisation for the Swedish DH sector is structurally analogous to this thesis's Norwegian question \cite{atabaki_optimising_2026}, and the only available qualitative study of Swedish DH-flexibility actors finds that weak institutional incentives and profit-oriented ownership structures limit flexibility provision despite adequate technical capacity \cite{fernqvist_district_2023}. Sweden's combination of large urban DH systems, growing wind integration and emerging local flexibility market structures makes it the more instructive Nordic comparator for the analysis in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.5 cross-country comparison}, whereas Denmark provides the primary technical benchmark throughout \autoref{2-subsec:2.2 DH as flexibility} and \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}.

Norway presents a structurally distinct profile within the Nordic group. National district heating penetration is approximately $10\%$ of national building heat demand \cite{SINTEF_energy_2023}, significantly lower than Danish or Swedish levels \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. The sector is concentrated in three cities with three separate operators: Oslo (Hafslund Celsio), Trondheim (Lunera Energi), and Bergen (Eviny Termo) \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}.

Sandberg et al. attribute this penetration gap to historical lock-in rather than to differences in operator profitability: Norway's legacy of abundant, historically cheap electricity and a direct-electric heating culture left it with few water-borne distribution systems, so the country now combines the lowest overall DH penetration in the Nordic region with the highest power-to-heat share of district heating generation \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. Norwegian DH operators must consequently compete against a low-cost electricity baseline and lack the scale, cooperative ownership structures, and policy support that sustain Danish and Swedish operators. This structural position shapes both the barrier analysis in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} and the case-selection rationale in \autoref{3-subsec:3.1 research design}.

\subsubsection{Power-to-Heat Technologies}
\label{2-subsec:2.2.2 P2H technologies}

This subsection establishes which of the three heat-supply technologies is the flexibility asset and which set the frame around it: the electrode boiler's speed, the heat pump's cycling limits and the waste-to-energy unit's heat-led inflexibility are the distinctions the merit order in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} encodes. Power-to-heat (P2H) technologies are the controllable electrical loads that couple the electricity system to the district heating network: electric boilers and large-scale compression heat pumps convert purchased electricity into heat and can therefore be dispatched against electricity price signals and reserve-market activation windows \cite{bloes_power--heat_2018}, \cite{golmohamadi_integration_2022}. A third heat source, waste-to-energy (WtE) incineration, is a heat-only technology in the Norwegian case and a combined heat and power (CHP) technology elsewhere in the Nordics, where it couples the two carriers in reverse and generates electricity as a by-product of heat production \cite{beiron_combined_2020}. The Norwegian configuration is the exception rather than a variant of the Nordic one: the CHP share of Norwegian district heat production was 1.5 percent in 2013 against 73 percent in Denmark and Finland \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}, and electricity from Norwegian WtE plants amounts to under one percent of national power generation, with the only large-scale CHP plant in a Norwegian district heating context sited on Svalbard \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fakta_nodate}. Where a turbine is present, dispatch remains heat-led in any case: in a modelled Swedish 50 MW WtE plant, condensing operation is available in only 20 to 25 percent of hours because the heat demand must be served first \cite{beiron_combined_2020}. It is treated here because it sets the merit-order floor around which the P2H assets dispatch, not because it is itself a P2H flexibility asset. The three sources differ in capital intensity, operating cost structure, ramp rate, and the mechanism through which they bear on flexibility provision, distinctions the commercial model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} depends on.

Electrode boilers convert electrical current directly to heat by passing it through a water or steam medium, a process whose ramp rate is limited in practice only by switchgear response times rather than by thermal inertia, which makes the electrode boiler the fastest-responding P2H asset. Centralised power-to-heat draws on large-scale heat pumps or on large electric boilers, often in the form of electrode boilers \cite{bloes_power--heat_2018}. Capital cost is low relative to the other power-to-heat assets: the Danish Energy Agency technology catalogue puts a large electric boiler at 0.07 MEUR per MW of heat capacity with a variable operating cost of 0.84 EUR/MWh excluding electricity, central estimates in 2025-EUR excluding taxes, and rates it as able to regulate 100\% of full load within 30 seconds from a 3\% minimum load, starting warm in roughly 36 seconds and cold in about five minutes at no startup cost \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}.

Electric resistance boilers are usually connected at low voltage, around 400 V, with capacities below 5 MW, while electrode boilers connect at medium voltage, around 10 kV, and reach $3-70 MW$; both types achieve conversion efficiencies of $95-99.9\%$ \cite{maruf_classification_2022}. In a statement to the online magazine Energiteknikk, Helle Grønli from AFRY points to a value-add on two fronts: electrode boilers could earn meaningful annual revenue from mFRR capacity payments, while optimising dispatch against the spot price could deliver sizable operational cost savings, mostly from heat-pump operation \cite{gronli_hvor_2025}. A further nuance sits at the price floor: when spot prices go negative, electric boilers can run purely to absorb electricity and dump the heat through available cooling capacity, a mode the Oslo system uses at Klemetsrud (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). 

Large-scale compression heat pumps extract thermal energy from a low-grade source and upgrade it to district heating supply temperature. A European survey of $149$ units totalling $1580 MW$ of thermal output records seven heat-source types in service, of which sewage water is the largest at $891 MW$, or $56\%$ of surveyed capacity across $54$ units, followed by ambient sea, lake and river water at $390 MW$, or $24\%$ across $34$ units; industrial waste heat, geothermal water, flue gas, district cooling and solar heat storage account for the remainder \cite{david_heat_2017}. Coefficients of performance (COP) under typical operating conditions lie between 3 and 4, representing the average practical COP achieved across a year rather than a seasonal mean, with individual surveyed units ranging from 2.65 at two geothermal plants in Milano, where the difference between heat-pump inlet and outlet reaches 75 degrees, up to 6.5 where the temperature increase is small \cite{david_heat_2017}. Capital costs of heat pumps are substantially higher than those of electrode boilers. The Danish Energy Agency technology catalogue puts a 20 MW seawater heat pump at 1.19 MEUR per MW of heat capacity and a 65 MW unit at 0.94 MEUR per MW, central estimates in 2025-EUR excluding taxes, against 0.07 MEUR per MW for a large electric boiler \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}. A dispatch optimisation of a hypothetical 260 MW heat-pump fleet in the Greater Copenhagen system, sized to cover roughly a tenth of the winter peak, assumes demand-averaged COPs of $2.9-3.2$ for distribution-connected units and $2.5-2.6$ at transmission level, calculated for a two-stage ammonia compression cycle, and finds distribution-connected heat pumps operating for roughly $3000$ to $3500$ full load hours in both the 2013 and 2025 scenarios, falling to $2000$ to $2500$ hours when connected to the transmission network \cite{bach_integration_2016}.

The lower electrical input per unit of thermal output, relative to electrode boilers, reduces the heat pump's price exposure. Its contribution to flexibility is constrained more by the economics and mechanical limits of cycling than by ramp rate: as the temperature lift between source and supply widens, COP deteriorates, and frequent start-stop operation accelerates compressor wear. So while electrode boilers can provide instantaneous up- and down-regulation, large-scale heat pumps have generally not been designed for frequent flexible regulation \cite{david_heat_2017}. A European survey of large-scale district heating heat pumps found the newest units running continuously at 7000 to 8000 hours per year and supplying part of the base load, others operating seasonally at 4000 to 7000 hours, and very few dispatching intermittently on the electricity price, though the survey notes that operating-hour data was sparse and the role of these units in their networks could not be clearly established \cite{david_heat_2017}. Electric boilers respond instantaneously, within 30 seconds, whereas large-scale heat pumps carry some lead time before becoming fully operational, so the two technologies suit different segments of the reserve markets rather than competing for the same products \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}. Catalogue regulation figures make the tiering concrete: a 20 MW seawater heat pump regulates about 5\% of full load per 30 seconds from a 25\% minimum load, needs half-hour warm and cold starts, and incurs a startup cost near 10.6 EUR per MW of heat capacity, while a 65 MW unit reaches about 65\% per 30 seconds from the same minimum load \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}.

The IEA HPT Annex 57 final report quantifies this tiering through the Sdr. Felding pilot in Denmark, in which a $1.3 MW$ heat pump records a start-up time of approximately seven minutes and a turn-down time of around four minutes, placing heat-pump participation in the aFRR window rather than the faster, seconds-scale windows accessible to electrode boilers, namely the Frequency Containment Reserve (FCR) and Fast Frequency Reserve (FFR) \cite{v_pedersen_annex_2024}.

Combined heat and power plants (CHP), including waste-to-energy (WtE) incineration facilities, occupy a structurally different position within the DH flexibility stack. Rather than being dispatchable against an electricity price signal, they operate in a heat-led mode. Thermal output is the primary production target, driven by waste throughput requirements or heat demand contracts, and electricity is generated as a by-product at a fixed or minimally adjustable heat-to-power ratio \cite{beiron_combined_2020}. Flexibility provision from CHP and WtE is accordingly indirect, achieved through two mechanisms: adjusting the heat-to-power extraction ratio within the operating range of the turbine cycle, and buffering variable heat production against demand using thermal storage to decouple generation from consumption in time \cite{wang_investigation_2019}. Catalogue minimum loads bound the inflexibility: the waste-fired heat-only boiler cannot be turned down below $70\%$ of full load and needs 8 hours for a warm start and 12 hours for a cold start, against a datasheet minimum load of $20\%$ and warm and cold starts of 0.5 and 2 hours for waste-fired CHP, whose furnace the catalogue nonetheless states can be down-regulated only to about $70\%$ of nominal capacity before steam quality and flue-gas residence-time requirements bind, electricity output remaining adjustable at short notice through full or partial turbine bypass \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}. 

Waste incineration supplies roughly half of all Norwegian district heating, the highest share amongst the Nordic countries \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. In Oslo, Hafslund Celsio's Klemetsrud WtE plant is the dominant base-load source in the system merit order, operating continuously with thermal output driven by waste-incineration throughput rather than by electricity market signals (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). Recovered heat, a category that in this statistic combines waste incineration with industrial surplus heat, supplied $58\%$ of the operator's 2025 production \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}. The plant is treated throughout as a heat-only unit for the purposes of this thesis. It does generate electricity: steam turbines on the incineration process produced 123 GWh in 2025 against 149 GWh in 2024 \cite{hafslund_arsrapport_nodate}. That output follows waste throughput rather than a dispatch decision, so it is treated as a by-product of the base-load heat duty rather than as a power-to-heat flexibility asset, and no flexibility value is attributed to it. The consequence for the value-gap argument is developed in \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap}.
This heat-led dispatch logic means that WtE capacity constrains the flexibility stack rather than contributing to it directly. Its inflexibility is compensated by the electrode boiler, heat-pump capacity and accumulator-tank storage. \autoref{fig:2.2.2 flexibility envelope} places the three technology classes side by side on the catalogue's own measures -- primary regulation per thirty seconds, minimum load, and warm and cold start times -- and the ordering it shows is the one the dispatch model encodes: only the electrode boiler meets mFRR activation requirements at these start times, which is why reserve capability is assigned to it alone in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model}.

\subsubsection{Thermal Energy Storage}
\label{2-subsec:2.2.3 thermal storage}

The dedicated thermal storage identified in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions} as the supply-side FTS buffer is what converts the power-flexibility action into a resource that can be shifted across time. It spans a wide envelope of power and duration, from short-duration accumulators suited to day-ahead arbitrage to seasonal stores that decouple summer collection from winter demand. Where an asset sits on that envelope fixes which flexibility products it can bid into, following the buffer-parameter logic of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}. \autoref{fig:2.2.3 storage typology} plots the storage classes on those two axes, power capacity against storage duration, and places the Haraldrud accumulator in the intra-day region where the commercial market channels operate.

Thermal accumulator tanks store pressurised hot water at district heating supply temperature inside insulated vessels. They are inexpensive among energy storage options: hot-water tanks cost between 50 and 100 times less per unit of stored energy than electrical storage \cite{hennessy_flexibility_2019}. Their round-trip thermal efficiency is high: the Danish Energy Agency technology catalogue reports a full-cycle round-trip efficiency of $98\%$ (uncertainty band $96-99\%$) and a standby loss of about $0.2\%$ per day for large-scale steel accumulator tanks \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}. 

Charge and discharge rates vary widely across the class: short-term thermal storage is generally taken to span storage periods from an hour up to a few days \cite{hennessy_flexibility_2019}, while the Danish Energy Agency reference tank of $175 MWh$ is dimensioned on a $60$-hour full-charge cycle, a rate set in practice by pipe dimensions and valve count rather than by the store itself \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}. Where the rate is high enough, the accumulator is what makes market operation possible: the Oslo operator charges the tank in cheap hours, typically overnight, discharges it into the demand peak in winter, and in summer uses it to absorb energy arriving from an mFRR activation (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). The connected electrode boiler can deliver across the full activation window rather than only as long as the heat demand profile permits. Oslo's Haraldrud facility, commissioned in 2023, is registered in its district heating licence as a single tank of $8{,}500 m^3$, 30 metres high and 22 metres in diameter, with an installed rating of $98 MW$ \cite{nve_konsesjonssak_2026}. Usable energy capacity is not a licensed quantity, because it depends on the temperature spread the operator actually exploits; the operator states an ideal storage capacity of approximately $550 MWh$ and momentary charge and discharge of up to about $100 MW$ (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). Those two figures together set the store's duration: at full discharge the nameplate capacity carries roughly five and a half hours of its own rated output, and the energy that can actually be cycled is less, because the top and bottom of a hot-water tank carry the thermocline and the return-temperature layer. \autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope} applies a usable fraction to the nameplate and works with just under five hours on that basis. Either way the store sits firmly in the intra-day class rather than the seasonal one, which fixes the time horizon over which it can arbitrage.

Accumulator tanks are common in Danish, Finnish, and Swedish district heating but have historically been more scarce in Norway \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. The Danish tank fleet, roughly 875 000 m\textsuperscript{3} across 284 plants in 2013 and about 50 GWh usable in 2017, also illustrates how enablers drive storage adoption: most tanks at small-scale CHP plants were designed for operation under a three-part electricity tariff that no longer applies, and the fleet's role is shifting from one defined by the electricity market toward facilitating fluctuating renewable production \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}. Outside Oslo, large-scale accumulator capacity in the Norwegian systems is limited: the Norwegian tanks recorded in a national scenario study for the building stock run to $280 MWh$ at Hamar, $350 MWh$ at Heimdal, $117 MWh$ at Gardermoen and $60 MWh$ at Bodø, and not every Norwegian district heating system has a tank at all \cite{SINTEF_energy_2023}, a technical asymmetry treated as a scope boundary in \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}. Dedicated vessels are not the only thermal store available to the operator. The water volume inside the supply and return pipes is itself an energy store, charged and discharged by varying supply temperature and mass flow rather than by any dedicated asset, and bounded in horizon by the time hot water takes to travel a pipeline segment \cite{mitridati_power_2018}. Network thermal mass therefore sits at the low-power, short-duration corner of the storage envelope, and the storage-equivalence it provides to the power system is the mechanism already introduced in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.3 sector coupling}.

Seasonal thermal energy storage technologies, principally pit thermal energy storage (PTES) and borehole thermal energy storage (BTES), operate on charge-discharge cycles of weeks to months, enabling the temporal decoupling of summer heat collection or waste-heat availability from winter heating demand. The four canonical underground storage typologies (tank, pit, borehole and aquifer) differ substantially in energy density: tank TES holds roughly $60-80\frac{kWh}{m^3}$, gravel-water PTES roughly $30-50\frac{kWh}{m^3}$, BTES around $15-30\frac{kWh}{m^3}$, and ATES around $30-40\frac{kWh}{m^3}$ \cite{dahash_advances_2019}. The pit figure depends on the storage medium: a gravel-water pit needs roughly half again the volume of a water-only pit for equivalent stored energy \cite{dahash_advances_2019}, and a water-filled pit is reported at $60-80\frac{kWh}{m^3}$, the same band as the tank \cite{sipos_power_heat_2025}. Round-trip efficiencies are operationally lower than for short-duration accumulators and depend strongly on the storage utilisation cycle: a five-year-average measured storage efficiency of $90.1\%$ has been reported for the Dronninglund PTES at a high utilisation cycle of 2.16, against approximately $66\%$ for the Marstal PTES operated at a cycle close to 1.0 \cite{pan_long-term_2022}. Power ratings are modest relative to storage volume: typical PTES charging and discharging capacities have run $5-50 MW$ against stores holding thousands of MWh per full cycle, and the rate at which that capacity can be changed is set by the pumps, valves and inlet and outlet capacity outside the store rather than by the store itself, at $0.2-0.5 MW$ per minute in the Høje Taastrup installation, so the catalogue frames PTES participation in the electricity market as indirect, through price-driven charging and discharging, rather than as reserve provision \cite{danish_energy_agency_energy_2025}. Denmark has deployed large-scale PTES extensively, with solar-coupled installations such as Vojens ($200\,000\ m^3$) and Dronninglund ($60\,000\ m^3$) \cite{dahash_advances_2019}, \cite{pan_long-term_2022}.

The levelised cost of stored heat at the $100\,000\ m^3$ scale runs from about $130\frac{\euro}{MWh}$ for an accumulation tank to about $160\frac{\euro}{MWh}$ for the shallow pit, under European cost conditions \cite{dahash_techno-economic_2021}. In Norway, seasonal DH storage remains primarily at the demonstration stage. A neighbourhood-scale case study at Furuset in Oslo finds that district heating alone reduces peak electricity demand by approximately $28\%$ relative to direct electric heating, while adding borehole seasonal thermal storage reduces peak district heating demand by a further $31\%$ and cuts $CO_2$ emissions by $20\%$ below the direct-electric-heating baseline \cite{kauko_assessing_2022}. The seasonal-storage configuration is not cost-saving under baseline conditions, however: it carries the lowest operational cost but the highest capital cost, leaving total system cost approximately $3\%$ above the electric-heating reference. It becomes competitive only when winter electricity prices double or when available grid capacity is reduced by more than $9 MW$ below the site's baseline of $22.6 MW$, a capacity the authors describe as unusually high and present as a special case rather than a representative neighbourhood. These figures are therefore indicative at neighbourhood scale and do not transfer directly to system scale. 

The commercial case for seasonal storage splits by value channel. Through the market channels available in the NO1 price environment the binding constraint is power rather than energy: a Norwegian techno-economic study of seasonal storage parameterises discharge power at about $0.5\frac{kW}{MWh}$ of storage capacity for BTES and about $3.4\frac{kW}{MWh}$ for PTES, against roughly $118\frac{kW}{MWh}$ for a tank thermal store \cite{tim_maarten_riesen_techno-economic_2025}, so mFRR activation and short-horizon arbitrage reach only a small fraction of a seasonal store's capital base, and this thesis therefore carries PTES and BTES into the valuation framework of \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} primarily on the societal side. A Norwegian residential study quantifies this dynamic under the Norwegian monthly-peak grid tariff structure: co-optimising seasonal thermal storage with the monthly-peak capacity tariff achieves a $4.3\%$ total-cost reduction relative to a tariff-only baseline, whereas a storage-only strategy that ignores the tariff signal produces a $69\%$ higher annual cost than the co-optimised case \cite{thorvaldsen_long-term_2024}. The mechanism transfers in direction to the DH operator setting: tariff-unaware storage operation systematically under-performs, and the value of storage depends on accessible flexibility products and tariff signals rather than on stored capacity alone. Through the fuel-displacement channel the direction reverses: a cost-minimising analysis of seasonal storage in a Norwegian district heating system, charging on otherwise-vented excess heat and displacing expensive peaking fuel, finds positive net present value with discounted paybacks of 5 to 20 years across price scenarios \cite{tim_maarten_riesen_techno-economic_2025}. The direction transfers; the magnitudes do not, since that analysis contains no operator perimeter, no market-access terms and no capacity charge. Its labelling of a system-cost saving as (business-economically profitable) is itself an instance of the perspective conflation that \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} formalises.

The value attributed to any thermal storage asset is bounded by the flexibility products available for the stored energy to be sold into, the enabler layer of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}. A review of power-to-heat and thermal storage participation in electricity markets, focused on the Finnish system, finds that the benefit a thermal store draws from the day-ahead and intraday markets rests largely on charging at times of lower electricity prices and discharging when prices are higher, and concludes that power-to-heat coupled with thermal storage needs additional income, for example from ancillary service markets, to secure profitable operation \cite{jaanto_thermal_2023}. This finding applies directly to the Norwegian DH context. Without a flexibility product that compensates the district heating operator for system services beyond mFRR capacity payments, the commercial value of storage investment is anchored on the NO1 spot-price variance quantified in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power}, where the mean within-day spread matched to a day-cycle store runs to $314$ NOK/MWh in 2024 against $809$ NOK/MWh in DK1. The societal value of the same storage assets accrues elsewhere: storage and optimised electric-boiler dispatch can increase the load relief the district heating system gives the grid, yet none of the gain from avoided or deferred network investment reaches the district heating company, falling instead to the network companies and their customers; contributions to local scarcity and flexibility needs go insufficiently rewarded for want of local markets; and the NO1 price reduction that follows from lower electric heating load accrues to electricity consumers \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. The relationship between storage value and market design architecture is therefore a structural feature of the commercial-/socio-economic gap, not a transient market condition.

\subsection{Barriers to Flexibility in District Heating}
\label{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}

This section explains why the flexibility resource documented in \autoref{2-subsec:2.2 DH as flexibility} earns so little of the value it creates: it catalogues the barriers that separate the operator's economic return on flexibility provision from its system-level societal value. The catalogue is used twice downstream. The dispatch model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} encodes the economic barriers as tariff and market-access terms, and the gap attribution in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} assigns the measured gap across the barrier categories established here. The corpus on district heating flexibility converges on a recurring set of mechanisms spanning tariff design, market-product gaps, regulatory frictions, and institutional arrangements. The most systematic ordering comes from \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}, which categorises them into a taxonomy of forty barriers grouped into nine categories: operational signalling, investment, permitting, ownership, district-energy technology conditions, grid access, physical environment, bounded rationality, and acceptance. This thesis collapses those nine categories into the four barrier types that organise the subsections below, mapping district-energy technology conditions, physical environment, and grid access onto technical barriers, \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers}, operational signalling and investment onto economic and market barriers, \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, permitting and ownership onto regulatory and policy barriers, \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}, and bounded rationality and acceptance onto institutional barriers, \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.4 institutional barriers}. \autoref{fig:2.3 barrier collapse} draws the mapping, with the grid-access category's qualified assignment marked.

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Graphics/2. Theory/2.3 - Sneum 9 to 4 barrier collapse map; FIG-05.png}
    \caption{The nine barrier categories of the district energy--electricity interface taxonomy, collapsed into the four barrier types used in this section \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. Thirty-four of the taxonomy's forty individual barriers affect power-to-heat and cooling; only two concern thermal storage.}

These four types are exhaustive for the thesis: the gap attribution in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} quantifies within them and introduces no barrier not catalogued here, so any barrier the results surface belongs in this section first. The four-way collapse is this thesis's own analytical construction and not a grouping \cite{sneum_barriers_2021} proposes: that study's contribution is the forty barriers and the nine categories holding them, and its conclusion argues explicitly for a finer sub-division of categories than the regulation-and-economy split common in the literature it reviews. One of the four assignments carries a qualification. Grid access is placed under technical barriers because network capacity, rather than connection pricing or connection rules, is the binding constraint in the Oslo case; the category's connection-cost dimension is developed in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} and its grid-code dimension in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}. \cite{sneum_barriers_2021} identifies the absence of a signal-providing scheme as the single barrier sufficient on its own to suppress flexibility, and finds that barriers of national origin dominate the taxonomy, which places the primary responsibility for removing them with national policy-makers rather than with the individual operators. In the resource-and-enabler terms of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}, every barrier in this taxonomy is a missing or adverse \textit{enabler}. The flexibility resource exists physically in the operator's electrode boilers, heat pumps, and accumulator, but markets, reserve products, and network tariffs either fail to reward its activation or actively penalise it. Degefa et al. and Sæle et al. make this split explicit, classifying markets, regulation, and tariffs as enablers separate from the flexibility resource itself \cite{degefa_comprehensive_2021}, \cite{saele_understanding_2023}. The four barrier types below are therefore a catalogue of the enablers that are absent or misaligned in the Norwegian case. \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.5 cross-country comparison} then sets the Norwegian condition under each barrier type against Denmark and Sweden, the two Nordic systems against which Norwegian district heating is most often measured.

\subsubsection{Technical Barriers}
\label{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers}

Technical barriers open the catalogue because they bound what the other three types can be blamed for: a constraint the hardware imposes is not a policy failure, and isolating the technical layer first keeps the economic and regulatory attribution in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} clean. The most visible technical constraint on district heating flexibility provision in Norway is the limited deployment of large-scale thermal storage. The Haraldrud accumulator, commissioned in 2023, is Oslo's principal dedicated DH storage asset \cite{nve_konsesjonssak_2026}. Large-scale accumulator capacity elsewhere in the Norwegian system remains limited, confining many operators to within-hour load modulation rather than the multi-hour to day-ahead dispatch optimisation that storage enables \cite{hennessy_flexibility_2019}, \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. Hafslund Celsio's Oslo system, with both a large P2H fleet and dedicated storage, represents a ceiling of Norwegian DH flexibility potential rather than a median, so findings from the Oslo case do not transfer directly onto other Norwegian operators without adjustments in their physical flexibility assets. 

New P2H capability additions face grid connection constraints that delay deployment and cap installed capacity below the economically optimal level. The broad electrification push across sectors has loaded distribution-grid networks and expanded connection queues. In Oslo, applications for roughly $1000MW$ of new electricity load stand against only about $200MW$ of reserved grid capacity, so a DH operator seeking to add electrode boiler capacity may face multi-year connection timelines and capacity restrictions \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. This caps the P2H capacity an operator can install below economically optimal levels, independent of the downstream market-access question.
The queue impact on power-to-heat is operator-confirmed: new electric-boiler connections above 5 MW route into the connection queue, the operator installs boilers from 10 MW upward, and its smallest planned boiler is deliberately below 5 MW to stay outside the queue (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). The statutory machinery is the maturity assessment for connection requests of 1 MW and above, with reserved capacity for smaller customers and a unilateral downgrade provision, in the network-regulation code \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2019}.

Network thermal dynamics, including supply temperature frameworks, pipe thermal inertia, and distribution-side control constraints, impose physical limits on the ramp rates achievable through network-level flexibility activation. These are noted as a boundary condition of the economic feasibility in \autoref{3-subsec:3.10 limitations}, but are not subject to detailed analysis in this thesis \cite{vandermeulen_controlling_2018}, \cite{guelpa_demand_2021}.

Across the literature, technical barriers are consistently treated as the most tractable of the four barrier types. Of the forty barriers categorised by \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}, only two concern thermal storage, which the author characterises as a "no-regrets technology", often seen as the default go-to investment to increase system performance. The bulk of the gap lies in the economic, regulatory, and institutional categories that follow \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}.

\subsubsection{Economic and Market Barriers}
\label{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}

The economic and market barriers are treated here because they are the ones the quantitative work can price. Where the technical barriers of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} bound what the assets can physically do, the barriers below bound what the operator earns for doing it, and each maps onto a term the dispatch model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} carries: the capacity charge onto the grid-tariff term and reserve-market access onto the reserve-revenue term. The regulated price ceiling is the exception that proves the rule, and it is why it is treated qualitatively in \autoref{3-subsec:3.7 gap and attribution}: because heat demand enters the model as a delivery obligation, heat revenue is invariant to dispatch and the ceiling gets no arithmetic row at all. Three mechanisms are taken in turn - the monthly-peak capacity charge and the interruptible terms that condition it, the reserve-market access regime that has replaced the bid-size threshold as the binding constraint for a large operator, and the absence of any product that pays for the system services falling outside it - and \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} attributes the measured gap across them.

The single most cited economic barrier in the Norwegian DH flexibility corpus is the capacity charge, the monthly peak-load capacity component of Elvia's distribution grid tariff. For the business classes with annual consumption above 100{,}000 kWh, in which a district heating operator of Hafslund Celsio's scale sits, the capacity charge is assessed on the single highest measured power draw within each calendar month, regardless of when that peak occurs or what system conditions prevailed at the time \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}. The regulation permits either a single-maximum or an averaged-peaks settlement basis, and the large-customer schedules use the single-maximum form \cite{rme_nettleie_2025}. The regulator itself has acknowledged that this settlement basis can yield a marginal network cost that does not reflect actual network costs, when the monthly peak falls in hours with available capacity \cite{eriksen_proposed_2022}, \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}.  The Norwegian Energy Regulatory Authority (RME) put that basis in question while consulting on a capacity-based tariff for low-voltage customers: it proposed a daily rather than a monthly peak for that class, suggested that larger commercial customers also be provided with a daily-peak basis from 2021, and asked respondents whether the monthly peak should still be used in settlement for certain customers at all \cite{eriksen_proposed_2022}. The consultation's remit was the low-voltage distribution grid, so it is not authority for the high-voltage business schedule under which a district heating operator of Hafslund Celsio's scale is billed, where the monthly-peak basis set out above still applies \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}, \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}. For a district heating operator that uses electric boilers to store thermal energy during low-price hours, a load increase that exceeds the month's running maximum resets the settlement peak and raises the capacity charge in proportion to the amount by which it exceeds it, for the whole of that month; a load increase that stays below the running maximum costs nothing. One cheap-hour charging event therefore carries the entire tariff cost, and every later event in the same month is free. \autoref{fig:2.3.2 effektledd timeline} traces that mechanism across a single settlement month. This holds even when the same load increase reduces the aggregate system peak and defers grid reinforcement at the network level. The tariff thereby penalises precisely the flexibility behaviour it should incentivise. The observation is not an operator-side complaint alone: Statnett's own 2018 Nordic flexibility outlook names the mechanism, noting that the capacity charge can prevent electric boilers from consuming even at very low power prices \cite{holmefjord_fleksibilitet_2018}, six years before the operator-commissioned THEMA assessment. For a Norwegian DH plant modelled on Oslo load data, the electric-boiler share of annual heat generation varies from $7\%$ to $17\%$ under a hydropower-dominated price pattern and from $2\%$ to $13\%$ under a thermal-dominated one, driven by the grid tariff structure applied, before any market-access barrier is considered \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}. \autoref{fig:2.3.2 eb share by tariff} plots the four tariff designs under both price patterns; the demand-charge columns are the effektledd case this subsection develops. The mechanism generalises beyond Norway, though the Norwegian form is unusually severe. For the Danish system, conventional grid tariffs can "obliterate the price signals from the wholesale electricity market" \cite{skytte_design_2017}. In that 2014 Danish case, an electric boiler is the cheapest heat source in only about $6\%$ of annual hours, a share that rises to roughly $74\%$ under a capacity-only tariff with no volumetric component, and to about $22\%$ under a capacity event-based design, where a charge levied only in grid-constrained hours removes hours of operation rather than raising the cost threshold. \autoref{fig:2.3.2 competitive window} draws the mechanism behind those shares: each tariff design shifts the spot-price threshold below which the electric boiler is the cheapest heat source, and the boiler's competitive window widens or narrows with it. The Norwegian capacity charge is a monthly-peak capacity charge rather than the volumetric per-kWh structure analysed there. The mechanism therefore differs in form and is arguably stronger: the monthly-peak settlement injects intra-month decision uncertainty on top of the constant wedge a volumetric tariff imposes. The direction of the distortion is nonetheless identical. Across the Nordic system, volumetric grid tariffs suppress a substantial volume of cross-sector flexibility investment: a revenue-neutral capacity tariff would unlock on the order of a $20\%$ increase in power-to-heat capacity and a $7\%$ increase in storage \cite{bergaentzle_cross-sector_2022}. Hafslund Celsio is connected at distribution level and faces Elvia's capacity charge on top of the transmission tariff modelled in that study, so the suppression of Oslo-specific flexibility value may be larger still. A comparable conclusion holds at plant level: a capacity-component tariff effectively eliminates electric-boiler operation in Norway and Finland, because a few low-price boiler hours trigger a monthly charge that outweighs the fuel saving \cite{sneum_economic_2018}. The two results are not in conflict. The Norwegian tariff comparison above holds the tariff level constant, calibrating all four structures to equal distribution system operator revenue, so it isolates the effect of structure alone \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}. Its suppression result is also seasonal rather than annual: the monthly-peak structure removes electric-boiler generation from the winter weeks entirely, even in hours of negative price, because the winter demand-charge rate is more than ten times the summer rate and is indifferent to when within the month the peak falls \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}. The plant-level result applies the actual Norwegian tariff level on top of that structure. Structure decides how much of the year the boiler can run in; level decides whether the remainder is worth running at all.

The interruptible (utkoblbar) tariff is the standard escape from the capacity charge, but it is not a regulated entitlement. The dedicated utkoblbar chapter of the tariff regulation was deleted on 1 July 2012, and the discount has since been discretionary: the DSO may offer it where it has a relevant network need for interruptible reserve and sets the level itself, but must offer it on general terms rather than negotiate case by case \cite{rme_nettleie_2025}, \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}. In practice it is the sector norm: most Norwegian district heating companies run their electric boilers on interruptible terms \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fakta_nodate, norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}, and the Oslo incumbent's entire boiler fleet operates on such terms at an effective volumetric rate of approximately 5 øre/kWh, with the disconnection option barely exercised over five years because price-based self-curtailment moves the boilers off the grid before the DSO needs to call (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). The capacity-charge barrier is therefore conditional rather than absolute. It binds firm-connection consumers and operators without interruptible terms \cite{sneum_economic_2018}, and it binds capacity additions, which face the successor regime of connection on condition (tilknytning på vilkår), where curtailment is a condition of connection and carries no compensation \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2019}. For the incumbent's legacy interruptible connections it is largely neutralised. Interruptible status is not in itself a bar to selling flexibility, but it conditions the sale. The approved balancing-market terms admit a regulating object that sits on reduced tariff for flexible consumption or on connection on terms, and impose three obligations in exchange: the provider must document, before applying, that it has notified the affected network company and agreed notification routines with it for volume clearing in the capacity and activation markets; it must correct its activation-market bids continuously for volume that a network disconnection makes unavailable; and where grid or operating conditions prevent activation of an already-accepted capacity bid, its deviation factor is set to one rather than two \cite{statnett_forslag_2025}. The arrangement that makes the operator's grid access cheap therefore carries an administrative and market-conduct cost on the reserve side rather than an exclusion. Danish distribution system operators institutionalise the same arrangement from the network side, offering electric boilers limited grid access with a negotiable minimum load \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}.

Sneum's taxonomy carries high grid-connection cost as a barrier in its own right, placed in the grid-access category and distinct from the tariff barriers grouped under operational signalling \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. The distinction matters because the two bear on different decisions: the capacity charge is a recurring cost that conditions how an existing asset is dispatched, whereas the connection charge is a one-off capital cost that conditions whether the asset is built at all. Its magnitude is not incidental to the investment case. The Danish technology catalogue reports grid-connection fees together with transformer and distribution-board costs inside the investment cost of an electric boiler, so the charge for obtaining access is carried as part of the plant's capital cost rather than recovered through operation \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}. In the Norwegian case the connection-cost dimension compounds the queue and capacity constraints of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} rather than substituting for them: an operator seeking new power-to-heat capacity faces both a wait for available network capacity and a charge for the connection that releases it, and the cheaper alternative on offer, connection on terms of disconnection, lowers the recurring charge while carrying no compensation for the curtailment it permits \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2019}. The barrier is economic in substance and falls on capacity addition rather than on dispatch, which is why it is developed here rather than among the technical constraints where the grid-access category as a whole is placed.  This does not make the capacity charge a barrier simply to be abolished. Capacity-charge designs are not inherently barrier-forming. Removing the capacity component while retaining a dynamic energy signal lets assets with high thermal mass pre-heat ahead of high-price periods, which simply shifts peaks rather than reducing them \cite{wilczynski_assessment_2023}. Some capacity signal is therefore needed for grid-peak suppression. The barrier lies in the specific peak-based monthly structure of the Norwegian capacity charge, not in capacity pricing. The design flaw is in which peak the tariff targets. Norway already runs a capacity tariff designed not to distort at another voltage level: the transmission and regional-network capacity tariff settles on reference hours that, by design, shall so far as possible not be predictable to the customer, which removes the incentive to shave the settlement peak itself \cite{rme_nettleie_2025}. The contrast sharpens the critique: the distortion is a distribution-level design choice, not an inherent property of capacity pricing. On a real Oslo case, a capacity-based tariff alone incentivises individual peak reduction without coordinating the coincident peak that actually drives grid capacity needs \cite{askeland_activating_2021}. Closing the gap between individual and coincident peak behaviour therefore requires a complementary local capacity-trading mechanism that the Norwegian system currently lacks. Oslo demand-response data corroborate this: the individual-peak, ex-post settlement of the capacity charge performs worst for substation-level peak reduction, even as it performs best at the individual-customer level \cite{hofmann_grid_2025}. The tariff nonetheless materially governs storage-coupled dispatch. Co-optimising a Norwegian building's seasonal-storage operation against the monthly measured-peak tariff, by absorbing cheap summer electricity to lower the costlier winter peak charge, reduces annual operating cost by $4.3\%$ and the monthly-peak tariff bill by $23.9\%$ relative to operation without storage \cite{thorvaldsen_long-term_2024}. The residential scale of these cases does not transfer numerically to the district heating operator. The mechanism, however, applies directly to Hafslund Celsio's electrode-boiler and accumulator dispatch under the capacity charge. Finally, the capacity charge does not bind all assets equally. A monthly-maximum load-demand tariff of the Oslo type is the worst tariff for electric-boiler deployment but the best for heat pumps: base load heat-pump operation spreads the capacity charge across many hours, whereas intermittent boiler dispatch concentrates it into a few. Tariff reform therefore releases more flexibility from electrode boilers than from heat pumps, the latter remaining base load regardless of tariff design unless a competing low-cost heat source emerges \cite{sandberg_energy_2019}, \cite{johannsen_incentivising_2021}.

Until December 2025 the mFRR minimum bid threshold functioned as a structural exclusion mechanism, converting the cost asymmetry above into a permanent revenue gap. At $10 MW$ in NO2, NO4 and NO5 and $5 MW$ in NO1 and NO3 \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}, the floor placed reserve-market revenue out of reach for all but the largest aggregated capacity. As established in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power}, the cut of the mFRR bid floor to $1 MW$ with effect from 2 December 2025 removes the bid-size barrier as such \cite{statnett_1_2025}. The cut is not uniform across the reserve products. The approved terms set a minimum of $1 MW$ in steps of $1 MW$ for the aFRR capacity market and for both the mFRR capacity and mFRR activation markets, while the mFRR-D markets retain a $10 MW$ floor relaxed only by a single bid of between 5 and $9 MW$ per station group and regulation direction; and in every case the volume a provider may offer is bounded by the capacity approved for it in prequalification rather than by the bid minimum \cite{statnett_forslag_2025}. A single Hafslund Celsio electrode-boiler site now qualifies on scale alone. What remains is a revenue gap of a different origin. That gap is not about bid size. Modelling of power-to-heat bidding into a reserve capacity market finds the economics governed by the composition of the competing heat production: the maximum average bidding price an operator can offer before business-as-usual operation is cheaper falls from about USD 150 per MW of electrical capacity in a heat-only-boiler system to between roughly USD 60 and USD 83 where a biomass CHP unit supplies base load, while the balancing potential itself stays bounded by the installed power-to-heat capacity and the production strategy of the system; the study concludes that policies rewarding power-balancing services, alongside investment support for power-to-heat capacity and heat storage, are needed to overcome those limits \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}. Norwegian practitioner accounts point the same way, attributing under-participation to a complex flexibility value chain, capacity-charge exposure, and a competence and digitisation gap as trading roles emerge in district heating control rooms \cite{gronli_hvor_2025}. Two of the three barriers the practitioner account names are documented in the system operator's own filing: network clearance with the distribution system operator forms part of every prequalification case, and sub-megawatt resources can reach the market only through flexibility groups bidding as aggregated units of 1 to 5 MW, a route Statnett opened administratively rather than through a market framework, and one that reached operation only after the cap it accompanies: the flexibility register opened for registration on 8 June 2026, with prequalification applications accepted from 17 August, and \autoref{fig:2.3.2 capacity area regime} sets the resulting allocation against the operator's own position -- eleven areas of $3 MW$ each, of which at most $2 MW$ per area is reliably available to a single regulating object while the flexibility-group reservation runs, against roughly $29 MW$ connected and more than $200 MW$ applied for \cite{statnett_apning_2026, statnett_innforing_2026, statnett_sporsmal_2026}.

The reserve products also differ in how well district heating assets match them, so participation turns on asset capability rather than scale alone. Fast-ramping electrode boilers can serve the frequency-containment products, whereas the slower heat-pump units are confined to the automatic restoration (aFRR) and mFRR windows that respond on a timescale of minutes \cite{javanshir_operation_2022}, \cite{boldrini_role_2022}. For the large incumbent, the barrier has moved from bid size to allocated access. Statnett announced on 6 March 2026, and implemented on 23 April 2026, a cap on the volume of regulating capacity that may be prequalified in grids below 110 kV \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. Norway is divided into 92 capacity areas (kapasitetsområder), each capped at 3 MW of upward and 3 MW of downward regulation counted across FCR, aFRR and mFRR combined \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The cap covers the regional grid below 110 kV and the distribution grid connected beneath it, and already-prequalified volume is counted against each ceiling, so existing participation consumes headroom that new applications cannot then use. Statnett gives observability as the rationale: most of the grid below 110 kV lies outside its network model, leaving the system operator without a view of flow, capacity, outages or operating state, and therefore unable to assess congestion risk from reserves it cannot see. Asked directly whether the restriction constituted a change to balancing service provider terms under Article 18 of the Electricity Balancing Guideline, and whether it had been submitted to the regulator for approval, Statnett answered that the limits were introduced as grid-motivated practice within existing prequalification terms, under which applications may be refused on grid grounds, and as a measure it judges necessary to discharge its system-responsibility obligation \cite{statnett_sporsmal_2026}. The most binding access constraint in this case therefore entered force through operational discretion rather than through the regulatory-approval route that carried the reduction in the minimum bid size. Asked in the same forum whether the restriction was temporary friction or a structural barrier to distributed flexibility, Statnett described opening the grid below 110 kV as requiring a substantial improvement in data flow, modelling and coordination with the network companies, and assessed it as a structural challenge that will take time to resolve rather than a short-lived friction \cite{statnett_sporsmal_2026}. The system operator does not present the barrier as transitional. Until 31 December 2026, 1 MW of each area's 3 MW is reserved for flexibility groups, aggregations of sub-1 MW resources bidding as units of 1 to 5 MW. Two distinct 1 MW rules therefore operate at once, and they run in opposite directions. The first is the bid-size floor, which sets the smallest volume that may be offered. The second is an aggregation ceiling: only resources below 1 MW of controllable capacity may be pooled inside a flexibility group, on the system operator's stated reasoning that larger resources already have direct market access and that direct participation gives it precise information on location and network impact, usable for congestion management \cite{statnett_praktisering_2026}. The reserved megawatt is a floor rather than a fixed partition: a flexibility group may prequalify the full 3 MW where an area's cap is otherwise free, so what the reservation guarantees is that at most 2 MW per area is reliably available to a single large regulating object while it runs \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. It is thus an explicit carve-out for the small, holding a slice of each area's scarce allocation for resources that could not reach the market alone and correspondingly reducing the share available to the large single objects that can. NO1 holds 25 capacity areas and 75 MW in total, of which the Oslo and Akershus group covers 11 areas and 33 MW \cite{statnett_innforing_2026} - the ceiling that binds the Oslo district heating system. That 33 MW is the arithmetic sum of eleven independent per-area caps of 3 MW each rather than a poolable regional allowance, so it is reachable only by an actor with qualifying objects in every one of the eleven areas, which no single site is \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. Against it the operator reports approximately 29 MW of connected reserve capacity and more than 200 MW applied for prequalification (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). Statnett processes applications in the order received, treats clearance with the relevant distribution system operator as part of each case, and states that processing must be expected to run beyond normal deadlines \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The system operator's own practice document states the consequence directly for aggregated flexibility groups: where a capacity area's aggregate limit is reached, new flexibility groups cannot be prequalified in that area until the system operator judges that operation there can absorb a larger share \cite{statnett_praktisering_2026}. It does not extend the statement to single-metering-point regulating objects, although the capacity-area regime itself is not group-specific. The cap arrived while the entry threshold fell to 1 MW, so the regime widened eligibility and rationed allocation at the same time.

Two further provisions condition what an accepted bid is worth. The transmission system operator may suspend bids in the mFRR activation market where it judges their pricing not to be socio-economically efficient, taking the offered volume instead at the prevailing day-ahead price for the zone; and it may mark bids unavailable where activation would worsen a local bottleneck, in which case the provider receives no compensation for the volume withheld \cite{statnett_forslag_2025}. Both are ordinary system-operator safeguards, and both sharpen the asymmetry this section catalogues: the socio-economic criterion enters the balancing market explicitly, as a ground on which a commercial bid may be set aside, while no provision running the other way converts socio-economic value the operator creates into a payment it receives.  The reserve product exclusion is compounded by the absence of any market instrument through which district heating operators can be compensated for system services that fall outside the mFRR activation window. This is a missing-market problem. The heat market clears first and is myopic to its interactions with the electricity market, so the inter-temporal flexibility of a district heating network coupled with CHPs and heat pumps could be modelled as a virtual electricity storage. Generalising financial and physical storage rights, so that heat systems can quantify and offer that flexibility to the power system, is identified as a promising direction for market design rather than an existing product \cite{mitridati_heat_2020}. Without a bilateral flexibility contract or a purpose-designed capacity product, these services go effectively uncompensated. Norway did operate one narrow capacity payment of this kind: RKOM, the seasonal balancing-capacity option market for mFRR, paid for a guarantee of upward bids in the October to April season, and district heating participation in it was negligible on the evidence available \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}, \cite{langset_national_2021}. Availability for mFRR is now remunerated through the mFRR capacity market, whose terms were approved in October 2025 and entered into force on 2 December 2025 \cite{statnett_forslag_2025}. Beyond that capacity market, no Norwegian market mechanism offers a capacity payment for DH-side load flexibility besides the mFRR activation payment itself. Finnish work on thermal storage reaches the same conclusion for its own market, noting that remuneration for providing flexibility, or additional compensation for the network-balancing service that power-to-heat delivers, would have to be introduced as a new incentive \cite{jaanto_thermal_2023}. The THEMA report attributes the gap to specific regulatory-design choices rather than to a textbook market failure: district heating receives no payment for the grid-service value it provides, the regulated price cap is computed monthly rather than hourly so hourly flexibility revenue is blunted, and operators in effect self-finance the electricity price support (strømstøtte) scheme for their customers \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. THEMA's headline estimate of NOK 1-1.6 billion per year is the societal value of converting heat supply to district heating, that is, fuel substitution and peak reduction. This is a broader quantity than the flexibility-specific value this thesis isolates. The flexibility subset is correspondingly smaller, and it is the quantity \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} attributes across the barrier mechanisms.

The NO1 spot price structure sets a ceiling on the arbitrage revenue available to Oslo-based operators, independently of market-access barriers. The compression that produces this ceiling is measured against the thermally influenced Nordic reference zones rather than against the other Norwegian bidding zones: in 2024 NO1's day-ahead interquartile range was $375$ NOK/MWh against $639$ in DK1 and $523$ in SE3, and NO1 saw $50$ hours above $2000$ NOK/MWh against DK1's $196$ \cite{nord_pool_nord_nodate}, which narrows the price spread that drives P2H dispatch value. The zonal price-duration evidence is set out in \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics}. Capacity-based tariffs of the Norwegian type reduce effective electrode-boiler utilisation to low levels \cite{sandberg_energy_2019}. Combined with the compressed NO1 price spread established in \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics}, this means the flexibility investment cannot recover on commercial returns alone in the Norwegian context. That reinforces the case for complementary reserve-market access rather than sole reliance on spot-arbitrage revenue.

Beyond the regulated price ceiling itself, the price models through which Norwegian operators bill heat do not transmit flexibility value to customers. Conventional volume-based district heating price models are inadequate for a flexible supply-and-demand system, and price-model design directly governs an operator's ability to capture flexibility value \cite{lygnerud_capturing_2024}. The time-differentiated models that have supported flexibility investment elsewhere in the Nordic market are foreclosed in Norway, both by this price-model convention and by the alternative-cost ceiling in the Energy Act §5-5 examined in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}.

\subsubsection{Regulatory and Policy Barriers}
\label{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}

Grid code is a different species of regulatory barrier from the price and tax instruments that follow, and Sneum's taxonomy carries it as one: limiting grid codes stand beside connection cost and connection capacity in the grid-access category \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. In the Norwegian balancing regime the code takes the form of cumulative compliance conditions an operator must satisfy before a flexible asset may be offered at all. Under the terms for balancing service providers approved in October 2025 and in force from 2 December 2025, the firm must hold a trading licence from the regulator, a settlement agreement with the Nordic settlement body, the provider agreement with the transmission system operator, registration in the sector's communication register and an approved electronic-communication channel, and must either be the balance responsible party for the asset or hold an agreement with that party regulating energy compensation in both regulation directions \cite{statnett_forslag_2025}. On the asset side the operator must own the regulating object or hold a contractual right to dispose of it, have it prequalified under the European system-operation regulation, and have it assigned to a station group \cite{statnett_forslag_2025}. The station-group rules then constrain how a mixed portfolio may be assembled: objects in one group must share a bidding zone and a balance responsible party, consumption and generation may not be grouped together, different generation technologies may not share a group, and energy storage is placed in a group of its own unless an assessment of its planned use allows otherwise \cite{statnett_forslag_2025}. For an operator whose flexible assets span consumption, storage and generation at once, these are rules about how a portfolio may be presented to the market rather than about how much of it there is, and they bind independently of the bid-size and allocation constraints of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}.  District heating prices in Norway are governed under §5-5 of the Energy Act. The wording originates in the 1986 District Heating Act and was carried forward into the 1991 Energy Act, so there is no separate law governing district heating \cite{nve_nves_2024}. The provision caps the district heating price at the price of electric heating in the customer's supply area; the statute fixes the price level, not the tariff structure, and specifies no computation period, the monthly settlement being administrative practice \cite{energidepartementet_lov_1991}. The wording has stood unchanged since 2008, while the effektledd sits in secondary legislation, an asymmetry in reform path that \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications} returns to. This cap-style mechanism is designed to protect customers from monopoly pricing, but it structurally suppresses any flexibility-value signal that would otherwise flow through the heat price. The THEMA report identifies this regulatory framework as a primary source of misaligned incentives \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. An operator who invests in flexibility assets that reduce system peak load and enable lower electricity prices at the system level cannot recover that investment through higher heat prices, because the price ceiling adjusts downward with the electricity price the operator helped reduce. The 2024 statistics show the coupling in operation: household district heating averaged about 119 øre/kWh including VAT against an electricity reference of 127.5 øre/kWh, the cap binding at roughly $93\%$ of the benchmark, and sector revenue fell $12\%$ year on year as heat prices tracked electricity prices down \cite{boeng_mindre_2025}. A comparative review of European district heating price-regulation regimes finds that fixing the DH price to an external benchmark may produce heat-sale revenues that fail to cover true costs, threatening operation, maintenance and investment in new technologies, and that a price cap set too low may threaten a system's long-term economic sustainability \cite{odgaarda_review_2020}. The closest Danish quantification is consumer-side rather than operator-side: in Aalborg's 1961-1972 single-family stock the privately optimal level of heat savings falls short of the economically optimal level under the existing tariff design, and a modelled shift to a fully variable tariff narrows that shortfall without closing it \cite{djorup_district_2020}.

The electricity tax applied to P2H consumption constitutes a cost component that reduces the operating margin of electric boilers and heat pumps. The reduced Norwegian rate for district heating producers is 0.60 øre/kWh in both 2025 and 2026, against a standard rate of 7.13 øre/kWh in 2026; eligibility attaches to the legal entity through a fuel-mix test requiring at least half waste, bio, surplus heat and heat pumps, and electric boilers are not a qualifying source \cite{the_norwegian_tax_administration_arsrundskriv_nodate}.

The already-low absolute level of the Norwegian reduced rate means that further tax reduction has limited scope as a flexibility policy lever, independently of how the two countries compare. Relative to the market-access architecture of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, the electricity tax is a second-order barrier as an input cost, treated in the commercial model as a fixed operating-cost component rather than a primary gap mechanism. A modelling precedent underscores the point. In a Nordic wind-integration study, the Danish tax on heat produced by electricity, 67.4 €/MWh, was assumed away entirely in order to improve the feasibility of using electric boilers or heat pumps, with the fuel taxes on competing heat production zeroed alongside it so that the removal would not create a tax-driven advantage \cite{meibom_value_2007}. The assumption itself is the evidence: an input-side cost wedge on power-to-heat can be large enough that removing it is treated as a precondition for assessing the technology at all. In the Norwegian case the input-side wedge is small, on the order of one percent of the marginal cost of power-to-heat; the binding constraints sit in the market-access architecture analysed in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}. The tax matters instead through a second channel: successive cuts in the standard rate, from 16.13 øre/kWh in 2020 to 7.13 in 2026, lower the price of electric heating and thereby mechanically lower the §5-5 ceiling on heat revenue; the industry association puts the sector cost near NOK 500 million per year, a figure to be read as an interested party's position \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fakta_nodate}, \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}. The elavgift is second-order as an input cost and first-order as ceiling risk.

A Norway-specific regulatory distortion is the $CO_2$ levy applied to waste incineration. For 2025 the levy was set at NOK 908 per tonne of $CO_2$ for non-quota-liable emissions and NOK 182 per tonne for quota-liable (EU ETS) emissions \cite{stortinget_avfallsforbrenning_2025}. From 1 January 2026 the two-rate structure is replaced by a single rate of NOK 848 per tonne of $CO_2$, with quota-liable emissions exempted from the levy altogether alongside hazardous waste and emissions that are captured and stored \cite{stortinget_stortingsvedtak_2025}. The restriction to fossil-derived emissions is not new to that change: the vedtak for 2024 and for 2025 each provide that the levy does not cover emissions from combustion of waste containing no fossil material, and each leaves the basis of assessment to regulation rather than settling in the vedtak whether a mixed stream is charged in proportion to its fossil share \cite{stortinget_stortingsvedtak_nodate}. The levy's carbon-content basis sets it apart from the Swedish waste-incineration tax, which was levied per tonne of municipal solid waste until its abolition at the end of 2022 and therefore made no distinction between its fossil-derived and biogenic fractions \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}. It directly affects the operating economics of WtE-anchored DH systems such as Hafslund Celsio's Klemetsrud plant, by raising the marginal cost of the dominant heat source in the merit order \cite{hafslund_arsrapport_nodate}. Whether the plant is liable at all therefore turns on its quota status: a quota-liable plant pays nothing under this levy, while a non-quota-liable one pays the full rate on its fossil-derived emissions. The planned carbon capture at the plant, expected in operation from the third quarter of 2029 \cite{hafslund_arsrapport_nodate}, would exempt the captured share on the separate statutory ground of capture and storage \cite{stortinget_stortingsvedtak_2025}.

A rising WtE cost shifts the dispatch balance toward electric boilers and heat pumps, potentially increasing the economic attractiveness of P2H flexibility. It simultaneously compresses operator margins and may reduce the financial headroom for the capital investment that flexibility requires. The net effect on the commercial/socio-economic gap through this channel is directionally ambiguous, and is flagged as an open analytical question in \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work}.

The extension of the Norway Price (Norgespris) scheme to district heating customers represents a compounding policy barrier. The Norgespris programme, originally designed to cap residential electricity bills, was extended to district heating customers with effect from 1 October 2025 and is state-funded from that date; the statutory reference price is 40 øre/kWh excluding VAT (50 øre/kWh including VAT), settled as a two-way contract for difference against the monthly zonal spot price, for households only and mutually exclusive with strømstøtte \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2025}, \cite{nve_norgespris_2026}. The monthly settlement basis means that no hourly price signal reaches district heating bills, by construction. Prior to the extension the operator self-financed the strømstøtte equivalent for its customers. Hafslund Celsio's audited accounts record a district heating result after tax of NOK $-52$ million in 2025 and, once the accounting gain on the sale of Hafslund Fiber AS is stripped out, NOK $-133$ million in 2024 \cite{hafslund_arsrapport_nodate}. The revenue coupling that drives the exposure is documented independently of operator funding: across the sector's roughly fifty concession companies, returns on invested capital correlate with power prices at 77 to 85\%, and sector returns have run near the cost of capital in recent years \cite{jonas_erraia_lonnsomheten_2023}. The loss figures therefore support a value-capture claim, not an unprofitability claim: the gap this thesis quantifies concerns flexibility value the operator cannot capture, not district heating being loss-making. The regime change therefore shifts the burden from the operator's profit \& loss to the state budget. The price-ceiling mechanism in the Energy Act §5-5 \cite{energidepartementet_lov_1991} continues to apply, now indexed to a politically set retail electricity price rather than to a market price. The THEMA NOK 1-1.6 billion conversion-value figure is based on pre-Norgespris conditions. The post-October 2025 regulatory configuration removes one operator-side cost channel while preserving the underlying flexibility-value-capture gap, since the §5-5 ceiling still suppresses any flexibility-driven margin uplift the operator might otherwise realise.

\subsubsection{Institutional Barriers}
\label{2-subsec:2.3.4 institutional barriers}

Institutional barriers close the catalogue because they are the residual: what remains once tariffs, market access and statute are accounted for is how the actors themselves are organised -- aggregation, coordination between network operators, and ownership -- and it is the barrier type the quantitative model can least price, which the gap attribution in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} carries as a residual rather than a priced term. The most consequential institutional gap is the absence of a formal aggregation framework through which multiple sub-threshold DH operators could pool flexibility capacity to clear mFRR minimum bids collectively \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. The Swedish SthlmFlex pilot, run jointly by Svenska kraftnät, Vattenfall Eldistribution, and Ellevio from winter 2020/2021, demonstrated the technical and institutional architecture of multi-operator local flexibility coordination. It included a reduced mFRR minimum bid of 1 MW for SthlmFlex-linked bids, against the standard Swedish 5 MW threshold \cite{nyman_how_2026}. The pilot has since concluded with very low market liquidity. Ellevio's own assessment is that short-term LFMs are not a viable congestion-management instrument for Stockholm in the near term \cite{scrocca_local_2026}. The Swedish experience therefore demonstrates both the institutional template and its commercial fragility. Even with reduced bid thresholds and explicit TSO-DSO coordination, sellers found revenue insufficient to justify participation; one aggregator estimated LFM income at approximately $10\%$ of ancillary-market income for equivalent effort \cite{nyman_how_2026}. The Danish EcoGrid 2.0 field trial (800 residential customers on Bornholm, 36 real activations) provides complementary peer-reviewed evidence that the LFM mechanism is operationally feasible at scale \cite{heinrich_ecogrid_2020}. The qualification is that flexibility services act as an insurance product against rare-but-consequential overloading events, rather than as a continuous revenue stream. Twenty-five interviews across the parallel CoordiNet Uppland and Skåne LFM pilots corroborate this barrier inventory, finding that the main barriers were the difficulty of understanding LFM design and the extensive market knowledge participation demanded, with existing regulation, manual and time-consuming processes, and insufficient profitability following as secondary barriers, while monetary incentives proved less important as a driver than expected \cite{palm_drivers_2023}. A Norwegian DSO interview study reaches the same verdict from the buyer side. Distribution companies rate local flexibility markets below the prominence they receive in the academic literature, and report that qualitative concerns, most prominently availability, reliability, predictability, and controllability, together with the operational risks that the associated uncertainties create, weigh more heavily in their adoption decisions than cost-benefit calculations \cite{saele_understanding_2023}. In Norway, no equivalent framework exists. What is institutionally locked in is no longer a bid-size threshold but the access regime documented in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, and two features define it. The first is an information asymmetry between the transmission system operator and the grid beneath it: Statnett states that most of the regional grid is not part of its network model, that it has no view of flow, capacity, outages or operating state there, and that the volume caps exist to limit overload risk in a grid it does not monitor \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The second is queue length: applications are processed in the order received, network clearance with the relevant distribution system operator forms part of each case, and Statnett states that processing must be expected to run beyond normal deadlines; the Oslo operator reports applications pending well past that point (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). The two accounts describe one constraint from opposite ends, a system operator managing risk it cannot observe and an asset owner holding capacity it cannot monetise, and the public record does not adjudicate between them. The regulatory-design layer sits underneath with the regulator itself: RME's national reporting concedes that the revenue-cap cost-norm model can favour investment over operational or flexibility measures, and industry consultation rejected the 2019 neutrality fix \cite{ove_flataker_national_2020}. The direction of travel points toward earlier system-operator coordination rather than toward a market: Statnett states that it is examining an earlier grid-clearance step in the connection process, so that network conditions including capacity-area headroom could be settled when connection is applied for, though it reports neither a finished solution nor a timeline \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. A systematic review of 38 studies of flexibility in the district energy sector finds that the barriers to exploiting flexibility-enabling technologies in district energy are mostly not technical, and reports proposals for new intermediary roles, such as aggregators pooling small heat pumps to a scale at which flexibility becomes valuable, as part of the remedy \cite{selvakkumaran_how_2021}. Norway's position, where no such intermediary exists, exemplifies the wider pattern.

A second institutional gap is the structural disconnect between the distribution system operator (DSO) and the transmission system operator (TSO) in the attribution of flexibility value. Flexibility provided by Hafslund Celsio's electric boilers reduces peak load on Elvia's distribution network, deferring grid reinforcement investment and accruing value to Elvia and ultimately to grid users through lower tariffs. Simultaneously, mFRR activation of the same assets provides balancing services to Statnett at the transmission level. Neither value flow reaches the district heating operator through any existing mechanism. No Norwegian local flexibility market comparable to the SthlmFlex pilot bridges the distribution-level gap, and at the transmission level the mFRR market remunerates activated balancing energy only, not the distribution-network value that the same dispatch creates, as set out in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}. The result is that district heating flexibility assets operate in a dual-benefit landscape where neither institutional beneficiary compensates the provider.

A third institutional condition specific to the Oslo case is Hafslund Celsio's position within the Hafslund group, which holds assets across hydropower generation and district heating and a regional grid stake (Elvia), under City of Oslo ownership \cite{hafslund_interim_nodate}. In principle, vertical integration of this type could align incentives for flexibility by internalising the grid value that DH flexibility creates within the same corporate perimeter. In practice, Swedish evidence points to a deep-rooted focus on firm-level profits rather than benefits for the energy system at large \cite{fernqvist_district_2023}, and within a diversified group that logic would operate through group-level investment priorities that need not track the marginal socio-economic value of district heating flexibility. This is presented as a structural condition of the Oslo case study, not as an evaluative judgement on Hafslund's decision-making. Its practical significance for the flexibility gap is an open question, and one the operator interview \autoref{3-subsec:3.2 interviews} does not settle.

The comparison reveals a consistent structural pattern. Norway has not updated its regulatory and market architecture to reflect the changed system conditions in which district heating flexibility has become system-valuable. Denmark and Sweden have taken different institutional pathways, and the Nordic comparison shows grid tariff design to be decisive: energy-component-based tariffs increase electric-boiler operation, where capacity-component-based tariffs can eliminate it altogether \cite{sneum_economic_2018}. The Norwegian distance from the comparators on any individual dimension is moderate. The grid tariff is more restrictive than Danish tariff structures but not qualitatively different from Swedish capacity charges, and the mFRR bid floor is now identical across the Nordic markets, although the capacity-area caps on reserve prequalification have no Danish or Swedish counterpart. The distinctive feature of the Norwegian case is that the remaining dimensions stack simultaneously in the direction that suppresses commercial flexibility value, producing a compounding barrier geometry that neither comparator replicates. The Nordic framework-conditions literature identifies Norway as the outlier on district heating penetration rather than on flexibility conditions, explaining that gap mainly by stronger competition from alternative heating options and the low share of installed hydronic systems in Norwegian buildings \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. \begin{keypoint}
Norway's distance from Denmark and Sweden on most single dimensions of \autoref{tab:2.3.5 cross country comparison} is moderate; the anomaly is that the adverse conditions bind simultaneously: a conditional but still-binding tariff architecture, administratively capped reserve access, the compressed NO1 price spread, and no aggregation framework. Neither comparator replicates that compounding geometry. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.3 norwegian own case} builds on it, folding the tariff and reserve-access conditions into the market-access architecture of its four-condition stack, and \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} attributes the measured gap across the layers. 
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{The Norwegian Context}
\label{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context}

Norwegian district heating cannot be modelled as a scaled-down Danish or Swedish case; this section assembles the system characteristics that set it apart. Both valuation perimeters run through these Norwegian specifics. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics} shows how the hydropower water-value pathway reroutes where the socio-economic value of flexibility lands. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.2 policy evolution} traces a decade of incremental regulatory change that has adjusted individual conditions without resolving the mismatch between where flexibility value arises and where its cost lands. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.3 norwegian own case} condenses both threads into the four-condition stack that makes the case irreducible to a Nordic transfer. The section is used to parameterise the model's price and demand environment (\autoref{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance}) and to ground the case-selection argument of \autoref{3-subsec:3.1 research design}. What it establishes is which barriers bind, and how hard, in the specific system the thesis quantifies.


\subsubsection{Energy System Characteristics}
\label{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics}

The system characteristics come first in the Norwegian context because they are what the value pathway runs through: the generation mix sets the marginal unit, the marginal unit sets what flexibility displaces, and both fix the price and demand environment the model inputs in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4.2 input data provenance} are drawn from. Norway's electricity system is dominated by hydropower, which accounts for approximately $90\%$ of annual electricity generation, with the remainder drawn primarily from wind, supplemented by gas turbines and a small thermal reserve \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023}, \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}; on a total-energy-supply basis hydropower covers $44\%$, the highest share among IEA member countries \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}. This structural condition creates a marginal generation unit that behaves differently from the thermal or wind-based units that set the price in most European systems. The marginal cost of a Norwegian hydropower reservoir is not a fuel price but an opportunity cost: producers bid the water value, which reflects what it would cost to cover the last unit of demand with a technology other than hydropower, and is therefore set by expected reservoir surplus or deficit within and between years and by prices in the areas Norway trades with rather than by any single commodity input \cite{multiconsult_drivere_2022}. As established in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.3 sector coupling}, district heating electric boilers that respond to low spot prices in this system do not primarily displace fuel combustion. They free hydropower generation capacity for higher-value export windows or reserve provision \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. The socio-economic value pathway, formalised as one of the two valuation perimeters in \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}, runs through hydro reservoir economics and cross-border market design, a characteristic with no direct parallel in fossil-heavy or wind-dominated European systems. The technical envelope of this pathway has been modelled directly \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}. In the reference scenario, roughly 149 hours of the year see Norwegian hydropower generation capacity, rather than the interconnector, bind the limit on electricity exported for European balancing. A shift of approximately $25\%$ of individual direct electric heating to district heating removes that binding constraint, leaving free hydropower generation capacity in every hour of the year and making the interconnector the limiting factor throughout. The same shift carries a trade-off, reducing Norway's capacity to absorb surplus European renewable generation, so the net system value is conditional rather than unambiguous. That study is an EnergyPLAN analysis of technical potential: it takes neither investment costs nor the electricity market into account, so it establishes the physical envelope of the pathway rather than its value. Independently of the operator-funded literature, NVE's long-term market analysis identifies district heating electric-boiler coupling as a long-duration flexibility resource, more effective than batteries for multi-hour and multi-day surplus \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023}. The same analysis projects the Norwegian capacity balance tightening toward a deficit in the most stressed hours by 2040. \autoref{fig:2.4.1 system schematic} draws the Oslo system this thesis models with the business-economic boundary marked on it: electricity enters through the transmission and distribution operators to the power-to-heat fleet, heat leaves the three production assets through the accumulator and the network to building demand, and reserve provision returns to the transmission operator bounded by capacity-area access. Its companion \autoref{fig:2.5.1 monetary schematic} puts the money flows on the same actor layout once both valuation perimeters have been defined.

The Norwegian power grid is divided into five bidding zones, NO1 through NO5, introduced in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.2 flexibility power}, within which a single spot price clears each hour via Nord Pool. Transmission constraints between zones produce systematic price differentials during periods of high cross-border flow or intra-Norwegian congestion. Norwegian spot prices exhibit comparatively low price-duration variance, which the Flex4RES assessment attributes to the system's dispatchable hydropower dominance, the property that makes Norway the most price-stable area in the Nordic region \cite{skytte_flexible_2019}. \autoref{fig:2.4.1 price duration} shows that flatness directly, plotting the NO1 price-duration curve against DK1 and SE3: the vertical extent of the curve is the arbitrage headroom an Oslo operator has to trade against, and it is materially smaller than in either thermally influenced reference zone. Oslo and the surrounding eastern grid fall within NO1, a zone where variable renewables supplied about $4.7\%$ of electricity production in 2024 against about $94\%$ from hydropower \cite{statistisk_sentralbyra_statbank_nodate}, so its hourly price profile is shaped by hydropower dispatch, interconnector schedules, and demand variation rather than by intermittent renewable production. For district heating operators based in Oslo, the direct consequence is a compressed arbitrage window: the spread between off-peak and on-peak spot prices is narrow, which reduces the commercial return on power-to-heat flexibility before any market-access barrier is applied. This compression is not a contingent feature of current market conditions but a structural property of the zone's generation mix, reinforcing the gap between commercial and socio-economic value on the revenue side. The same compression measured against Danish and Swedish reference zones is set out in the cross-country comparison in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.5 cross-country comparison}.

Norwegian heating demand is structurally concentrated in winter \cite{NVE_langsiktig_2023} and skewed toward direct electric resistance heating, a legacy of the Norwegian hydropower build-out between 1950 and 1980, which laid the groundwork for an electricity-based heating resource, and of the relatively low electricity prices that followed and are identified as a main reason for the low penetration of district heating in Norway relative to the other Nordic countries \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. Norwegian electricity demand is correspondingly highly price-inelastic at the short horizon. For southern Norway (the NO1, NO2 and NO5 zones), the short-run price elasticity is estimated at approximately $-0.09$ over 2013--2023, a period that includes a more-than-fourfold increase in average spot prices, and it becomes statistically indistinguishable from zero in the coldest, highest-price periods \cite{idso_short-term_2024}. Temperature explains the bulk of demand variation. The price signal that drives operator-side flexibility decisions does not pass through to consumer demand in any short-run manner relevant to the commercial/socio-economic gap framing. This direct electric heating stock creates a sharp winter peak in electricity demand that intensifies both congestion on the NO1 grid and the scarcity value of generation capacity during peak periods. District heating networks can in principle reduce this peak by converting electricity to stored thermal mass during off-peak hours and supplying building heat from storage during morning and evening demand peaks, a function that corresponds to the virtual electricity storage concept introduced in \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.3 sector coupling}. The system-level scale of the available resource has been quantified in a stochastic TIMES-Norway model run to 2050: residential space heating accounts for $83-90\%$ of all cost-efficient demand-response operation, with residential hot water contributing $6-14\%$ and industry only $3-5\%$ \cite{ahang_analysis_2025}. The same model shows demand response smoothing Norwegian electricity prices and reducing regulated-hydropower income by $0-4\%$ across the five price zones, which locates a meaningful share of the socio-economic benefit on actors other than the operator that provides the flexibility. That framing is residential-side and this thesis values flexibility from the operator-supply side, but the dispatch pattern is the same: thermal flexibility shifts winter electric heating load away from peak. In a Balmorel analysis of the future renewable northern European energy system, power-to-heat in flexible district heating and increased transmission line capacity appear to have a larger economic potential than demand response from households, industry and the tertiary sector \cite{kirkerud_role_2021}. The extent to which this peak-shaving function is commercially accessible to district heating operators depends on the reserve products and tariff structures available to them, a question addressed directly in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} and \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model}.

The Norwegian district heating sector is small by Nordic standards but geographically concentrated. Aggregate national net production was approximately $7669 GWh$ in 2024, of which about $6850 GWh$ was delivered to consumers after distribution losses \cite{statistisk_sentralbyra_statbank_nodate}. By heat-central type, 2024 net production was roughly $38\%$ waste incineration, $29\%$ solid biofuel, $12\%$ electric boilers and $11\%$ heat pumps \cite{statistisk_sentralbyra_statbank_nodate}, \cite{boeng_mindre_2025}; Norway has the highest power-to-heat share in the Nordic district heating group \cite{sandberg_framework_2018}. \autoref{fig:2.4.1 dh fuel mix} sets that mix beside the sector's energy balance, and the two panels carry different points: panel (a) shows how small a share of national heat production the two power-to-heat technologies hold even at that Nordic-leading position, and panel (b) shows how much of gross production is lost in distribution before heat reaches a customer.

Hafslund Celsio's Oslo system produced approximately 1.8 TWh of heat in 2025, of which 1.628 TWh was sold to customers \cite{hafslund_forelopig_nodate}; district heating covers about 20\% of Oslo's heating demand, and around 25\% of the city's capacity demand could be met by district heating \cite{energikommisjonen_mer_2023}. The production system spans roughly fifteen heat centrals with just over 1,000 MW of installed capacity across all energy carriers \cite{nve_konsesjonssak_2026}. The operational range is wide: summer load runs at roughly 50 to 75 MW, against an observed winter day near 414 MW and a production record around 800 MW (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). At the other end of the price distribution, the Klemetsrud site can dump 60 to 65 MW of heat through its cooling capacity, which lets the electric boilers keep absorbing electricity when prices go negative (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026). 

Trondheim and Bergen operate secondary systems under Lunera Energi and Eviny Termo respectively \cite{norsk_fjernvarme_fjernkontrollenno_nodate}, with the remainder distributed across smaller municipal and cooperative networks. The Oslo system is substantially more technically capable than any of the secondary systems, owing to its scale, the diversity of its generation stack (waste-to-energy base-load, electrode boilers, and large-scale heat pumps), and the Haraldrud accumulator commissioned in 2023.

The Haraldrud facility, licensed at $8{,}500 m^3$ and an installed rating of $98 MW$ \cite{nve_konsesjonssak_2026} and operated at an ideal storage capacity of approximately $550 MWh$ with up to about $100 MW$ of momentary charge and discharge (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026), is larger than any of the Norwegian thermal stores catalogued in \autoref{2-subsec:2.2.3 thermal storage}. \subsubsection{Policy Evolution 2015-2025}
\label{2-subsec:2.4.2 policy evolution}

The policy environment governing Norwegian district heating flexibility has shifted materially over the decade from 2015 to 2025. It accumulated institutional changes that individually addressed specific inefficiencies without resolving the market-design mismatch that is the thesis's central object of study. Tracing this evolution situates the quantitative modelling in \autoref{3-sec:3} against a specific regulatory timeline, and frames the barrier analysis in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} as a snapshot of a system in incremental transition rather than a static condition.

Grid tariff reform entered the regulatory agenda in 2015, when NVE opened its first consultation on replacing the then-current distribution-network tariff with a capacity-based element intended to reduce peak congestion and improve price signals for flexible consumers \cite{andersen_horing_2015}. These consultations ultimately produced the capacity charge structure. For business customers with annual consumption above 100{,}000 kWh, the charge is levied on the single highest hourly power draw within each calendar month; the averaged form, the mean of the three highest daily maxima in the month, applies to the step tariff for business customers below that annual threshold and therefore not to a district heating operator of Hafslund Celsio's scale \cite{elvia_dagens_2022}. As documented in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}, this regime functions as a commercial barrier to the specific flexibility behaviour it was ostensibly designed to encourage. A district heating operator that raises its electrical load during an mFRR activation or a spot-arbitrage event risks setting a new monthly peak that persists for the full billing period, which makes the net financial case for activation ambiguous even when the spot-price signal is favourable \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}.

A Nordic-incentives analysis covering Norwegian district heating was published in 2018 \cite{sneum_economic_2018}. The Flex4RES consortium's summary report followed in 2019, a Nordic-Baltic cross-country assessment of how sector coupling across heat, gas and transport, together with electricity transmission and generation, can supply flexibility for variable renewables, and of the regulatory barriers standing in its way; district heating is one of its central flexibility routes and Norway one of the countries assessed \cite{skytte_flexible_2019}. The barrier taxonomy later adopted as the scaffolding for \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} is the 2021 taxonomy study \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. These documents pre-date the Haraldrud commissioning and the 2025 Norgespris extension, establishing a reference baseline against which subsequent regulatory developments can be compared.

The institutional capacity to reform these conditions consolidated over the same period. The Storting adopted the Energy Act amendments transposing the European Union's Third Energy Market Package on 22 March 2018, establishing a statutorily independent regulatory authority that cannot be instructed in the exercise of its delegated powers or in the disposition of its allocated budget \cite{stortinget_vedtak_2019}; RME was established as that authority from 1 November 2019, separating market regulation from NVE's broader resource-administration mandate, a date set by the implementing regulation rather than by the lovvedtak \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2019}.

The 2022 International Energy Agency review of Norwegian energy policy observed that Norwegian demand-side flexibility options remain in the pilot phase and recommended that the government provide incentives for introducing sector-wide demand-side flexibility through network tariffs and flexibility services \cite{iea_-_international_energy_agency_norway_2022}.

The year 2023 brought parallel advances in the evidence base and in the physical system. The SINTEF ZEN research programme's "Report 47" modelled the Norwegian building stock to 2050, quantifying how energy efficiency combined with district heating and heat-pump deployment could lower national peak electricity demand and reduce system energy costs \cite{SINTEF_energy_2023}. Vista Analyse published a cost-benefit analysis of energy-flexible heating systems at the building level, assessing the private and socio-economic consequences of possible changes to the TEK17 building-code flexibility requirements \cite{rosnes_energifleksible_2023}. Its valuation of avoided generation and grid investment informs the socio-economic framing in \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}. In 2023, the Haraldrud accumulator was commissioned, materially changing the technical basis of Oslo system flexibility.

In 2024, Hafslund Celsio commissioned an assessment from THEMA Consulting \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}, the first operator-funded external study of the socio-economic value of Norwegian district heating and of the barriers constraining it. The report values the flexibility of Celsio's 283 MW of electric boilers in Statnett's reserve markets at approximately NOK 330{,}000 per MW per year, derived from an FCR-N capacity price of NOK 47{,}000 per MW per month applied across an October--April heating season, and finds that district heating receives no payment for its grid-relief value beyond what can be realised in those reserve markets; these socio-economic estimates serve as reference comparators in \autoref{4-sec:4 results}.

The year 2025 brought two regulatory developments with direct bearing on the commercial case for district heating flexibility. With effect from 1 October 2025, the Norgespris electricity price support scheme was extended to district heating customers with household consumption, who from that date can choose between Norgespris and the pre-existing strømstøtte scheme, both state-funded \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2025}, \cite{nve_norgespris_2026}. Norgespris fixes the spot-price component of the heat bill at 50 øre/kWh including VAT for consumption up to 4500 kWh per month, settled as a credit or surcharge line item on the district heating invoice while the customer's underlying contract with the operator is unchanged; commercial and industrial consumption is excluded. Correspondingly, Hafslund Celsio's pre-extension self-financing of strømstøtte is replaced by state reimbursement \cite{nve_norgespris_2026}. Hafslund attributes the recent weakening of district heating profitability to strømstøtte alongside the increased incineration levy and the reduced electricity tax, reporting an after-tax result of NOK $-52$ m for the district heating business in 2025 against NOK 286 m in 2024, or NOK $-133$ m once adjusted for the accounting gain on the sale of Hafslund Fiber \cite{hafslund_arsrapport_nodate}.

The $CO_2$ levy on waste incineration was restructured over the same period, from the 2025 two-rate design to a single rate of NOK 848 per tonne from 1 January 2026 with quota-liable emissions exempted \cite{stortinget_stortingsvedtak_2025}; the rates, the fossil-only basis and their dispatch consequences are treated in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.3 regulatory barriers}.

In September 2025 the energy minister publicly acknowledged that district heating carries important significance in the power system by relieving the grid, particularly on cold days, and stated that the government would consider measures to maintain socio-economically profitable district heating production; the concrete measure announced was fiscal, dropping a planned increase in the 2026 waste-incineration $CO_2$ levy, rather than any new market product for flexibility \cite{hovland_varsler_2025}. A 2025 consultancy note by Grønli from AFRY, operator-adjacent and not peer-reviewed, attributes the sector's limited uptake of flexibility markets to the capacity-tariff risk, a complex value chain, and a competence gap at district heating companies rather than to an absence of available revenue \cite{gronli_hvor_2025}.

A 2026 sector-coupling optimisation for the Swedish district heating sector is the most recent structurally analogous study in the Nordic literature \cite{atabaki_optimising_2026}. Its Norwegian parallel at the firm level, with paired commercial and socio-economic valuation, is the gap this thesis addresses.

\subsubsection{Why Norway Is Its Own Case}
\label{2-subsec:2.4.3 norwegian own case}

The preceding subsections establish two structural claims. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics} documents that the Norwegian power system routes the socio-economic value of power-to-heat flexibility through hydro reservoir economics and cross-border export pricing, a value pathway absent from the European literature that developed primarily around fossil displacement and VRE curtailment avoidance. \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.2 policy evolution} documents that a decade of incremental regulatory activity has modified specific elements of the market environment without resolving the core mismatch between where flexibility value is created and where its cost is borne. This subsection addresses whether these two features, in combination, make Norwegian district heating flexibility analytically distinct from any Nordic comparator, or whether it constitutes a scaled-down version of the Danish or Swedish case that can be modelled by adaptation.

The hydro value pathway is necessary but not sufficient to explain the structural character of the Norwegian case. If it were the only distinguishing feature, the analytical gap could in principle be addressed by adapting a Danish or Swedish sector-coupling optimisation and substituting the NO1 price signal for DK1 or SE3. What prevents this reduction is the co-occurrence of four conditions that individually appear elsewhere in the Nordic group but do not stack simultaneously in Denmark or Sweden (see \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.5 cross-country comparison}): the hydropower water-value pathway of \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics}, which routes the system value of flexibility through reservoir economics rather than fossil displacement; a substitution-price cap on heat revenues under the Energy Act §5-5, under which the heat price may not exceed the price of electric heating in the supply area \cite{energidepartementet_lov_1991}  and is therefore now indexed to a politically set retail electricity price since the state-funded Norgespris extension took effect for district heating customers on 1 October 2025 \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2025}; a market-access architecture that penalises load-raising flexibility under the standard capacity-charge tariff, offers escape only through interruptible terms that presuppose disconnectability, and administratively caps reserve-market access per capacity area even for prequalified actors; and a $CO_2$ levy trajectory that raises the operating cost of the waste-to-energy base-load anchoring the Oslo merit order. No single Nordic comparator faces all four simultaneously, and the compounding interactions between them, in particular the joint effect of the price cap and the market-access architecture on the operator's net margin from a flexibility event, are not captured by any of the single-condition barrier studies reviewed in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers}.

The consequence for the valuation framework is that the gap between commercial and socio-economic value of district heating flexibility in Norway is not merely a magnitude difference relative to Danish or Swedish equivalents: it is a structural outcome produced by a specific combination of barriers that interact and reinforce one another within a system whose marginal value mechanism is already fundamentally different. Quantifying the gap at the operator level therefore requires a Norway-specific model rather than a transferred Nordic case study, and the institutional explanation of why the gap persists requires accounting for each of the four compounding conditions rather than isolating a dominant single cause. \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value} formalises the two valuation perimeters that structure the quantification, and \autoref{3-sec:3} specifies the model built to measure the gap they define.

\subsection{Commercial and Socio-Economic Value}
\label{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}

This section defines the two valuation perimeters the thesis measures against each other: the business-economic (bedriftsøkonomisk) perimeter of the operator's own ledger, and the socio-economic (samfunnsøkonomisk) perimeter of all welfare effects regardless of whose balance sheet they land on. The definitions are covered because the research question is a comparison between the two, and any ambiguity in what each perimeter counts would surface as a spurious gap. They are used as the accounting rules of the quantitative work: the dispatch model in \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} computes the first perimeter, the triangulation in \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation} the second, and the gap arithmetic in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} the division between them. The second perimeter contains the first rather than standing beside it, so what is reported is how the socio-economic total splits between the value the operator captures and the value that reaches no one, not one figure subtracted from another. \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.1 economic definitions} draws the two perimeters item by item; \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap} then states the four-step mechanism through which the gap between them arises. What holds the two perimeters apart is the set of missing enablers, which is why the policy discussion in \autoref{5-subsec:5.3 implications} works on the enablers rather than on the perimeters.

%-------------------------------------------------------
\subsubsection{Defining the Two Perspectives}
\label{2-subsec:2.5.1 economic definitions}

The two perimeters are drawn item by item here because the quantity this thesis reports is how the wider of them divides, and an item placed on the wrong side of the line moves that division without changing anything real. Each item below is an accounting rule: whether a flow falls inside the operator's ledger, whether it falls outside that ledger but inside the societal boundary, and which actor receives it. The enumeration is what \autoref{3-subsec:3.4 operator model} and \autoref{3-subsec:3.6 socio-economic valuation} are built to compute, and an item placed in the wrong perimeter would surface in \autoref{4-subsec:4.3 valuation gap} as gap that is not there.  The commercial valuation perimeter encompasses the cash flows that accrue to the district heating operator, and only those flows. On the revenue side, this perimeter includes spot-market arbitrage revenue from purchasing low-price electricity for power-to-heat conversion and displacing more expensive backup boiler dispatch during high-price hours; reserve-market payments for mFRR capacity and, where the asset can follow the automatic control signals aFRR requires, aFRR capacity, comprising both the capacity component paid regardless of activation and the activation payment received on top when the reserve is called \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}, a revenue line bounded in practice by administratively capped access rather than by asset scale (\autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}); and avoided peak-load charges under the standard effektledd regime, a revenue line that is marginal for assets on interruptible terms. On the cost side, the perimeter includes electricity purchased at the prevailing spot price, the network tariff, which for the incumbent's interruptible connections is an effective volumetric charge of approximately 5 øre/kWh rather than the standard effektledd, capital servicing on power-to-heat and storage assets, the electricity tax on power-to-heat consumption, levied on district heating producers at the reduced rate of 0.60 øre/kWh \cite{the_norwegian_tax_administration_arsrundskriv_nodate}, and variable operating costs including maintenance and staffing. The perimeter explicitly excludes the value that flexible operation creates for the grid operator, for other market participants, and for the broader energy system: these belong to the complementary perimeter. 

The socio-economic valuation perimeter encompasses all welfare effects attributable to district heating flexibility provision, regardless of which actor's balance sheet they appear on. In addition to the flows counted in the bedriftsøkonomisk perimeter, it includes avoided grid reinforcement costs, which accrue to the distribution system operator Elvia and to its network customers rather than to the heat producer, depending on how the avoided investment feeds through to the network company's measured efficiency and regulated cost base \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}; freed grid connection capacity that enables faster connection of new loads, a value for which the district heating operator receives no reward \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}; system price effects, whereby withdrawing power-to-heat demand from high-price hours reduces residual load so that a lower-marginal-cost unit becomes price-setting \cite{nagel_european_2023}, and the resulting price reduction transfers surplus to electricity consumers across the bidding zone \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}; displaced fossil or high-cost peaking generation and the corresponding reduction in $CO_2$ emissions, a channel that reaches the Norwegian case through the interconnected market rather than through a domestic fossil plant \cite{meibom_value_2007, penttinen_optimal_2021}; and, in the Norway-specific case, reduced peak load on dammed hydropower, which frees generation capacity for potential export \cite{askeland_balancing_2019}, though the same analysis finds that lower domestic demand also raises forced export to drain reservoirs and reduces the import the system can absorb, and it is a technical simulation that excludes investment costs and the electricity market. The value of freed export capacity accrues primarily to hydropower producers and indirectly to export market participants rather than to the district heating operator. The perimeter also incorporates a security-of-supply component: by delivering heat from energy sources other than electricity, district heating diversifies the energy supply and reduces dependence on the power system, which lessens the consequences of interruptions in electricity supply and can also reduce the risk that they occur, a system reliability benefit with a diffuse beneficiary set, although the heat system is itself vulnerable to prolonged outages because production, control systems, fans and circulation pumps all require electricity \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. The conceptual basis for the socio-economic perimeter is the standard Norwegian framework for socio-economic analysis - the Direktoratet for økonomistyring guidance material, Finansdepartementet's circular R-109/2021, and NOU 2012:16 - which fixes the calculation rate, the tax-financing cost and the carbon valuation any such analysis is to apply \cite{finansdepartement_prinsipper_2021}, and which the Norwegian district heating and heating-system valuations drawn on here state they are built against \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024, rosnes_energifleksible_2023}, adapted here to a private infrastructure operator context in which the externalities accrue to identifiable institutional actors rather than to anonymous social welfare.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Graphics/2. Theory/2.5.1 - monetary schematic, BO within SO; FIG-14.png}
    \caption{Monetary flows around the district heating operator, the companion of \autoref{fig:2.4.1 system schematic} on the same actor layout. Dashed marks the bedriftsøkonomisk perimeter and solid the socio-economic perimeter, the latter a strict superset of the former. Spot purchases and the electricity tax leave the operator, the grid tariff goes to the distribution company, mFRR capacity and activation revenue arrive from the transmission system operator bounded by capacity-area access, and heat sales reach customers under the price ceiling of the Energy Act. These two perimeters are not two measurements of the same value. They are two accounting boundaries applied to the same physical asset and the same flexibility action. \autoref{fig:2.5.1 monetary schematic} draws the two boundaries on the actor map: the flows that enter the operator's ledger, and the value streams that cross the outer perimeter without ever reaching it. A single mFRR activation that reduces peak demand on the NO1 grid yields one value when assessed against the operator's income statement, and a larger value when assessed against total welfare. The difference is not a measurement error but a structural consequence of the institutional arrangements that determine which actor captures which cash flow. Anchoring the scale of that difference against prior work is difficult, because the field lacks a shared method. A systematic review of 123 district heating benefit and economic-assessment studies finds that flexibility, although identified as a benefit in eleven of them, is monetised in none \cite{ahmed_district_2025}. The same review finds that welfare-level societal benefits are the least frequently assessed benefit category, and that no harmonised framework for valuing district heating benefits exists across the corpus. The choice of valuation perimeter is, consequently, not a presentational detail but the decision that fixes what the resulting figure means.

Two studies in the corpus, one Danish and one Austrian, apply paired perspectives to the same district heating asset and are the closest methodological precedents for the approach taken here. The first pairs the two perspectives across two models: EnergyPLAN on the whole Samsø energy system from a socio-economic perspective, and energyPRO on the business-economic case of the Ballen-Brundby plant within it. Optimising the plant's investment configuration puts the thermal storage optimum at roughly 10 to 15 MWh, which the authors note is far less than what was of interest from the island perspective, and they describe the resulting misalignment between what is relevant from a societal perspective and from a plant-operator perspective as one that needs to be overcome \cite{ostergaard_business_2019}. \autoref{fig:2.5.2 storage optimum} draws the two readings side by side: the plant-level cost curve bottoming at 10 to 15 MWh, and the island-level benefit still rising at the largest storage increment the study tests. The second combines a heat-merit-order business-economic analysis with a socioeconomic assessment of seasonal storage in Linz, and likewise finds that storage value depends on multi-function dispatch integration rather than on raw capacity \cite{moser_socioeconomic_2018}. Its societal layer rests partly on input-output multipliers rather than welfare externalities, however, so it transfers as a method rather than as a comparator. The Norwegian institutional conditions documented in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3 barriers} and \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context} (the grid tariff structure, the Energy Act §5-5 price cap, and the hydro-dominated value pathway) make direct transfer of either study's parameter values inadvisable, so the definitions applied here are constructed from the ground up for the Norwegian case. To the best of the author's knowledge, no paired bedriftsøkonomisk and samfunnsøkonomisk valuation of thermal flexibility has been conducted for Norwegian district heating at the firm level, a gap the methodology in \autoref{3-sec:3} is designed to fill.

\subsubsection{The Value Gap Mechanism}

\label{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap}

\begin{figure}[!h]

In the resource-and-enabler terms of \autoref{2-subsec:2.1.1 definitions}, the value gap this thesis quantifies is what opens when a flexibility resource carries samfunnsøkonomisk value that no enabler converts into a bedriftsøkonomisk return. It operates through a four-step structural mechanism. First, thermal flexibility provision by a district heating operator reduces costs elsewhere in the energy system: avoided grid reinforcement at the distribution level, freed balancing capacity at the transmission level, and suppressed peak clearing prices that reduce consumer expenditure during high-demand events, each of which constitutes a real welfare gain attributable to the flexibility action \cite{mitridati_heat_2020, mitridati_power_2018, Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. Second, these cost reductions accrue primarily to Elvia as avoided distribution-grid capital expenditure, to Statnett as freed reserve capacity available for redeployment, and to electricity consumers as lower peak clearing prices. None of these actors are obligated under current market design to compensate the district heating operator for the service that generates them: district heating is not paid for the network benefit it delivers beyond what can be realised in the reserve markets and through lower tariffs for interruptible consumption \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}, and for connections granted on terms of disconnection or load limitation the network regulation rules out compensation outright, both for entering such an agreement and for the curtailment itself (\autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}) \cite{energidepartementet_forskrift_2019}. This is the split-incentive configuration, in which the benefits of an action do not fall on the stakeholder performing it \cite{sneum_barriers_2021}. The same divergence appears for Norwegian network companies: flexibility resources can create value for other actors in the flexibility value chain, such as the transmission system operator, that may not be accounted for in the DSOs' own cost-benefit analyses, and can carry wider societal benefits beyond the power system altogether \cite{saele_understanding_2023}. The authors raise it as a limitation of their DSO-only perspective rather than as a measured finding. Third, the operator bears the full cost of flexible operation: electricity purchased at the prevailing spot price, effektledd peak charges that, for standard connections, may rise rather than fall when a load-raising flexibility event sets a new monthly maximum, a channel largely defused for the incumbent's interruptible connections (\autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers}), and capital servicing on power-to-heat and storage assets \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}, \cite{djorup_district_2020}, alongside the opportunity cost of heat not sold during arbitrage windows at the price ceiling the Energy Act §5-5 sets \cite{energidepartementet_lov_1991}. Fourth, no transfer mechanism currently bridges the gap between the samfunnsøkonomisk value generated and the bedriftsøkonomisk cost borne. The mFRR bid floor was cut to $1 MW$ with effect from 2 December 2025 \cite{statnett_1_2025}, from a zone-differentiated level of 10 MW in NO2, NO4 and NO5 and 5 MW in NO1 and NO3 \cite{dalen_fleksibilitet_nodate}. The cut matters less for district heating than its framing suggests: at 5 MW the NO1 floor already sat below the scale of the larger boiler installations, which the incumbent often installs from 10 MW upward (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026), so bid size was not what kept them out of the reserve market. What binds is network-side prequalification - clearance with the distribution system operator and approval by the transmission system operator - and, since April 2026, the per-area volume caps of \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The floor cut widened formal eligibility for the sub-threshold fringe; it did not open access for the incumbent, which had cleared the floor throughout. Threshold access is not the same as a reward, however: modelling of power-to-heat bidding into a reserve capacity market finds the economics bounded by the installed power-to-heat capacity and by the composition of the competing heat production, and concludes that policies rewarding power-balancing services, alongside investment support for power-to-heat capacity and heat storage, are needed to overcome those limits \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}. For the largest operator the constraint inverts: reserve access is administratively capped under the capacity-area regime even where scale, automation, and prequalification are all satisfied, so the missing enabler is not eligibility but allocation \cite{statnett_innforing_2026}. The Danish contrast shows the same enabler working: electric-boiler deployment in Danish district heating is driven primarily by the demand for ancillary services rather than the demand for heat, historically by the profit chance in the FCR market, and where Danish electric boilers do meet an access constraint, it sits on the connection side - most distribution system operators offer them limited grid access, capping available electric capacity in hours of high load - rather than in an administrative cap on reserve-market volume \cite{danish_energy_agency_technology_2024}. No distribution-level product compensates peak-shaving, and district heating receives no grid-benefit payment for the congestion it relieves \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. The effektledd is a capacity charge on measured load within the billing period, a structure in which every hour can act as a calculation hour and off-peak hours are not exempt \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}; nothing in that basis carves out load raised by a reserve activation, even when the activation reduces net system congestion. Finally, price-model redesign can transmit flexibility value, but only along the operator-to-customer axis: Swedish case evidence finds that the price models best capturing flexibility are the power-plus-fee and marginal-pricing models, with marginal pricing delivering the highest reductions in both system and customer costs, so that route moves value between the operator and its building-owner customers and leaves the transfer from the wider power system unaddressed \cite{lygnerud_capturing_2024}. The boundary this mechanism draws is now articulated in government primary text: the proposition amending the Energy Act on surplus-heat utilisation states that the benefits at issue are mainly societal while the decisions rest on business-economic criteria, that requirements imposing costs beyond an actor's own business-economic benefit constitute a disproportionate burden, and that the proposed utilisation duty binds only where business-economically profitable \cite{det_kongelige_energidepartement_endringer_2026}. The result is a structural underinvestment equilibrium in which the operator's privately rational decision, to build out flexibility capacity and operate it actively, is attenuated by rules that assign the costs of flexibility to the actor who provides it while distributing its benefits to actors who bear none of the cost. This is the same misalignment between what is relevant from a plant-operator perspective and what is relevant from a societal perspective that the paired Danish island study of \autoref{2-subsec:2.5.1 economic definitions} identified, which is evidence that the gap is structural rather than hypothetical \cite{ostergaard_business_2019}. System-level Nordic modelling reaches the same split from the other direction: demand-response deployment described as no-regret from a societal perspective yields private arbitrage value of only about 1.2 to 3.6 EUR/MWh, with the explicit conclusion that subsidies may be needed and a call for producer-perspective revenue modelling of the kind this thesis performs \cite{nagel_european_2023}. A 2020 NMBU master's thesis reviewing the European sector-coupling literature had already reached the antecedent conclusion: the integrated whole was blocked by a missing market and compensation model, at least in Norway \cite{verbeeten_state--art_2020}. The equilibrium is not a market failure in the textbook sense of an externality that no institutional arrangement has yet recognised. It is a regulatory-design outcome that made sense under the conditions prevailing when the relevant rules were written, and that has not been re-anchored to the changed system conditions produced by the electrification transition, the growth of variable renewable generation in adjacent bidding zones, and the grid-constraint trajectory documented for the Norwegian system in \autoref{2-subsec:2.4 norwegian context}; the regulator states the same diagnosis for the heat side of that framework, observing that the rules governing electricity supply and the electricity market have been developed and amended several times since the Energy Act of 1991 while the district heating regulation has stood unchanged, so that questions now arise in several areas that were not live when those rules were drafted \cite{nve_nves_2024}.  The legislative history of the surplus-heat duty shows where the state drew its line, and it drew it twice. In extending the analysis obligation to data centres above 2 MW and to other plants above 20 MW of electric capacity, categories the European energy-efficiency directive does not cover, the ministry stated plainly that the Norwegian proposal is stricter than the directive; for thermal plants the duty tracks it \cite{energidepartementet_prop_2023}. The power to order that the heat actually be used was then withheld from those same two categories, on two stated grounds: a concern not to impose requirements deviating materially from the framework conditions of other countries, and a concern that a mandate could disturb the incentives for confidential cooperation between producers of surplus heat and the external parties able to use it \cite{energidepartementet_prop_2023}. The competitiveness argument and the voluntary-cooperation argument are the two that recur wherever a transfer mechanism is proposed, and here they sit on the record inside the same instrument that widened the duty to analyse.

\begin{keypoint}
The gap mechanism runs in four steps: (1) flexibility provision creates value elsewhere in the energy system; (2) that value accrues to Elvia, Statnett and electricity consumers; (3) the operator bears the full cost of providing it; (4) no general transfer mechanism routes the remaining value back, and for the largest operator reserve access is capped by allocation rather than eligibility. Each step is evidenced separately above.
\end{keypoint}

A live Norwegian regulation shows the same mechanism operating inside an instrument built to correct it. Since 1 April 2025 a developer planning a large thermal or electric plant, including any new district heating or cooling network at any capacity, must produce a cost-benefit analysis of the possibilities for using the surplus heat, obtain the regulator's approval before construction begins, and may be required as a condition of that approval to use the heat \cite{stortinget_forskrift_2025}. What the regulation demands is a business-economic analysis accompanied by a simple description of the project in a socio-economic perspective including non-priced effects, and positive cost-benefit is then defined by reference to the socio-economic analysis \cite{stortinget_forskrift_2025}. The regulator's own guidance resolves the composite: the socio-economic analysis is read as the business-economic analysis plus that simple description, and a plant without positive business-economic cost-benefit is assumed to have positive cost-benefit in the regulation's sense only rarely \cite{formal_kost-nytteanalyse_2025}. A regulation written to capture an externality thus settles, in the ordinary case, on the private cash-flow answer, and the externality it exists to internalise carries weight only where it is qualitatively described. The transfer problem set out above is therefore neither confined to flexibility nor hypothetical: it is visible in the one Norwegian instrument that has recently tried to bridge the same divide.  

The mechanism above describes the dominant direction of the gap: the samfunnsøkonomisk value of district heating flexibility exceeds the bedriftsøkonomisk value the operator can capture. This is the direction the Norwegian institutional arrangements predict and the pattern documented across the Nordic precedents. The directional claim warrants one qualification. Stylised Swedish district heating configurations with high power-to-heat penetration show that operator-optimised dispatch of stored power-to-heat can displace electricity co-generation from a competing biomass-CHP unit \cite{monie_power--heat_2022}. In that waste-to-energy configuration, biomass-CHP electricity output falls from $115 GWh$ to zero as power-to-heat capacity scales to 60 MW, with a corresponding loss of system balancing capacity that the operator's profit and loss does not register. The implication is not that the gap can run negative in the Norwegian case at current power-to-heat penetration. The displacement channel requires a co-generation unit whose electricity output can be pushed out of the merit order, and the Oslo system's base-load does not offer one: its waste incineration does generate power - 123 GWh from the Klemetsrud steam turbines in 2025, against 149 GWh in 2024 \cite{hafslund_arsrapport_nodate} - but a waste-fired plant is conventionally operated as base load for heat generation and dispatched at full load for most of the year \cite{beiron_combined_2020}, and the incumbent runs its lines to burn waste rather than to produce heat (operator interview, Hafslund Celsio, 25 June 2026), so that output follows throughput rather than a dispatch decision stored power-to-heat could crowd out. Rather, at sufficiently high power-to-heat build-out, the assumption that samfunnsøkonomisk value always weakly exceeds bedriftsøkonomisk value requires re-examination. The analysis in \autoref{4-sec:4 results} operates within the dominant-direction regime. The boundary condition under which the gap could reverse is flagged for the future work in \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work}.

\subsubsection{How the Field Was Mapped}

The corpus underpinning this chapter was assembled through an informational rather than a structural review process. The starting point was a seed set of supervisor-supplied papers and the author's own keyword seeding. This was expanded in Research Rabbit and Consensus by snowballing across citation and co-citation links, and supplemented by targeted Scopus searches on the themes where the seed set was thin: grid tariff design, balancing-market access for demand-side assets, and Norwegian district heating specifically. 

This is an informational review, and is presented as such. Across 123 studies, the field has no harmonised method for monetising district heating benefits \cite{ahmed_district_2025}, so a protocol-driven review would impose a comparability the underlying literature does not possess. \subsubsection{Research-Gap Matrix}
\label{2-subsec:2.6.2 research gap matrix}

The corpus can be positioned on two dimensions that jointly define the thesis's contribution. The first is analytical scope: whether a study models a single operator or plant, an aggregate market or region (system-level), or compares multiple systems or reviews the field (multi-system). The second is valuation perspective, the distinction formalised in \autoref{2-subsec:2.5 commercial and societal value}: whether a study measures the DH operator perspective only, the socio-economic perspective only, or both on consistent scenarios (the paired case). Geographic specificity, the third dimension relevant to the contribution claim, is encoded by colour rather than as a third axis, so that the Norwegian work is visually separable from the broader Nordic and European corpus. \autoref{fig:2.6.2 research gap matrix} plots the principal sources on this grid.

\begin{figure}[!h]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{Graphics/2. Theory/2.6.2 - research-gap matrix; FIG-17.png}
    \caption{Research-gap matrix for the valuation of thermal flexibility in district heating: analytical scope on the columns against valuation perspective on the rows, with marker colour encoding geography. Among prior work the firm-level paired cell holds a single marker and it is Danish; no Norwegian study occupies that cell, and it is the cell this thesis enters. The multi-system column is empty by construction, since field-spanning reviews survey valuations but perform none. Conceptual and baseline sources that take no valuation stance are part of the corpus but are not plotted as valuation markers.}
    \label{fig:2.6.2 research gap matrix}
\end{figure}

The matrix makes the gap legible without recourse to the prose, and two field-wide observations frame it. First, a recent critical review of local flexibility markets in Europe notes that "the future electrification of industry and the transformation of district heating systems remain comparatively underexplored, despite their clear relevance as new sources of flexibility within a sector-coupling perspective" \cite{scrocca_local_2026}. The gap the matrix identifies is therefore recognised at the European level, not only in the Norwegian-specific corpus. Second, the gap is methodological as much as geographic. Across 123 district heating benefit studies, flexibility is quantified in only four and monetised in none \cite{ahmed_district_2025}, so the paired row is sparse across the whole field, not merely for Norway. Within that field, the two single-perspective rows are both populated for Norway. The bedriftsøkonomisk row has firm-level Norwegian precedent \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}, and the samfunnsøkonomisk row carries the system-level Norwegian welfare diagnosis of the THEMA report \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}, with further Norwegian system-level studies populating the same row in \autoref{fig:2.6.2 research gap matrix}. The paired row, by contrast, is almost empty. Three studies in the corpus take both perspectives, but two of them do so on other objects: one values consumer-side heat savings under Danish tariff design \cite{djorup_district_2020} and one assesses building-level flexibility requirements in the Norwegian building code \cite{rosnes_energifleksible_2023}, neither at the level of a district heating firm's flexibility. The only study that assesses district heating flexibility itself from both a bedriftsøkonomisk and a samfunnsøkonomisk perspective sits in the firm-level $\times$ paired cell, as a single Danish marker on a small biomass island with no hydropower interaction \cite{ostergaard_business_2019}, and the divergence between the two perspectives is its central result. Its two perimeters are carried by different models on different system boundaries, a plant-level energyPRO dispatch analysis against an island-level EnergyPLAN system-cost simulation, so even there the paired reading is assembled across two scenario sets rather than run on one. A separate study pairs a plant-level monetisation with a regional macroeconomic layer for an Austrian storage case, but that layer is a time-series macroeconometric simulation reporting gross regional product, net export balance, and employment effects rather than a welfare cost-benefit analysis, so the matrix places it in the samfunnsøkonomisk row rather than the paired one \cite{moser_socioeconomic_2018}. To the extent the cell that defines this thesis is occupied by prior work at all, it is occupied by a single study that is methodologically reusable but substantively non-transferable, and no Norwegian marker appears in that firm-level cell.

\subsubsection{Where This Thesis Fits and What It Adds}
\label{2-subsec:2.6.3 where this thesis fits}

This thesis occupies the firm-level $\times$ paired cell of \autoref{fig:2.6.2 research gap matrix}, applied to the Norwegian district heating case: a single operator's flexibility assessed against both the business economic and the socio-economic perimeter on consistent scenarios. It draws on the two nearest populated regions of the map. From the single-perspective Norwegian work it takes the operator-ledger modelling precedent \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016} and the welfare-side institutional diagnosis of the THEMA report \cite{Thema_fjernvarme_2024}. From the paired Nordic work it takes the methodological template of the Danish Samsø study \cite{ostergaard_business_2019}, adopted for its two-perimeter structure rather than for its biomass-island results. The precise claim the matrix supports is therefore not that the value of thermal flexibility is unstudied, but that, to the best of the author's knowledge, no paired business economic and socio-economic valuation of thermal flexibility has been conducted for Norwegian district heating at the firm level. What makes the cell more than a geographic blank is the regulatory detail that defines it: the effect of grid-tariff structure on power-to-heat dispatch is well studied in general \cite{bergaentzle_cross-sector_2022}, \cite{johannsen_incentivising_2021}, \cite{sandberg_energy_2019}, and the Norwegian monthly-peak capacity charge examined in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.2 market barriers} has itself been modelled at plant level as a suppressor of electric-boiler flexibility \cite{kirkerud_impacts_2016}; what that work stops short of is pricing the same charge against a socio-economic counterfactual, so the barrier is documented as an operator-side cost without an estimate of what it withholds from the system. The thesis also quantifies before it monetises: a technical flexibility envelope (\autoref{3-subsec:3.3 technical envelope}) sizes the physical resource in MWh and MW and supplies the physical scale against which both perimeters are read, directly addressing the field-wide finding that flexibility has been monetised in none of the 123 reviewed studies \cite{ahmed_district_2025}. The methodology developed in \autoref{3-sec:3} is constructed specifically to fill that cell.

\subsubsection{Adjacent Gaps Left for Future Work}
\label{2-subsec:2.6.4 future work}

Naming the cell the thesis fills also sharpens the adjacent cells it does not. Three are worth flagging, both to anchor the future-work discussion in \autoref{5-subsec:5.5 future work} and to keep the contribution claim precise.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Multi-system paired valuation.} The thesis takes Oslo as its primary case; extending the paired BØ/SØ assessment across Trondheim, Bergen, and smaller municipal systems is a distinct research area, not a marginal extension, because the storage and capacity asymmetries documented in \autoref{2-subsec:2.3.1 tech barriers} and \autoref{2-subsec:2.4.1 system characteristics} break the single-case parameterisation.
  \item \textbf{End-user welfare and distributional effects.} The welfare consequences of flexibility activation for the heat customer fall outside the scope set in \autoref{1-subsec:1.4 scope and limitations}, and remain almost entirely unmonetised in the field: end-user and distributional benefits are valued in only a handful of the 123 studies in the systematic review \cite{ahmed_district_2025}.
  \item \textbf{Business-model and instrument design.} This thesis names the missing transfer mechanism (\autoref{2-subsec:2.5.2 value gap}) but does not design it. The concrete shape of a capacity-payment product, an aggregation contract, or a bilateral DSO flexibility agreement remains open. The closest precedent for that design question is a systematic review of district-energy business models \cite{selvakkumaran_how_2021}.
\end{itemize}
